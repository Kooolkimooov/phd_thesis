\section{Introduction}

Experimental validation of theorical work is a crucial step in the development of new technologies
and methods. This chapter presents the experiments conducted to validate the theoretical work
presented in \cref{cable_modelling,robot_chain_control}.

The first experimental campaign presented in
\cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation} was conducted to gather the dataset used
in \cref{cm_acm_experimental_validation} in order to quantify the accuracy of the proposed model in
comparaison to actual cables in underwater conditions. In addition to using a varied set of cables
changes experimental conditions such as the speed of the robot and the direction of motion.

The second experimental campaign presented in \cref{e_robot_chain_control_experiment} was conducted
to validate the feasability of the proposed control methods and simulations presented in
\cref{robot_chain_control}. The robot chain used was scaled down from the three Bluerovs with a
surface vehicle to a pair of Bluerovs for practical reasons.

\section{Augmented catenary model experimental validation}
\label{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}

The motivation and objective of these experiments were to study the accuracy of catenary models in
estimating the shape of tethers in water under dynamic conditions. This model is required for
model-based control of tethered underwater vehicles, such as obstacle avoidance.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{e_acmev_experimental_setup}

The experiment was conducted in a \qty{7.2}{\meter} long, \qty{4.2}{\meter} wide, and
\qty{3}{\meter} deep tank (\cref{e_acmev_es_robot_in_tank}). The cable's and robot's positions were
recorded by a 5-camera, \qty{100}{\Hz} Qualisys\footnote{\url{www.qualisys.com/cameras/underwater}}
underwater motion capture system. The calibration of this system provided a standard deviation of
\qty{2.2}{\mm} across the tank's calibrated volume for a known object's length. The robot and each
cable were equipped with passive reflective markers
(\cref{e_acmev_es_robot_markers,e_acmev_es_all_cables}), and the cable's markers were evenly spaced
out by \qty{20}{\centi\meter}. The cables were attached to the robot and to a fixed point on the
side of the tank with thin wire
(\cref{e_acmev_es_robot_cable_attachment_point,e_acmev_es_rod_cable_attachment_point}) so that the
joint between the tether and its attachment point closely approximated a theoretical spherical
joint. The cables used are shown in \cref{e_acmev_es_all_cables}, and their characteristics are
listed in \cref{e_acmev_es_cables_characteristics}.

The experiment was repeated three times for each cable under six experimental conditions, enabling
the testing of more or less taut cables with accelerations of different amplitudes. Each
experimental condition was a combination of a direction of motion (sway or surge), a speed
(\qty{0.3}{\m\per\s} or \qty{0.6}{\m\per\s}), and a starting point characterized by its horizontal
distance from the fixation point (approximately \qty{1.5}{\m} or \qty{2}{\m}).

In every experiment, the robot received the following commands:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item stabilization with auto-depth at \qty{1}{\m} depth for \qty{2}{\s},
	\item abrupt start with a constant surge or sway command applied for \qty{2.5}{\s},
	\item reversing of the thrusters for \qty{3}{\s} with a doubled velocity in the opposite direction to
	      achieve an abrupt stop,
	\item slowing down the thrusters to half the initial velocity for \qty{0.2}{\s}, and
	\item deactivation of the auto-depth control and complete stop of the thrusters after \qty{13}{\s}. The
	      robot then drifted slowly.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Real time feedback from the Qualisys system}
\label{e_rcce_ctcl_real_time_feedback_from_qualisys_system}

A prerequisite for any experimental application of a control system was the availability of a
reliable measurement of the system state. In the simulations presented in
\cref{robot_chain_control}, the state measurement was assumed to be perfect. In practice, however,
such conditions are not met. Since the focus of this work was on the control of a robot chain, the
already available and highly accurate Qualisys underwater motion capture system was employed, as it
had been used in the previous experiment
(\cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}). Its real-time
capabilities\footnote{\url{https://docs.qualisys.com/qtm-rt-protocol/}} were exploited to provide
live state feedback.

From a technical standpoint, the Qualisys cameras operated as part of a network connected to a
dedicated computer running the \ac{qtm} software for motion capture and recording. In addition to
its primary recording functions, \ac{qtm} provided a server interface that could be queried locally
or from a separate computer to obtain real-time scene data. This data included two-dimensional
marker positions in camera frames, three-dimensional positions in the world frame, or
six-dimensional poses of predefined rigid bodies composed of grouped markers.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/closed_loop_diagram}
	\caption{Principle diagram of the implementation of the closed loop.}
	\label{e_rcce_ctcl_rtfftqs_closed_loop_diagram}
\end{figure}

A dedicated \ac{ros} package was implemented in \citeurl{filliungQtm_interface2025} to acquire the
pose of a predefined robot from the Qualisys system and to publish this information to a \ac{ros}
topic. This topic served as an almost ideal measurement of the robot's pose in the world frame and
was used to command the Bluerovs in a closed-loop configuration
(\cref{e_rcce_ctcl_rtfftqs_closed_loop_diagram}). The \ac{ros} node matched the \qty{100}{\Hz}
sampling rate of the Qualisys system, which is sufficient for Bluerov control, given its relatively
slow dynamics. \marginnote{Shannon can rest easy, the sampling rate is more than adequate.}

Additionally, a simulation twin of the node was implemented with an identical \ac{ros} interface,
enabling testing of control algorithms relying on Qualisys data without requiring physical
hardware.

\subsubsection{State estimation with \acl{ekf}}
\label{ex_rcce_ctcl_state_estimation_with_extended_kalman_filter}

During the experiments, limitations in the coverage of the motion capture system were observed.
While the underwater Qualisys system was initially assumed to provide complete and convex coverage
of the experimental volume for the robot's pose (\cref{e_rcce_ctcl_sewekf_coverage}), in practice,
gaps in tracking occurred. These discontinuities were dependent on the robot's orientation
(particularly when facing the positive \vect{x} direction in the figure), self-obscuration and
cross-obscuration, and the visibility conditions in the pool
(\cref{e_rcce_ctcl_sewekf_robot_in_pool}).

To mitigate these issues, an \ac{ekf} was implemented using the \texttt{robot\_localization}
package from \cite{mooreGeneralizedExtendedKalman2016}. The filter fused position and orientation
data from the Qualisys system with inertial measurements from the robot's \ac{imu} and depth
measurements from the pressure sensor. This sensor fusion approach was comparable to the
literature's \ac{usbl}-\ac{imu}-depth standard odometry, and provided continuous state estimation
during short-term losses of camera tracking. However, during prolonged losses, the accumulated
estimation error rendered the filter inaccurate.

\subsection{Controller implementation}
\label{ex_rcce_controller_implementation}

The experiments started by employing a single Bluerov controlled by a \ac{pd} controller. This
configuration was chosen for its simplicity and robustness, making it suitable for validating the
end-to-end control pipeline. The \ac{pd} controller used real-time pose feedback from the Qualisys
system, processed through the \ac{ekf}, to drive the robot along a predefined trajectory. This step
ensured that the measurement, estimation, and actuation components of the system operated reliably
before deploying the more complex \ac{mpc} controller.

Subsequently, the \ac{mpc} controller described in \cref{robot_chain_control} was interfaced with
\ac{ros} as detailed in \citeurl{filliungBluerov2025}. In particular, a conversion function was
implemented to translate the force actuation computed by the \ac{mpc} into the \ac{pwm} commands
expected by the Bluerov. This conversion relied on the robot's theoretical maximum thrust force.

The first \ac{mpc} implementation involved a single Bluerov commanded to track a target pose
(\cref{e_rcce_ctcl_mpc_diagram}). Building on the validated infrastructure from the \ac{pd} test,
this configuration served as a demonstrator of the \ac{mpc} interface. It was employed to identify
suitable controller parameters and to guide the subsequent development of the robot chain
implementation.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/mpc_kalman_diagram}
	\caption{Software schematic of the single \ac{mpc} Bluerov experiment.}
	\label{e_rcce_ctcl_mpc_diagram}
\end{figure}

Finally, an implementation consisting of a follower Bluerov responding to a leader Bluerov, while
respecting constraints imposed by the entire chain, was deployed. These constraints were primarily
determined by the cable between the leader and the follower, and by the pool's physical dimensions.
In the two-Bluerov setup, the leader’s trajectory was still required to be predicted, even though
it was controlled manually, in order to compute constraints for the follower’s motion. This
prediction was based on the leader's pose measurements, obtained in the same manner as for the
follower, and the leader’s control inputs, which were broadcast via \ac{ros}
(\cref{e_rcce_ctcl_mpc_leader_follower_diagram}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/mpc_leader_follower_kalman_diagram}
	\caption{Software schematic of the leader-follower \ac{mpc} Bluerov experiment.}
	\label{e_rcce_ctcl_mpc_leader_follower_diagram}
\end{figure}

In both configurations, a recovery procedure was implemented for cases in which the \ac{mpc} failed
to converge to a solution. In this procedure, the optimization tolerance was progressively
increased until either convergence was achieved—at which point the tolerance was reduced again—or
the tolerance reached a predefined maximum value. In the latter case, the robot and the experiment
were stopped to prevent unsafe operation.

\subsubsection{One robot \acs{pd} experiment} \label{e_rcce_es_one_robot_pd_experiment}

The preliminary integration test was carried out using a single Bluerov controlled by a \ac{pd}
controller (see the leader side of \cref{e_rcce_ctcl_mpc_leader_follower_diagram}). The objective
of this step was to verify the correct operation of the measurement pipeline, state estimation, and
control actuation under simple closed-loop conditions before deploying the \ac{mpc} controller. The
target trajectory was a straight line at a constant depth and low speed, similar to the open-loop
trajectories executed in \cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}.

The proportional gain was set to \num{1} in translation and \num{0.05} in orientation. The
derivative gain was set to \num{10} in the \vect{x}-\vect{y} plane, \num{1} in the vertical
direction, and \num{0.01} in orientation. An offset of \num{-0.55} was applied to the \vect{z}
component of the control output to compensate for the Bluerov's buoyancy.

\subsubsection{One robot \acs{mpc} experiment} \label{e_rcce_es_one_robot_mpc_experiment}

In this configuration, the \ac{ros} node operated at a rate of \qty{10}{\Hz} with a prediction
horizon of \qty{0.5}{\s}. The Bluerov model was configured as underactuated, with pitch and roll
actuation excluded from the \ac{mpc}. Actuation was held constant over the horizon.

The robot's pose tracking was weighted at \num{7.5} for position and \num{10.0} for yaw. The final
pose over the horizon was given an additional weight of \num{3.0}. An objective term to minimize
speed was included with a weight of \num{1.0}. Actuation was weighted at \num{2e-4}, except for
vertical actuation, which was weighted at \num{1e-4}.

The computed actuation was limited to half the theoretical maximum thrust. The robot's pose was
constrained to remain within the pool's physical boundaries and the camera tracking volume. The
linear speed was further constrained to \qty{0.5}{\m\per\s}, in addition to the minimization
objective.

The optimization problem was solved with a tolerance of \num{1e-6} and a maximum of \num{100}
iterations.

\subsubsection{Two robots \acs{mpc} experiment} \label{e_rcce_es_two_robots_mpc_experiment}

In this experiment, the cable of the two-robot chain system was a \qty{1.5}{\meter} weighted cable
(\cref{e_acmev_es_all_cables}.\subref{e_acmev_es_weighted}), initially treated as a constraint
rather than being physically attached to the robots.

As several Bluerovs were employed, the set of visual markers assigned to each robot for pose
estimation had to be unique (\cref{e_rcce_es_rovs}). These unique configurations were determined
experimentally.

The leader robot was controlled as in \cref{e_rcce_es_one_robot_pd_experiment}, using the same
setup. The \ac{ros} node again operated at \qty{10}{\Hz} with a horizon of \qty{0.5}{\s}, and the
models for both robots were set as underactuated. Pitch and roll actuation were excluded from the
\ac{mpc}, and actuation was held constant over the horizon. The models were fine-tuned in buoyancy
to match the physical Bluerovs.

The follower robot's absolute pose was given zero weight, as only its relative position to the
leader was relevant. An objective term to minimize the follower's speed was included with a weight
of \num{10.0}. Actuation was weighted at \num{1e-6}, except for yaw, which was weighted at
\num{1.0} to limit unwanted yaw motion.

As in the single-robot case, the actuation was limited to half the maximum theoretical thrust. The
pose was constrained to the pool's boundaries and the tracking volume. The linear speed limit of
\qty{0.5}{\m\per\s} was applied, along with the essential constraint that the inter-robot distance
not exceed the cable length of \qty{1.5}{\meter}. Additionally, the cable's lowest point was
constrained by the pool's depth.

The optimization problem was solved with the same tolerance of \num{1e-6} and a maximum of
\num{100} iterations.

\subsection{Conclusion}
\label{e_rcce_conclusion}

The experiments presented in this chapter demonstrated the feasibility of controlling a robot chain
in an underwater environment using real-time motion capture data. A preliminary \ac{pd} integration
test confirmed the correct operation of the sensing, estimation, and actuation pipeline. The
subsequent single-robot \ac{mpc} experiment validated the control framework under constrained and
observable conditions. Finally, the two-robot \ac{mpc} experiment provided insights into the
additional challenges posed by inter-robot constraints, limited tracking space, and complex
environmental geometry.

The results show that reliable state estimation and constraint handling are absolutely essential
for robust operation in multi-robot underwater systems. It is also evident that the accuracy and
continuity of external tracking strongly influence controller performance, particularly in
leader-follower configurations.

Future work will focus on performing new experiments in improved conditions, including larger and
less turbid environments with enhanced tracking coverage, in order to mitigate the limitations
observed in the present setup. Additionally, the control framework will be extended and validated
on a three-robot chain system, enabling the study of more complex coordination strategies and the
scalability of the proposed approach.

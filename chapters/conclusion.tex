\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

\minitoc{}

This manuscript addressed the modelling and control of chains of tethered underwater robots, with a
focus on fast, analyzable cable representations and chain-level controllers that consider tether
geometry. Two technical threads were developed in tandem: cable modelling suitable for real-time
use in control, and coordination of robot chains under geometry-consistent constraints. The main
outcomes are synthesized below, followed by a discussion of limitations and perspectives for future
work.

\section{Cable Modelling}
\label{c_cable_modelling}

The classical catenary model was recalled and adapted for control applications and accuracy
validation (\cref{cable_modelling}). A compact parameterization was derived that relates the curve
to endpoint geometry and cable length, while curvilinear discretization was introduced to generate
uniformly spaced samples along the arc, matching how measurements are typically acquired in
practice. The model also provides closed-form expressions for horizontal and vertical tensions
along the cable, which can be directly reused as external forces in vehicle dynamics. As expected,
the vertical catenary remains a strong baseline in quasi-static regimes, and its analytical form
makes it compatible with real-time control pipelines and optimization-based controllers.

To accommodate steady hydrodynamic effects under motion, an augmented model was proposed
(\Cref{cm_augmented_catenary_model}). The cable curve is obtained by applying two rotations to the
vertical catenary: a sway-related rotation and a surge-related rotation. This construction yields
the \thetagammamodel{} while preserving analytical tractability and curvilinear discretization. The
model was evaluated on a motion-capture dataset comprising \num{129} sequences and \num{8} distinct
cables spanning different buoyancies and flexibilities
(\cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}). The residual between measured and
modelled point clouds, computed frame-wise over uniformly spaced samples, was used as the accuracy
metric. Across cables and conditions, the augmented form systematically outperformed the purely
vertical catenary, with median residual reductions ranging from \qty{10}{\percent} to
\qty{75}{\percent}. For the most compliant and negatively buoyant cables, typical median residuals
reached values between \qty{10}{\milli\meter} and \qty{13}{\milli\meter}.

Two approaches were explored to provide dynamics for the augmentation parameters: a
physics-inspired surrogate based on a pendulum analogy driven by endpoint motion, and data-driven
symbolic regression. While promising, these preliminary efforts did not yield a robust dynamic law
suitable for deployment in chain control. For this reason, the control chapter deliberately
retained the vertical catenary model.

\section{Robot Chain Control}
\label{c_robot_chain_control}

Building on a standard marine-craft model and catenary-based tether forces, a unified chain model
was formulated (\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}). This representation embeds horizontal and
vertical forces from the catenary directly into each vehicle's dynamics, enabling environment
clearance, inter-robot distances, and admissible thrusts to be expressed in a geometry-consistent
fashion.

Three control strategies were examined under a common scenario involving varying bathymetry, leader
manoeuvres, and mixed surface and underwater vehicles. A constrained \ac{mpc} optimized inputs over
a finite horizon while enforcing chain dynamics and catenary-induced constraints. A planning layer
generated feasible waypoint sequences followed by a \ac{pid} tracker, providing a low-complexity
alternative with explicit feasibility filtering. A lightweight reactive behavior, adapted from
leader-follower visual servoing but relying on relative pose feedback rather than image features,
offered a fast fallback mechanism. Simulations compared tracking errors, inter-robot spacing,
seafloor clearances, and computation times. The \ac{mpc} demonstrated the most systematic
constraint handling and overall performance when sufficient computation was available. The
planner-tracker strategy struck a balance between feasibility and simplicity, while reactive
control remained attractive for fast safety overrides and as a fallback layer.

\section{Limitations}
\label{c_limitations}

Several limitations were identified throughout this work. A consistent dynamic law for the
augmentation parameters remains to be established; without it, the \augmentedmodel{} cannot
reliably deliver the accuracy gains observed in \cref{cm_acm_experimental_validation}. Experimental
validation was restricted to pool trials with one and two robots and relied on motion capture,
leaving broader trials with less constraining state estimation and larger fleets still to be
conducted. Finally, real-time \ac{mpc} in multi-robot configurations remains sensitive to model
size, horizon length, and solver settings, making careful formulation and computational
optimization essential.

\section{Perspectives and Future Work}
\label{c_perspectives}

Several directions naturally extend the work presented in this thesis. Dynamic equations for the
augmentation parameters should be derived and validated, potentially using learning-based
identification from onboard sensing. Exploring alternative \ac{mpc} formulations that incorporate
visual servoing features may reduce the search space and improve efficiency. A transition from
motion capture to onboard sensing, including \ac{dvl}, acoustics, vision, and pressure sensors,
will enable validation in tanks and eventually at sea, with mixed \ac{rov}/\ac{usv} chains and
realistic tasks. Finally, integration of environment interaction models with explicit clearance
guarantees and contingency handling for slack cables and contact will be essential for deployment
in unstructured settings.

This thesis contributes an analytically tractable modelling layer for tethers, an augmented variant
that captures steady hydrodynamic deformations, and a set of chain-control strategies that
explicitly respect tether geometry. Together with the experimental demonstrations, these results
support the viability of cable-aware control for robot chains and provide a practical foundation
for future implementations at scale and in the field.

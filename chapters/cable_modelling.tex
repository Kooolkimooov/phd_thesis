\chapter{Cable modelling}
\label{cable_modelling}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter will address the modeling of an underwater cable, with particular emphasis on its
geometric shape, when attached to underwater robotic systems operating under various dynamic
conditions. With an accurate geometric model, the cable will serve as a means of estimating the
robots' positions and as a constraint element in control strategies.

To be suitable for integration within a control framework, the model will be required to maintain a
high level of computational efficiency without compromising accuracy. In addition, the
parameterization of the model will rely solely on data acquired from onboard sensors such as
inertial measurement units (IMUs), water pressure sensors, and cameras.

\Cref{cm_catenary_model} will introduce the catenary model, a classical geometric formulation for
representing the shape of a cable subjected to gravitational forces. Subsequently,
\cref{cm_augmented_catenary_model} will present an augmented version of this model, designed to
incorporate hydrodynamic forces that influence the cable when the robot undergoes certain types of
motion.

\section{Catenary model}
\label{cm_catenary_model}

\subsection{Definition}

The catenary model describes the static equilibrium shape of a homogeneous, flexible cable
suspended between two fixed points under the influence of gravity. This shape, depicted in
\cref{cm_catenary_curve}, is defined when the cable has a fixed length \catL{} and is solely
subjected to its own weight. In underwater environments, the cable also experiences a buoyant
force, which acts in the opposite direction to weight. For the catenary model to remain valid, the
net vertical force --resulting from the combination of weight and buoyancy-- must be non-zero.

The resulting curve lies within a vertical plane, denoted by \lplanefullxz{v}, and is described
relative to a Cartesian reference frame \lframefull{v}. The origin \ldpoint{O}{v} is set at the
lowest point of the curve, and the axis \ldvect{z}{v} is aligned with the direction of
gravitational action in this frame. The coordinates of the points along the curve are expressed as
$\fpoint{P}{v} = \left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bounded by the
attachment points $\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD+\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catdH\right)$. The elevation \coord{z}{v} of each point is given by
the standard catenary equation:

\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD+\catdD,~\catD\right]
	\label{cm_catenary_equation}
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is referred to as the catenary parameter, which governs the
curvature of the cable.

\subsection{Parameters Derivation}

The catenary shape is governed by a combination of geometric parameters: the half horizontal
distance \catD{}, the horizontal offset \catdD{}, the sag \catH{}, the elevation difference
\catdH{}, and the cable length \catL{}. These are interrelated through the catenary parameter
\catC{}, which controls the curvature of the cable:

\begin{align}
	\catC          & = \frac{4\catH + 2\catdH + 4\catL \sqrt{\frac{\catH(\catH + \catdH)}{\catL^2 - \catdH^2}}}{\catL^2 - \left(2\catH + \catdH\right)^2} \\
	\catH          & = \frac{\cosh(\catD\catC) - 1}{\catC}                                                                                                \\
	\catH + \catdH & = \frac{\cosh\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

In practice, all parameters are not always available simultaneously. However, several estimation
methods can be employed to compute \catC{}, depending on the known quantities.

\subsubsection{From Mass and Tension}

As shown in \cref{mb_catenary_model_derivation}, the catenary parameter \catC{} is defined by:

\begin{align}
	\catC = \frac{\lambda_m g}{T_h}
	\label{cm_catenary_parameter_mt}
\end{align}

where $g$ is the gravitational acceleration, $\lambda_m$ is the cable's linear mass, and $T_h$ is
the horizontal component of the cable's tension. Notably, $T_h$ remains constant along the entire
cable.

In underwater environments, buoyancy modifies the effective force acting on the cable.
Incorporating this, the expression becomes:

\begin{align}
	\catC = \frac{\left(\lambda_m - \lambda_v \rho_w\right) g}{T_h}
\end{align}

where $\lambda_v$ is the cable's linear volume and $\rho_w$ is the water density.

\subsubsection{From Endpoints Coordinates}

If the cable's endpoints \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v} are known, the elevation difference
\catdH{} and horizontal span \catl{} can be deduced geometrically, as depicted in
\cref{cm_catenary_curve}. With an additional measurement of the total length \catL{}, the catenary
parameter \catC{} is estimated by solving the following transcendental equation, typically via
Brent's method~\cite{brentAlgorithmGuaranteedConvergence1971}, as used
in~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}:

\begin{align}
	\catC^2 (\catL^2 - \catdH^2) - 4 \sinh^2\left(\frac{l\catC}{2}\right) = 0
\end{align}

\subsubsection{From Endpoint Coordinate and Tangent}

As described in~\cite{druptInertialmeasurementbasedCatenaryShape2022}, the catenary parameter may
also be computed when the coordinates of an endpoint and the tangent vector at a known position $s$
along the cable are available. In such a case, the following expression is used:

\begin{align}
	\catC = \frac{2 \abs{\tan(\beta)}}{\catL - \catdH - 2s}
\end{align}

where $\beta$ is the angle between the tangent vector and the horizontal axis. This method is valid
under the assumptions that $s$ is very small and $\catL \gg \catdH$.

\section{Augmented catenary model}
\label{cm_augmented_catenary_model}

As soon as the underwater cable is moved fast enough, hydrodynamics forces become non-negligible.
The drag force dampens the movement with an amplitude that is proportional to the square of the
speed. This effect is amplified by the added mass when significant accelerations are present. In
the steady state case where all points on the cable move at the same velocity, they are subjected
to the same drag forces. As a consequence, the resulting uniform acceleration of the cable is
tilted, and its intensity is increased depending on the velocity. The addition of two degrees of
freedom in the cable shape model to account for this effect is used here to extend the standard
catenary model.

\subsection{Definition}

\subsubsection{$\gamma$ degree of freedom}

The first degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\gamma$ around the catenary axis \vect{e}, which is defined as the unit vector pointing from the
attachment point \fpoint{A}{v} to \fpoint{B}{v} (see \cref{cm_gamma_augmented_catenary_curve}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/gamma_augmented_catenary_schematic}
	\caption{
		\verticalmodel{} (blue) and \gammamodel{} (red) for $\gamma = \qty{20}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_gamma_augmented_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

This degree of freedom accounts for deformations induced by sway motions, in which the endpoints of
the cable deviate laterally, i.e., in the direction normal to the original vertical plane
\lplane{v}. The corresponding rotation is applied to both the frame \lframe{v} and the plane
\lplane{v}, resulting in the rotated frame \lframefull{\gamma} and the plane \lplanefullxz{\gamma},
respectively.

The catenary curve may be computed either directly within this rotated frame or, equivalently, by
applying the same rotation to the curve initially defined in \lframe{v}, since the parameters of
the curve remain unchanged under this transformation.

\subsubsection{$\theta$ degree of freedom}

The second degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\theta$ around the normal to the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}. This transformation accounts
for deformations induced by surge motion, in which the endpoints of the cable move within the plane
\lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, depending on whether the $\gamma$ rotation has already been applied
(see \cref{cm_theta_augmented_catenary_curve}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/theta_augmented_catenary_schematic}
	\caption{
		\gammamodel{} (red) and \thetagammamodel{} (green) for $\theta = \qty{30}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_theta_augmented_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

The \thetamodel{}'s curve may be computed directly in the rotated frame, or alternatively, it may
be derived by first computing a catenary curve in \lframe{v} or \lframe{\gamma} between the initial
point \point{A} and a virtual endpoint obtained by rotating \fpoint{B} by $-\theta$ around
$\point{A}$ within \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, and then applying the inverse rotation to the
resulting curve. This procedure preserves the parameterization of the original catenary while
accounting for the inclination induced by surge dynamics.

\subsubsection{\thetagammamodel{}}

The degrees of freedom $\theta$ and $\gamma$ can be composed to define a \thetagammamodel{}, which
is intended to represent the cable's shape in more general configurations involving both sway and
surge-induced deformations. This model retains the computational efficiency and analytic simplicity
of the standard catenary formulation while incorporating additional dynamic effects.

Two valid composition orders may be employed, depending on the chosen modeling convention:

\begin{itemize}
	\item In the \emph{$\gamma$-then-$\theta$} composition, the $\gamma$ rotation is first applied to define
	      the frame \lframe{\gamma} and plane \lplane{\gamma}, thereby accounting for sway-induced
	      displacements of the cable endpoints. The \thetamodel{} is then constructed within this rotated
	      configuration by rotating around the normal to \lplane{\gamma}.
	\item In the \emph{$\theta$-then-$\gamma$} composition, the \thetamodel{} is first computed within the
	      initial vertical plane \lplane{v} by applying a surge-induced inclination. The resulting curve is
	      then rotated around the \vect{e}-axis by angle $\gamma$, yielding the final shape in the
	      \thetagammamodel{}.
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the final curve captures the combined effects of surge and sway motions while
relying on an analytically tractable formulation. For consistency in implementation, a fixed
composition order should be adopted, as the two approaches are not commutative in general.

\chapter{Cable modelling}
\label{cable_modelling}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter will address the modeling of an underwater cable, with particular emphasis on its
geometric shape, when attached to underwater robotic systems operating under various dynamic
conditions. With an accurate geometric model, the cable will serve as a means of estimating the
robots' positions and as a constraint element in control strategies.

To be suitable for integration within a control framework, the model will be required to maintain a
high level of computational efficiency without compromising accuracy. In addition, the
parameterization of the model will rely solely on data acquired from onboard sensors such as
inertial measurement units (IMUs), water pressure sensors, and cameras.

\Cref{cm_catenary_model} will introduce the catenary model, a classical geometric formulation for
representing the shape of a cable subjected to gravitational forces. Subsequently,
\cref{cm_augmented_catenary_model} will present an augmented version of this model, designed to
incorporate hydrodynamic forces that influence the cable when the robot undergoes certain types of
motion.

\section{Catenary model}
\label{cm_catenary_model}

\subsection{Definition}

The catenary model describes the static equilibrium shape of a homogeneous, flexible cable
suspended between two fixed points under the influence of gravity. This shape, depicted in
\cref{cm_catenary_curve}, is defined when the cable has a fixed length \catL{} and is solely
subjected to its own weight. In underwater environments, the cable also experiences a buoyant
force, which acts in the opposite direction to weight. For the catenary model to remain valid, the
net vertical force --resulting from the combination of weight and buoyancy-- must be non-zero.

The resulting curve lies within a vertical plane, denoted by \lplanefullxz{v}, and is described
relative to a Cartesian reference frame \lframefull{v}. The origin \ldpoint{O}{v} is set at the
lowest point of the curve, and the axis \ldvect{z}{v} is aligned with the direction of
gravitational action in this frame. The coordinates of the points along the curve are expressed as
$\fpoint{P}{v} = \left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bounded by the
attachment points $\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD+\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catdH\right)$. The elevation \coord{z}{v} of each point is given by
the standard catenary equation:

\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD+\catdD,~\catD\right]
	\label{cm_catenary_equation}
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is referred to as the catenary parameter, which governs the
curvature of the cable.

\subsection{Parameters Derivation}

The catenary shape is governed by a combination of geometric parameters: the half horizontal
distance \catD{}, the horizontal offset \catdD{}, the sag \catH{}, the elevation difference
\catdH{}, and the cable length \catL{}. These are interrelated through the catenary parameter
\catC{}, which controls the curvature of the cable:

\begin{align}
	\catC          & = \frac{4\catH + 2\catdH + 4\catL \sqrt{\frac{\catH(\catH + \catdH)}{\catL^2 - \catdH^2}}}{\catL^2 - \left(2\catH + \catdH\right)^2} \\
	\catH          & = \frac{\cosh(\catD\catC) - 1}{\catC}                                                                                                \\
	\catH + \catdH & = \frac{\cosh\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

If the cable's endpoints \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v} are known, the elevation difference
\catdH{} and horizontal span \catl{} can be deduced geometrically, as depicted in
\cref{cm_catenary_curve}. With an additional measurement of the total length \catL{}, the catenary
parameter \catC{} is estimated by solving the following equation for \catC{}:

\begin{align}
	\catC^2 (\catL^2 - \catdH^2) - 4 \sinh^2\left(\frac{l\catC}{2}\right) = 0
\end{align}

\section{Augmented catenary model}
\label{cm_augmented_catenary_model}

As soon as the underwater cable is moved fast enough, hydrodynamics forces become non-negligible.
The drag force dampens the movement with an amplitude that is proportional to the square of the
speed. This effect is amplified by the added mass when significant accelerations are present. In
the steady state case where all points on the cable move at the same velocity, they are subjected
to the same drag forces. As a consequence, the resulting uniform acceleration of the cable is
tilted, and its intensity is increased depending on the velocity. The addition of two degrees of
freedom in the cable shape model to account for this effect is used here to extend the standard
catenary model.

\subsection{Definition}

\subsubsection{$\gamma$ degree of freedom}

The first degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\gamma$ around the catenary axis \vect{e}, which is defined as the unit vector pointing from the
attachment point \fpoint{A}{v} to \fpoint{B}{v} (see \cref{cm_gamma_augmented_catenary_curve}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_gamma_schematic}
	\caption{
		\verticalmodel{} (blue) and \gammamodel{} (red) for $\gamma = \qty{20}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_gamma_augmented_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

This degree of freedom accounts for deformations induced by sway motions, in which the endpoints of
the cable deviate laterally, i.e., in the direction normal to the original vertical plane
\lplane{v}. The corresponding rotation is applied to both the frame \lframe{v} and the plane
\lplane{v}, resulting in the rotated frame \lframefull{\gamma} and the plane \lplanefullxz{\gamma},
respectively.

The catenary curve may be computed either directly within this rotated frame or, equivalently, by
applying the same rotation to the curve initially defined in \lframe{v}, since the parameters of
the curve remain unchanged under this transformation.

\subsubsection{$\theta$ degree of freedom}

The second degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\theta$ around the normal to the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}. This transformation accounts
for deformations induced by surge motion, in which the endpoints of the cable move within the plane
\lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, depending on whether the $\gamma$ rotation has already been applied
(see \cref{cm_theta_augmented_catenary_curve}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_theta_schematic}
	\caption{
		\gammamodel{} (red) and \thetagammamodel{} (green) for $\theta = \qty{30}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_theta_augmented_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

The \thetamodel{}'s curve may be computed directly in the rotated frame, or alternatively, it may
be derived by first computing a catenary curve in \lframe{v} or \lframe{\gamma} between the initial
point \point{A} and a virtual endpoint obtained by rotating \point{B} by $-\theta$ around
$\point{A}$ within \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, and then applying the inverse rotation to the
resulting curve. This procedure preserves the parameterization of the original catenary while
accounting for the inclination induced by surge dynamics.

\subsubsection{\thetagammamodel{}}

The degrees of freedom $\theta$ and $\gamma$ can be composed to define a \thetagammamodel{}, which
is intended to represent the cable's shape in more general configurations involving both sway and
surge-induced deformations. This model retains the computational efficiency and analytic simplicity
of the standard catenary formulation while incorporating additional dynamic effects.

Two valid composition orders may be employed, depending on the chosen modeling convention:

\begin{itemize}
	\item In the \emph{$\gamma$-then-$\theta$} composition, the $\gamma$ rotation is first applied to define
	      the frame \lframe{\gamma} and plane \lplane{\gamma}, thereby accounting for sway-induced
	      displacements of the cable endpoints. The \thetagammamodel{} is then constructed within this
	      rotated configuration by rotating around the normal to \lplane{\gamma}.
	\item In the \emph{$\theta$-then-$\gamma$} composition, the \thetamodel{} is first computed within the
	      initial vertical plane \lplane{v} by applying a surge-induced inclination. The resulting curve is
	      then rotated around the \vect{e}-axis by angle $\gamma$, yielding the final shape in the
	      \thetagammamodel{}.
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the final curve captures the combined effects of surge and sway motions while
relying on an analytically tractable formulation. For consistency in implementation, a fixed
composition order should be adopted. In the following, any point belonging to an \thetagammamodel{}
is referred as \lupoint{p}{\theta\gamma}.

\subsection{Experimental validation}
\label{cm_experimental_validation}

The experimental validation is done on a dataset whose aquisition is detailed in
\cref{ex_catenary}. The dataset contains \num{129} recording sequences of \num{8} cables being
moved underwater by a robot. More precisely, the data contains the shape of the cable with \scal{n}
regularly spaced points along its length from end to end. These points are refered as
\llpoint{p}{m}{k} in the following, where \scal{k}~\inzeroton{} is the index of the point.

For each cable, the dataset possesses sequences with variing experimental parameters:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the direction of the robot's motion: surge or sway,
	\item the speed of the robot: \qty{0.3}{\meter\per\second} or \qty{0.6}{\meter\per\second},
	\item the initial horizontal distance between the two attachment points: \qty{1.5}{\meter} or
	      \qty{2.0}{\meter}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Curvilinear discretization of the \thetagammamodel{}}

The catenary equation \cref{cm_catenary_equation} is written as a function of \coord{x}{v} which,
when it is regularly discretized and the corresponding \coord{z}{v} is computed does not yield
regularly spaced points along the length of the cable that can be compared to the measured points
\llpoint{p}{m}{k}. This is a consequence of the non-linear nature of the catenary equation. To
overcome this issue, the \thetagammamodel{} is discretized by computing varring steps of
\coord{x}{v} beforehand
\begin{align}
	\coord{x}{v} = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left( \scal{s} \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

where \scal{s} is the signed length of the cable between the lowest point and the point of
interest. The then discretized points of the model are reffered as \llpoint{p}{\theta\gamma}{k} in
the following where \scal{k}~\inzeroton{} is the index of the point.

\subsubsection{Accuracy metric}

In order to quantify the potential accuracy improvement provided by the \thetagammamodel{}, a
minimization procedure is employed to estimate the values of the augmentation parameters $\theta$
and $\gamma$.

The model accuracy is defined by the residual function:
\begin{align}
	\res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}(\theta, \gamma)}
	\label{cm_residual}
\end{align}

where $\fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}(\theta, \gamma)$ are the points corresponding to
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \thetagammamodel{} based on the attachment points
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n} for a given pair of parameters $(\theta, \gamma)$.

The optimal parameters are then obtained by solving the following minimization problem:
\begin{align}
	(\theta, \gamma) = \argmin_{(\theta, \gamma) \in [-\pi, \pi]^2} \res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma)
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Statistical results}

In order to assess the performance of the \thetagammamodel{} in terms of accuracy we compare the
accuracy define in \cref{cm_residual} with the accuracy of the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{} and
\thetamodel{}:
\begin{align}
	\res{v}      & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}}      \\
	\res{\gamma} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k}} \\
	\res{\theta} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k}}
\end{align}

where \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}, \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k} are the
points corresponding to \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{} and
\thetamodel{} based on the attachment points \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n}.

We show in \cref{cm_cables_boxplot} the residuals for the three model variants. There is a general
trend showing that the \thetagammamodel{} has lower residuals than the \verticalmodel{} as well as
the \gammamodel{} and \thetamodel{}. Over all their sequences, cable \subref{ex_catenary_weighted}
and \subref{ex_catenary_chain} show lower residuals for all three models, as expected regarding
their physical properties (\cref{ex_catenary_cables_characteristics}). Indeed, these cables are the
closer to the hipothesis of the catenary model because they are flexible and negatively buoyant.
Cables \subref{ex_catenary_rope} and \subref{ex_catenary_elastic} are also negatively buoyant but
to a lesser extend. The floating cable \subref{ex_catenary_floating} is positively buoyant and
shows good improvements with the \thetagammamodel{}. For the stiffer cables
\subref{ex_catenary_coaxial}, \subref{ex_catenary_four_pairs} and \subref{ex_catenary_two_pairs},
the improvement in accuracy for the \thetagammamodel{} still exists albeit to a lesser extent.
Indeed, the stiffer the cable, the higher the residuals and lesser the residual gained with the
\thetagammamodel{}. This is mainly due to them having permanent deformations as a result of being
coiled. Furthermore they are lightly or neutrally buoyant. As a result, the impact of weight is
insufficient to generate the catenary shape.

\section{Conclusion and perspectives}

\subsection{Augmentation parameters dynamics}

Defining a dynamic model for the proposed degrees of freedom is a nontrivial task, primarily due to
the intuitive construction of the \thetagammamodel{} and the absence of a straightforward
physics-based formulation. However it is indeed essential for the development of a control method
that would be able to account for a cable's shape; but in a reasonable applicative case the shape
of the cables is not available for the augmented parameters to be optimized upon.

\subsubsection{Physics-based model}

A proposed physical analogy relies on a pendulum mounted on a horizontally translating cart, where
the length of the pendulum may vary dynamically based on catenary parameters.

The equation governing the angle $\alpha$ between the vertical axis and the pendulum is derived in
details in \cref{mb_pendulum_derivation}
\begin{align}
	d\ddot{\alpha} = & -\ddot{x} \cos\left(\alpha\right) + g \sin\left(\alpha\right) + 2\dot{d} \dot{\alpha}~+ \nonumber \\
	                 & \frac{1}{2m_2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c
	\sqrt{
		\left(\dot{x} + d \dot{\alpha} \cos\left(\alpha\right) + \dot{d} \sin\left(\alpha\right)\right)^2
		+ \left(d \dot{\alpha} \sin\left(\alpha\right) - \dot{d} \cos\left(\alpha\right)\right)^2
	} \left(\dot{x} \cos\left(\alpha\right) + d \dot{\alpha}\right)
\end{align}

The terms in this expression are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $d$: length of the pendulum,
	\item $x$: horizontal position of the cart,
	\item $m_1$, $m_2$: masses of the cart and the suspended body, respectively,
	\item $g$: gravitational acceleration,
	\item $\rho_{w}$: water density,
	\item $A_c$: cross-sectional area of the suspended mass,
	\item $C_c$: hydrodynamic drag coefficient.
\end{itemize}

This formulation accounts for drag effects. It may serve as a conceptual foundation for the
development of surrogate or reduced-order dynamic models that approximate the evolution of $\theta$
and $\gamma$ under underwater motion. However, none of the attempted configurations yielded
satisfactory results (\cref{cm_dynamics_cables_boxplot}).

\subsubsection{Data based model}

Another approach to determine a more accurate dynamics is the use of data based models such as
neural networks, model identification, etc.

\chapter{Fast Cable Modelling for Model-Based Control}
\label{cable_modelling}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}
\label{cm_introduction}

This chapter focuses on the development of fast and efficient cable models tailored for integration
within model-based control frameworks of underwater robotic systems. This is achieved on the basis
of the \catenarymodel{} which is parameterizing using exclusively data available from onboard
sensors, \acp{imu}, pressure sensors, and cameras. Such sensor-based parameterization enables
real-time updates and adaptability to changing conditions.

The chapter begins with \cref{cm_catenary_model}, where the classical catenary model is introduced.
This model provides an analytical representation of a flexible cable under the influence of
gravity. Although the catenary model is computationally efficient, it is limited to static
conditions. To overcome this limitation, an augmented version of the model is introduced in
\cref{cm_augmented_catenary_model}. This augmented model incorporates additional degrees of
freedom, allowing it to account for the dynamic effects of hydrodynamic forces experienced during
underwater motion.

\section{Catenary Model}
\label{cm_catenary_model}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{cm_cm_definition}

The catenary model describes the static equilibrium shape of a homogeneous, flexible cable
suspended between two fixed points under the influence of gravity. This shape, illustrated in
\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}, is defined when the cable has a fixed length \catL{} and is subject
only to its own weight. In underwater environments, the cable also experiences a buoyant force
acting in the opposite direction to weight. For the catenary model to remain valid in such
conditions, the net vertical force --the sum of weight and buoyancy-- must be non-zero. In the
following we assume that the weight is predominant over the buoyancy.

The resulting curve (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}) is confined to a vertical plane, denoted as
\lplanefullxz{v}, and is defined within a Cartesian reference frame \lframefull{v}. The origin
\ldpoint{O}{v} is positioned at the lowest point of the curve, with the \ldvect{z}{v} axis aligned
with the direction of gravitational forces. The coordinates of any point along the curve are
defined as $\fpoint{P}{v} = \left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bound by
the attachment points $\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD-\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catH\right)$ (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}). The \coord{z}{v}
coordinate of each point is given by the standard catenary equation:
\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD-\catdD,~\catD\right]
	\label{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation}
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is the catenary parameter and governs the cable's curvature.

\subsection{Derivation of the Catenary Equation}
\label{cm_cm_derivation_of_the_catenary_equation}

In order to derive the shape of a homogeneous, infinitely flexible, non-elastic cable subjected
solely to its own weight, which is uniformly distributed along its length, a cable of length
\catL{} and mass per unit length \ldscal{\lambda}{m} is considered. The cable is assumed to hang
between two fixed points, \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}, whose coordinates are expressed in the
plane \lplanefullxz{v}, where \ldpoint{O}{v} is defined as both the origin of the coordinate system
and the lowest point of the cable.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/catenary_derivation_schematic_0}
	\caption{
		Cable of length \catL{} hanging between \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}.
	}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_schematic}
\end{figure}

The overall weight \scal{W} of the cable is given by:
\begin{align}
	W = \lambda_m \catL g
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_weight_equation}
\end{align}

At \ldpoint{O}{v}, the tension in the cable is purely horizontal, as the tangent to the cable at
this point is horizontal, and the infinite flexibility of the cable prevents any vertical load
transfer.

At a general point \fldpoint{P}{v}{1} (\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_schematic}) along the cable, the tension
is composed of both vertical and horizontal components, and its magnitude \ldscal{T}{1} is
expressed as:
\begin{align}
	T_1 = \sqrt{T_h^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_tension_equation}
\end{align}

where \ldscal{L}{1} denotes the arc-length of the cable between \ldpoint{O}{v} and
\fldpoint{P}{v}{1}.

Considering an infinitesimal cable element of length \scal{dL}, the infinitesimal horizontal
displacement \scal{dx} is given by:
\begin{align}
	dx = \cos{\left(\omega\right)} dL
\end{align}

where $\cos{\omega} = \frac{T_h}{T_1}$. Substituting this yields:
\begin{align}
	dx = \frac{T_h}{T_1} dL = \frac{T_h}{\sqrt{T_h^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}} dL
\end{align}

Introducing the change of variable
\begin{align}
	\catC = \frac{\lambda_m g}{T_h}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_change_of_variable}
\end{align}

the expression simplifies to:
\begin{align}
	dx & = \frac{\frac{\lambda_m g}{\catC}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{\lambda_m g}{\catC}\right)^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}} dL \\
	   & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(L_1 \catC\right)^2}} dL
\end{align}

Integration from \ldpoint{O}{v} to \fldpoint{P}{v}{1} leads to:
\begin{align}
	x & = \int_0^{L_1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(L_1 \catC\right)^2}} dL \\
	  & = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(L_1 \catC\right)}{\catC}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_horizontal_catenary_coordinate}
\end{align}

Next, the vertical displacement \scal{dz} is related to \scal{dx} via:
\begin{align}
	dz = \tan{\left(\omega\right)} dx
\end{align}

where $\tan{\omega} = \frac{W_1}{T_h}$, with \ldscal{W}{1} being the weight of the cable segment
between \ldpoint{O}{v} and \fldpoint{P}{v}{1}. Thus,
\begin{align}
	dz & = \frac{W_1}{T_h} dx = \frac{\lambda_m L_1 g}{T_h} dx \\
	   & = L_1 \catC dx                                        \\
	   & = \sinh\left(x \catC\right) dx
\end{align}

Integration from \ldpoint{O}{v} to \fldpoint{P}{v}{1} yields:
\begin{align}
	z & = \int_0^x \sinh\left(x \catC\right) dx       \\
	  & = \frac{\cosh\left(x \catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

thus obtaining the standard catenary equation described previously in \cref{cm_cm_definition}.

\subsection{Parameter Derivation}
\label{cm_cm_parameter_derivation}

The catenary shape is governed by a set of geometric parameters that define its curvature and
spatial configuration (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}). These parameters include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \catC: the catenary parameter that characterizes the curvature of the cable and depends on the tension and weight distribution,
	\item \catH: the sag, defined as the vertical distance between the lowest point of the cable and \point{B},
	\item \catdH: the elevation difference between the two endpoints, positive if \point{A} is higher than \point{B} and negative otherwise,
	\item \catD: the horizontal half-distance, representing the horizontal separation between \point{B} and the vertical axis,
	\item \catdD: the horizontal offset, indicating any lateral asymmetry between the endpoints, positive if \point{A} is further from the origin than the symmetric projection of \point{B} with relation to $\left(\ldpoint{o}{v}, \ldvect{z}{v}\right)$, negative otherwise,
	\item \catl: the horizontal distance between the two endpoints, defined as $\catl = 2\catD+\catdD$ (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}),
	\item \catL: the total length of the cable, which is always greater than or equal to the Euclidean distance between the endpoints.
\end{itemize}

These parameters are interrelated through the following mathematical expressions:
\begin{align}
	\catC          & = \frac{4\catH + 2\catdH + 4\catL \sqrt{\frac{\catH(\catH + \catdH)}{\catL^2 - \catdH^2}}}{\catL^2 - \left(2\catH + \catdH\right)^2} \\
	\catH          & = \frac{\cosh(\catD\catC) - 1}{\catC}                                                                                                \\
	\catH + \catdH & = \frac{\cosh\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

In practical applications, the shape of a catenary must often be determined between two endpoints
defined in an arbitrary frame \lframe{f}, where \ldvect{z}{f} is colinear to \ldvect{z}{v}. These
endpoints, \fpoint{A}{f} and \fpoint{B}{f}, are used to calculate the horizontal distance \catl{}
and the elevation difference \catdH{}:
\begin{align}
	\catl  & = \sqrt{(\ldcoord{b}{f}{x} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{x})^2 + (\ldcoord{b}{f}{y} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{y})^2} \\
	\catdH & = \ldcoord{b}{f}{z} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{z}
\end{align}

Using only these two parameters, the known length of the cable \catL{} and the catenary equation
(\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation}), the catenary parameter \catC{} can be determined. Starting with
\begin{align}
	\catdH = \frac{\cosh{\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right)}}{\catC} - \frac{\cosh(\catD\catC)}{\catC}
	\label{cm_cm_pd_dH}
\end{align}

And the addition of the arc length of the curve (see
\cref{cm_acm_ev_curvilinear_discretization_of_the_catenary_model} for more details on this
expression):
\begin{align}
	\catL = \frac{\sinh\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right)}{\catC} + \frac{\sinh(\catD\catC)}{\catC}
	\label{cm_cm_pd_L}
\end{align}

Using the trigonometric identity:
\begin{align}
	\cosh{\clubsuit}-\cosh{\spadesuit}=2\sinh{\left(\frac{\clubsuit+\spadesuit}{2}\right)}\sinh{\left(\frac{\clubsuit-\spadesuit}{2}\right)} \\
	\sinh{\clubsuit}+\sinh{\spadesuit}=2\sinh{\left(\frac{\clubsuit+\spadesuit}{2}\right)}\cosh{\left(\frac{\clubsuit-\spadesuit}{2}\right)}
\end{align}

With $\clubsuit = (\catD + \catdD)\catC$ and $\spadesuit = \catD\catC$ in
\cref{cm_cm_pd_dH,cm_cm_pd_L}, we obtain:
\begin{align}
	\catdH & = \frac{2\sinh{\left(\frac{2\catD + \catdD}{2}\catC\right)}\sinh{\left(\frac{\catdD\catC}{2}\right)}}{\catC} \label{cm_cm_dp_dH_prod} \\
	\catL  & = \frac{2\sinh{\left(\frac{2\catD + \catdD}{2}\catC\right)}\cosh{\left(\frac{\catdD\catC}{2}\right)}}{\catC} \label{cm_cm_dp_L_prod}
\end{align}

Finally, computing
\eqref{cm_cm_dp_L_prod}\textsuperscript{2}~--~\eqref{cm_cm_dp_dH_prod}\textsuperscript{2} with
$\catl = 2\catD + \catdD$ gives:
\begin{align}
	\catL{}^2 - \catdH{}^2                                         & = \left(\frac{2\sinh{\left(\frac{\catl{}\catC{}}{2}\right)}}{\catC{}}\right)^2 \left[\cosh^2{\left(\frac{\catdD{}\catC{}}{2}\right)} - \sinh^2{\left(\frac{\catdD{}\catC{}}{2}\right)}\right] \\
	\Leftrightarrow{} \catC{}^2\left(\catL{}^2 - \catdH{}^2\right) & = 4\sinh^2{\left(\frac{\catl{}\catC{}}{2}\right)} \label{cm_cm_pd_minimization_function}
\end{align}

This relation captures the balance between the geometry of the end points, the length of the cable,
and the way the cable naturally sags under its own weight. It shows that the cable cannot take an
arbitrary shape: once the distance and height difference between the end points and the total
length of the cable are fixed, the curve and its internal forces are uniquely determined. In
particular, the parameter \catC{} reflects the level of horizontal tension in the cable, so the
equation highlights the direct link between geometry, material length, and the mechanical state of
the system.

\Cref{cm_cm_pd_minimization_function} is solved numerically due to its transcendental nature. Once \catC{} is determined,
the catenary equation (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation}) is used to construct the \catenarymodel{}.

\section{Augmented Catenary Model}
\label{cm_augmented_catenary_model}

When the underwater cable is moved at sufficiently high speed, hydrodynamic forces become
non-negligible. Indeed, considering a constant drag coefficient, the standard drag force
formulation dampens the movement with an amplitude proportional to the square of the speed. This
effect is amplified by the added mass when significant accelerations are present. At equilibrium,
where all points on the cable move at the same velocity, they are subjected to identical drag
forces. Consequently, the total uniform acceleration of the cable is tilted, and its intensity
increased depending on the velocity. To account for this effect, two degrees of freedom were
proposed to the cable shape model, extending the \verticalmodel{}.

\subsection{Definition}
\label{cm_acm_definition}

The proposed augmented catenary model is based on intuitions while observing a cable's behavior
during during experiments: The cable would sway when the robot moved in the direction normal to the
cable's plane while staying in a plane while in steady state, with a relatively short out-of-plane
transition beforehand; and during in-plane motion, the cable would take on a shape similar to the
catenary curve, but in a tilted frame (\cref{cm_acm_picture_theta}). again with a short transition
beforehand.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\figurewidth]{catenary_experiment_bluerov_picture_0.png}
	\caption{
		Picture of a cable during in-plane motion of the robot, the cable has a catenary-like shape in a
		tilted frame.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_picture_theta}
\end{figure}

This intuitive model will now be formalized by introducing two additional degrees of freedom to the
\catenarymodel{}.

\subsubsection{\scal{\gamma} Degree of Freedom} \label{cm_acm_d_gamma_degree_of_freedom}

The first degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
\scal{\gamma} around the catenary axis \vect{e}, which is defined as the unit vector pointing from
the attachment point \fpoint{A}{v} to \fpoint{B}{v} (see \cref{cm_acm_d_gdof_schematic}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_gamma_schematic}
	\caption{
		\verticalmodel{} (blue) and \gammamodel{} (red) for $\gamma = \qty{20}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_d_gdof_schematic}
\end{figure}

This degree of freedom accounts for deformations induced by sway motions, in which the endpoints of
the cable deviate laterally, i.e., in the direction normal to the original vertical plane
\lplane{v}. The corresponding rotation is applied to both the frame \lframe{v} and the plane
\lplane{v}, resulting in the rotated frame \lframefull{\gamma} and the plane \lplanefullxz{\gamma},
respectively.

The catenary curve can be computed either directly within this rotated frame \lframe{\gamma} or,
equivalently, by applying the same rotation to the curve initially defined in the vertical frame
\lframe{v}, since the parameters of the curve remain unchanged under this transformation.

\subsubsection{\scal{\theta} Degree Of Freedom} \label{cm_acm_d_theta_degree_of_freedom}

The second degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
\scal{\theta} around the normal to the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma} (see
\cref{cm_acm_d_tdof_schematic}). This transformation accounts for deformations induced by surge
motion, in which the endpoints of the cable move within the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma},
depending on whether the \scal{\gamma} rotation has already been applied.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_theta_schematic}
	\caption{
		\gammamodel{} (red) and \thetagammamodel{} (green) for $\theta = \qty{30}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_d_tdof_schematic}
\end{figure}

The \thetamodel{}'s curve can be computed directly in the rotated frame, or alternatively, it may
be derived by first computing a catenary curve in \lframe{v} or \lframe{\gamma} between the initial
point \point{A} and a virtual endpoint obtained by rotating \point{B} by \scal{-\theta} around
\point{A} within \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, and then applying the inverse rotation to the
resulting curve. This procedure preserves the parameterization of the original catenary while
accounting for the inclination induced by surge dynamics.

\subsubsection{\thetagammamodeltitle{}}
\label{cm_acm_d_augmented_catenary_model}

The degrees of freedom \scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} can be composed to define a
\thetagammamodel{}, which represents the cable's shape in more general configurations involving
both sway and surge-induced deformations. Two valid composition orders may be employed, depending
on the chosen modeling convention:

\begin{itemize}
	\item In the \emph{\scal{\gamma}-then-\scal{\theta}} composition, the \scal{\gamma} rotation is first
	      applied to define the frame \lframe{\gamma} and plane \lplane{\gamma}, thereby accounting for
	      sway-induced displacements of the cable endpoints. The \thetagammamodel{} is then constructed
	      within this rotated configuration by rotating around the normal to \lplane{\gamma}.
	\item In the \emph{\scal{\theta}-then-\scal{\gamma}} composition, the \thetamodel{} is first computed
	      within the initial vertical plane \lplane{v} by applying a surge-induced inclination. The resulting
	      curve is then rotated around the \vect{e}-axis by angle \scal{\gamma}, yielding the final shape in
	      the \thetagammamodel{}.
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the final curve captures the combined effects of surge and sway motions while
relying on an analytically tractable formulation. For consistency in implementation, a fixed
composition order should be adopted. In the future, any point belonging to an \thetagammamodel{}
will be referred to as \lupoint{p}{\theta\gamma}.

\subsection{Experimental Validation}
\label{cm_acm_experimental_validation}

The experimental validation was conducted on a dataset whose acquisition is detailed in
\cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}. The dataset contains \num{129} recording
sequences spanning \num{8} cables being moved underwater by a robot. More precisely, the data
includes the shape of the cable with \scal{n} regularly spaced points along its length from end to
end. These points are referred to as \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} in the following, where
\scal{k}~\inzeroton{} is the index of the point and expressed in the world frame \lframe{w}
determined by the recording setup where the \ldvect{z}{w}-axis coaxial with the \ldvect{z}{v}-axis.

For each cable, the dataset includes sequences with varying experimental parameters:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the direction of the robot's motion: surge or sway,
	\item the speed of the robot: \qty{0.3}{\meter\per\second} or \qty{0.6}{\meter\per\second},
	\item the initial horizontal distance between the two attachment points: \qty{1.5}{\meter} or
	      \qty{2.0}{\meter}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Curvilinear Discretization of the \catenarymodeltitle{}}
\label{cm_acm_ev_curvilinear_discretization_of_the_catenary_model}

The catenary equation \cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation} is written as a function of \coord{x}{v},
which, when it is regularly discretized and the corresponding \coord{z}{v} is computed, does not
yield regularly spaced points along the curve that can be compared to the measured points
\llpoint{p}{m}{k}. This is a consequence of the non-linear nature of the catenary equation. To
address this issue, the \thetagammamodel{} was discretized by computing varying steps of
\coord{x}{v} beforehand.

From the catenary equation, the infinitesimal signed arc-length \scal{ds} of the cable between the
origin (lowest point) and the point of interest is expressed as:
\begin{align}
	ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dz^2}
\end{align}

Since $\frac{dz}{dx} = \sinh\left(x\catC\right)$, it follows that:
\begin{align}
	ds & = \sqrt{dx^2 + \left(\sinh\left(x\catC\right) dx\right)^2} \\
	   & = \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\left(x\catC\right)} dx
\end{align}

Integrating over a given horizontal distance \scal{x} from the origin, the corresponding arc-length
\scal{s} is:
\begin{align}
	s & = \int_{0}^{\Delta x} \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\left(x\catC\right)} dx \\
	  & = \frac{\sinh\left(x \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

This relation leads to the expression of the horizontal distance from the origin \scal{x} as a
function of the arc-length distance \scal{s} from the same origin:
\begin{align}
	x = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(s \catC\right)}{\catC}
	\label{cm_acm_ev_cdotcm_equation}
\end{align}

The length of cable from the curvilinear origin to \llpoint{p}{\theta\gamma}{0} and
\llpoint{p}{\theta\gamma}{n} are
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{s}{0} = -\frac{\sinh{\left(\left(\catD{} + \catdD\right)\catC{}\right)}}{\catC{}} \text{ and } \ldscal{s}{n} = \frac{\sinh{\left(\catD{}\catC{}\right)}}{\catC{}}
\end{align}

Then the steps of \coord{x}{v} are computed from \ldscal{s}{0} or \ldscal{s}{n} by adding --or
subtracting-- fractions of \catL{} and using \cref{cm_acm_ev_cdotcm_equation}.

\subsubsection{Accuracy Metric}
\label{cm_acm_ev_accuracy_metric}

The model accuracy is defined by the residual function:
\begin{align}
	\res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}(\theta, \gamma)}
	\label{cm_acm_ev_am_residual}
\end{align}

where $\fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}\left(\theta, \gamma\right)$ are the points corresponding to
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \thetagammamodel{} based on the attachment points
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n} for a given pair of parameters $\left(\theta,
	\gamma\right)$.

To quantify the achievable accuracy improvement provided by the \thetagammamodel{}, a minimization
procedure was employed to estimate the values of the augmentation parameters \scal{\theta} and
\scal{\gamma}. The optimal parameters were obtained by solving the following minimization problem:
\begin{align}
	(\theta, \gamma) = \argmin_{(\theta, \gamma) \in [-\pi, \pi]^2} \res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma)
	\label{cm_acm_ev_am_minimization}
\end{align}

To go into more details about the minimization cost: an \augmentedmodel{} was computed with the
candidate \scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} and the norm of the distance from the computed points
\fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k} to the measured points \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} was computed and then
averaged on a frame by frame basis. This means that there is no relation between two consecutive
values of \scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} and that their continuity is not enforced. In turn,
signal processing such as fitting or learning used to estimate the dynamic properties of
\scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} may be penalized by such discontinuities.

\subsubsection{Statistical Metric}
\label{cm_acm_ev_statistical_metric}

To assess the statistical significance of the accuracy improvement provided by the
\augmentedmodel{}, the resituals were studied with medians, quartiles and percentiles instead of
the more common mean and standard deviation. This choice is motivated the statistical distribution
of the residuals not being a normal distribution. \Cref{cm_acm_ev_sm_distribution} shows an example
of the distribution of the residuals for a sequence of the dataset which does not have the shape of
a normal distribution. More importantly, the $\text{mean} - 2\times \text{standard deviation}$
metric below which \qty{2.5}{\percent} of the residuals are found is \emph{below} \qty{0}{\mm}
which is not physically possible for values computed using \cref{cm_acm_ev_am_residual}.

\subsubsection{Statistical Results}
\label{cm_acm_ev_statistical_results}

To assess the performance of the \thetagammamodel{} in terms of accuracy, the accuracy defined in
\cref{cm_acm_ev_am_residual} was compared with the accuracy of the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{},
and \thetamodel{}:
\begin{align}
	\res{v}      & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}}      \\
	\res{\gamma} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k}} \\
	\res{\theta} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k}}
\end{align}

where \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}, \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k}, and \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k} are the
points corresponding to \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{}, and
\thetamodel{} based on the attachment points \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n}.

\begin{figure}
	\centering

distributions for these cables exhibit wider interquartile ranges, indicating greater variability
in model performance across different experimental conditions. In these cases, the weight is
insufficient to generate the ideal catenary shape, and the inherent stiffness creates resistive

\subsubsection{Comparaision to a Fully Dynamic Model}
\label{cm_acm_ev_comparaision_to_a_fully_dynamic_model}

To further assess the performance of the \thetagammamodel{}, we compare its accuracy to that of a
fully dynamic model, as presented in \cite{peraudIMUbasedMonitoringBuoyBallast2024}. This
comparison focuses on cable \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted}, which is representative due to its high
flexibility and negative buoyancy.

The fully dynamic model used models the dynamics of a variable-length underwater cable. It is able
to incorporate discrete elements like buoys, ballast, and virtual \ac{imu} at any point along its
length. Using a finite difference method, the cable is discretized into segments whose mechanical
properties, hydrodynamic forces, and motion are computed based on force balance, compatibility, and
angular momentum equations, with boundary conditions set by the robots' positions and winch
operation. Newton-Raphson iterations advance the simulation from a physically valid starting
configuration.

Both models were evaluated on the same dataset, which includes a range of experimental conditions
presented in \cref{cm_acm_experimental_validation}. The residuals were computed for each trial, and
the distributions are summarized in \cref{cm_acm_ev_sr_cables_boxplot_dynamic_diff}. The comparison
reveals several key points:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Both the \thetagammamodel{} and the dynamic model achieve similar median residuals across all
	      experimental conditions, indicating that the simplified model can match the dynamic model's typical
	      accuracy for this cable type.
	\item The dynamic model exhibits extremely low first percentile, in some cases below
	      \qty{0.1}{\milli\meter}. These values are well below the measurement accuracy of the dataset,
	      suggesting that the dynamic model can occasionally fit the data almost perfectly.
	\item The spread of the residuals is comparable between the two models, indicating similar consistency in
	      performance across trials.
	\item Both models show a small number of higher residuals, but the dynamic model's lower bound is
	      consistently lower, as highlighted by the arrows in the figure.
\end{itemize}

The fully dynamic model, by accounting for the full physics of the cable, in particular
out-of-plane deformation, can achieve extremely low errors in favorable conditions. However, the
\thetagammamodel{} provides nearly equivalent median accuracy and similar robustness, while being
much simpler and more tractable for control and estimation tasks. The extremely low errors of the
dynamic model may not be practically significant, as they are below the noise floor of the
measurement system.

\section{Perspectives on the Augmentation Parameters' Dynamics}
\label{cm_perspectives_on_augmentation_parameters_dynamics}

A dynamic model of the proposed degrees of freedom is required in order to design a control method
that accounts for the cable's shape. The construction of the \thetagammamodel{} does not lend
itself to a straightforward physics-based formulation, which makes the derivation of such a model a
challenging task because, in practical applications, the exact shape of the cable is unavailable,
and the augmented parameters cannot be directly optimized without such data.

\subsection{Physics-Based Model}
\label{cm_poapd_physics_based_model}

A proposed physical analogy relies on a pendulum mounted on a horizontally translating cart
(\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}), where the length of the pendulum may vary dynamically based
on catenary parameters.

\subsubsection{Derivation of the Pendulum's Dynamics}
\label{cm_poapd_pbm_derivation_of_the_pendulums_dynamics}

To describe the dynamics of the augmented catenary model with a moving cart pendulum. Let
\scal{\dot{x}} be the speed of the moving cart \ldscal{m}{1} its mass, \ldscal{m}{2} the pendulum's
mass, \scal{\alpha} the angle between the vertical axis and the pendulum and finally
$\scal{l}=f(\catL, \dots)$ the length of the pendulum (\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/pendulum_derivation_schematic}
	\caption{Pendulum and parameters.}
	\label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}
\end{figure}

The angle \scal{\alpha} is then used in the augmented catenary model. The speed \scal{\dot{x}}
corresponds to the average of the two endpoints' speeds. While the mass \ldscal{m}{1} is the mass
of the robots attached to the endpoints. If the tether forms a symmetrical catenary curve, its
weight and drag can be integrated to be applied to a point halfway between the the height of lowest
point and of the attachment points. Moreover, if the attachment points are close, this distance may
be approximated to be half of the tether length:
\begin{align}
	l = \frac{\catL}{4}
	\label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_l_4}
\end{align}

However this first approximation does not take into account cases like the attachment points being
spaced out or a tether that is not symmetrical. Some of these methods
(\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_computation_of_l}) may be more feasible in a real application than
others. In any of these cases, the pendulum's length is time dependant as a function of the robots'
position:
\begin{align}
	\scal{l} = f\left(\scal{t}, \catL{}, \ldpoint{P}{0}(t), \ldpoint{P}{n}(t)\right)
	\label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_function_l}
\end{align}

with \ldpoint{p}{0} and \ldpoint{P}{n} the attachment points positions.

The dynamics of the pendulum is then derived with the criteria $\scal{l}>0$ since $\scal{l}=0$
would mean that the tether is perfectly taunt and out of the scope of this model.

Note that \scal{l} and \scal{x} \emph{are considered as parameters and not variables}. Let
$\vect{A} = \left(\scal{x}, 0, 0\right)$ be the attachment point of the support whose mass is
\ldscal{m}{1} with \scal{x} the horizontal distance between it and the origin. The position of
\ldscal{m}{2} is $\vect{B} = \left(\scal{x} + \scal{l} \sin{\scal{\alpha}}, 0, -\scal{l}
	\cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)$ (\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}).

The speed of \ldscal{m}{2} is:
\begin{align}
	\vect{\dot{B}} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \dot{\scal{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} \\
		0                                                                                                    \\
		\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} - \scal{\dot{l}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

\trigsh{}

Kinetic energy is written:
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{E}{k} & = \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{m}{1} \norm{\vect{\dot{A}}}^2 + \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{m}{2} \norm{\vect{\dot{B}}}^2 \nonumber                                                                                                                                                                                                  \\
	              & =\frac{1}{2}  \left(\ldscal{m}{1} \scal{\dot{x}}^2 + \ldscal{m}{2} \left(\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \dot{\scal{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} \right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \dot{\scal{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} - \scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2\right) \right)
\end{align}

And potential energy is written:
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{E}{p} & = \ldscal{m}{1} \scal{g}~\vect{A}\cdot\ldvect{z}{v} + \ldscal{m}{2} \scal{g}~\vect{B}\cdot\ldvect{z}{v} \nonumber \\
	              & = - \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{g}\scal{l}\cos{\alpha}
\end{align}

The Lagrangian operator is:
\begin{align}
	\lagr{} & = \ldscal{E}{k} - \ldscal{E}{p} \nonumber                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        \\
	        & = \frac{1}{2}  \left( \ldscal{m}{1} \scal{\dot{x}}^2 + \ldscal{m}{2} \left(\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} \right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} - \scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2\right) \right) + \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{g}\scal{l}\cos{\alpha}
\end{align}

In addition, we have the non-conservative drag force:
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{F}{D} & = - \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{\rho}{w} \ldscal{A}{c} \scal{C}{c} \norm{\vect{\dot{B}}}\vect{\dot{B}}     \nonumber                                                                                                                                                                              \\
	              & = - \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{\rho}{w} \ldscal{A}{c} \scal{C}{c} \sqrt{\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}-\scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} \\
		0                                                                                                    \\
		\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} - \scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

With the Lagrange principle:
\begin{align}
	\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial \lagr{}}{\partial \scal{\dot{\alpha}}}
	 & = \frac{\partial \lagr{}}{\partial \scal{\alpha}}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{x}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{x}{v}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{y}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{y}{v}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{z}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{z}{v} \label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_lagrange}
\end{align}

Finally, substituting in~\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_lagrange}:
\begin{align*}
	 & \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{l}^2\scal{\ddot{\alpha}} + 2\ldscal{m}{2}\scal{l}\scal{\dot{l}}\scal{\dot{\alpha}} + \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{l}\scal{\ddot{x}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}} - \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{l}\scal{\dot{x}} \scal{\dot{\alpha}} \sin{\scal{\alpha}} + \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{\dot{l}}\scal{\dot{x}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}}                                                                                                                 \\
	 & = - \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{l}\scal{\dot{\alpha}}\scal{\dot{x}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} + \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{\dot{x}}\scal{\dot{l}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} - \ldscal{m}{2}\scal{g}\scal{l}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}                                                                                                                                                                                                                               \\
	 & - \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{\rho}{w} \ldscal{A}{c} \scal{C}{c} \sqrt{\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}-\scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2}\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right) l\cos{\scal{\alpha}} \\
	 & - \frac{1}{2} \ldscal{\rho}{w} \ldscal{A}{c} \scal{C}{c} \sqrt{\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}-\scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2}\left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} -\scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right) \scal{l}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}
\end{align*}
\begin{align}
	\Leftrightarrow~ & \scal{\ddot{\alpha}} = -\frac{1}{\scal{l}}\left( \scal{\ddot{x}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{g}\sin{\scal{\alpha}} + 2\scal{\dot{l}}\scal{\dot{\alpha}} + \frac{1}{2\ldscal{m}{2}} \ldscal{\rho}{w} \ldscal{A}{c} \scal{C}{c} \sqrt{\left(\scal{\dot{x}} + \scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{\dot{l}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2 + \left(\scal{l} \scal{\dot{\alpha}}\sin{\scal{\alpha}}-\scal{\dot{l}} \cos{\scal{\alpha}}\right)^2}\left(\scal{\dot{x}}\cos{\scal{\alpha}} + \scal{l}\scal{\dot{\alpha}}\right)\right)
\end{align}

\trigft{}

\subsection{Data-Based Model}
\label{cm_poapd_data_based_model}

Another approach to determine a more accurate dynamic description of the augmentation parameters
was investigated using data-driven methods. In contrast to the physics-based formulation presented
previously, this methodology relied on experimental datasets and symbolic regression to derive
explicit equations governing the cable's deformation. The objective was to capture the temporal
evolution of the augmentation angles \scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} directly from the motion of
the attachment points, thereby complementing the \augmentedmodel{} with a dynamics describing the
augmentation parameters.

The dataset considered consisted of the data described in
\cref{cm_acm_experimental_validation,e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation} along with
the values of \scal{\theta} and \scal{\gamma} computed with \cref{cm_acm_ev_am_minimization}.

To relate these augmentation parameters to attachment point motion, symbolic regression was applied
using the \texttt{PySR} library (\cite{cranmerInterpretableMachineLearning2023},
\citeurl{cranmerPySR2025}). This tool employed an evolutionary algorithm to identify compact
algebraic expressions that balance predictive accuracy with interpretability. Both first and second
order candidate models were considered, with velocity or acceleration of the attachment points
serving as inputs. After preliminary testing, second-order formulations proved most suitable, as
they allowed the influence of drag forces --proportional to squared velocities-- to be captured
naturally.

A representative outcome for the angle \ldscal{\theta}{t} took the form
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{\ddot{\theta}}{t} = \num{0.0753} \left[ \ldscal{a}{y} (\ldscal{\theta}{t} + \num{0.4637}) - \ldscal{a}{x} - \sin{\left(\tanh^4{\left(\ldscal{\dot{\gamma}}{t-2}\right)}\right)}\cdot(\ldscal{\dot{\theta}}{t-2} + \ldscal{a}{z}- \num{1.1639}) \right]
\end{align}

where \ldscal{a}{x}, \ldscal{a}{y}, and \ldscal{a}{z} are the attachment point accelerations
expressed in the catenary frame. This equation illustrates how symbolic regression incorporated
nonlinear terms and delayed signals to reproduce the tether's dynamic response.

The identified expressions were evaluated on withheld experimental sequences. In these validation
tests, reconstructed trajectories obtained by double integration of the estimated angular
accelerations exhibited improved long-term consistency and closer alignment with the true cable
evolution compared to first-order or purely autoregressive approaches. Although instantaneous
errors were not always minimized, the learned equations respected the overall curvature trends of
the system and generalized across velocity regimes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{cm_conclusion}

This chapter presented a comprehensive approach to modeling underwater cables, focusing on
balancing computational efficiency and modeling accuracy for integration into control frameworks.
Starting from the \verticalmodel{}, the chapter introduced an \augmentedmodel{} with additional
degrees of freedom to account for hydrodynamic effects encountered during robot-induced cable
motion. Experimental validation demonstrated that the augmented model significantly improves
accuracy over the standard catenary, particularly in dynamic scenarios. While a physics-based
dynamic model for the augmentation parameters remains challenging, initial attempts and analogies
were discussed, and future work will explore data-driven approaches to further enhance predictive
capabilities. The developed models provide a solid foundation for robust cable-aware control
strategies in underwater robotics.

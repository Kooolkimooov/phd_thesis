\chapter{Fast Cable Modelling for Model-based Control}
\label{cable_modelling}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}
\label{cm_introduction}

This chapter focuses on the development of fast and efficient cable models tailored for integration
within model-based control frameworks of underwater robotic systems. Accurate cable modeling is
essential for achieving precise control, as the cable's shape and tension directly influence the
robot's dynamics and positioning. However, this accuracy must be balanced with computational
efficiency to ensure real-time applicability.

The primary objective of this chapter is to present a cable modeling approach that maintains
relatively high while remaining computationally lightweight. This is achieved by parameterizing the
model using data exclusively from onboard sensors, including inertial measurement units (IMUs),
pressure sensors, and cameras. Such sensor-based parameterization enables real-time updates and
adaptability to changing conditions.

The chapter begins with \cref{cm_catenary_model}, where the classical catenary model is introduced.
This model provides an analytical representation of a flexible cable under the influence of
gravity. Although the catenary model is computationally efficient, it is limited to static
conditions. To overcome this limitation, an augmented version of the model is introduced in
\cref{cm_augmented_catenary_model}. This augmented model incorporates additional degrees of
freedom, allowing it to account for the dynamic effects of hydrodynamic forces experienced during
underwater motion.

\section{Catenary model}
\label{cm_catenary_model}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{cm_cm_definition}

The catenary model describes the static equilibrium shape of a homogeneous, flexible cable
suspended between two fixed points under the influence of gravity. This shape, illustrated in
\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_curve}, is defined when the cable has a fixed length \catL{} and is subject
only to its own weight. In underwater environments, the cable also experiences a buoyant force
acting in the opposite direction to weight. For the catenary model to remain valid in such
conditions, the net vertical force --the sum of weight and buoyancy-- must be non-zero. In the
following we assume that the weight is predominant over the buoyancy.

The resulting curve is confined to a vertical plane, denoted as \lplanefullxz{v}, and is defined
within a Cartesian reference frame \lframefull{v}. The origin \ldpoint{O}{v} is positioned at the
lowest point of the curve, with the \ldvect{z}{v} axis aligned with the direction of gravitational
forces. The coordinates of any point along the curve are defined as $\fpoint{P}{v} =
	\left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bound by the attachment points
$\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD-\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catH\right)$. The \coord{z}{v} coordinate of each point is given by
the standard catenary equation:
\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD-\catdD,~\catD\right]
	\label{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation}
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is the catenary parameter and governs the cable's curvature.

	}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Derivation of the catenary equation}
\label{cm_cm_derivation_of_the_catenary_equation}

In order to derive the shape of a homogeneous, infinitely flexible, non-elastic cable subjected
solely to its own weight, which is uniformly distributed along its length, a cable of length
\catL{} and mass per unit length $\lambda_m$ is considered. The cable is assumed to hang between
two fixed points, \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}, whose coordinates are expressed in the plane
\lplanefullxz{v}, where \ldpoint{O}{v} is defined as both the origin of the coordinate system and
the lowest point of the cable.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/catenary_derivation_schematic_0}
	\caption{
		Cable of length \catL{} hanging between \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}.
	}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_schematic}
\end{figure}

The overall weight \scal{W} of the cable is given by:
\begin{align}
	W = \lambda_m \catL g
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_weight_equation}
\end{align}

At \ldpoint{O}{v}, the tension in the cable is purely horizontal, as the tangent to the cable at
this point is horizontal, and the infinite flexibility of the cable prevents any vertical load
transfer.

At a general point $\fldpoint{P}{v}{1}$ (\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_schematic}) along the cable, the tension
is composed of both vertical and horizontal components, and its magnitude $T_1$ is expressed as:
\begin{align}
	T_1 = \sqrt{T_h^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}
	\label{cm_cm_dotcm_tension_equation}
\end{align}

where $L_1$ denotes the arc-length of the cable between \ldpoint{O}{v} and \fldpoint{P}{v}{1}.

Considering an infinitesimal cable element of length $dL$, the infinitesimal horizontal
displacement $dx$ is given by:
\begin{align}
	dx = \cos{\left(\omega\right)} dL
\end{align}

where $\cos{\omega} = \frac{T_h}{T_1}$. Substituting this yields:
\begin{align}
	dx = \frac{T_h}{T_1} dL = \frac{T_h}{\sqrt{T_h^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}} dL
\end{align}

Introducing the change of variable $\catC = \frac{\lambda_m g}{T_h}$, the expression simplifies to:
\begin{align}
	dx & = \frac{\frac{\lambda_m g}{\catC}}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{\lambda_m g}{\catC}\right)^2 + \left(\lambda_m L_1 g\right)^2}} dL \\
	   & = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(L_1 \catC\right)^2}} dL
\end{align}

Integration from \ldpoint{O}{v} to \fldpoint{P}{v}{1} leads to:
\begin{align}
	x & = \int_0^{L_1} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(L_1 \catC\right)^2}} dL \\
	  & = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(L_1 \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

Next, the vertical displacement $dz$ is related to $dx$ via:
\begin{align}
	dz = \tan{\left(\omega\right)} dx
\end{align}

where $\tan{\omega} = \frac{W_1}{T_h}$, with $W_1$ being the weight of the cable segment between
\ldpoint{O}{v} and \fldpoint{P}{v}{1}. Thus,
\begin{align}
	dz & = \frac{W_1}{T_h} dx = \frac{\lambda_m L_1 g}{T_h} dx \\
	   & = L_1 \catC dx                                        \\
	   & = \sinh\left(x \catC\right) dx
\end{align}

Integration from \ldpoint{O}{v} to $\fldpoint{P}{v}{1}$ yields:
\begin{align}
	z & = \int_0^x \sinh\left(x \catC\right) dx       \\
	  & = \frac{\cosh\left(x \catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

thus obtaining the standard catenary equation described previously in \cref{cm_cm_definition}.

\subsection{Parameter derivation}
\label{cm_cm_parameter_derivation}

The catenary shape is governed by a set of geometric parameters that define its curvature and
spatial configuration. These parameters include:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \catC: the catenary parameter, a critical value that characterizes the curvature of the cable and depends on the tension and weight distribution,
	\item \catH: the sag, defined as the vertical distance between the lowest point of the cable and the highest endpoint,
	\item \catdH: the elevation difference between the two endpoints, positive if \point{A} is higher than \point{B} and negative otherwise,
	\item \catD: the horizontal half-distance, representing the horizontal separation between \point{B} and its projection onto the vertical axis,
	\item \catdD: the horizontal offset, indicating any lateral asymmetry between the endpoints, positive if \point{A} is further from the origin than the symmetric projection of \point{B} with relation to $(\ldpoint{o}{v}, \ldvect{z}{v})$, negative otherwise,
	\item \catl: the horizontal distance between the two endpoints, defined as $\catl = 2\catD+\catdD$,
	\item \catL: the total length of the cable, which is always greater than or equal to the Euclidean distance between the endpoints.
\end{itemize}

These parameters are interrelated through the following mathematical expressions:
\begin{align}
	\catC          & = \frac{4\catH + 2\catdH + 4\catL \sqrt{\frac{\catH(\catH + \catdH)}{\catL^2 - \catdH^2}}}{\catL^2 - \left(2\catH + \catdH\right)^2} \\
	\catH          & = \frac{\cosh(\catD\catC) - 1}{\catC}                                                                                                \\
	\catH + \catdH & = \frac{\cosh\left((\catD + \catdD)\catC\right) - 1}{\catC}
\end{align}

In practical applications, the shape of a catenary must often be determined between two endpoints
defined in an arbitrary frame \lframe{f}, where \ldvect{z}{f} is colinear to \ldvect{z}{v}. These
endpoints, \fpoint{A}{f} and \fpoint{B}{f}, are used to calculate the horizontal distance \catl{}
and the elevation difference \catdH{}:
\begin{align}
	\catl  & = \sqrt{(\ldcoord{b}{f}{x} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{x})^2 + (\ldcoord{b}{f}{y} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{y})^2} \\
	\catdH & = \ldcoord{b}{f}{z} - \ldcoord{a}{f}{z}
\end{align}

Given these values and the known cable length \catL{}, the catenary parameter is determined by
solving the following nonlinear equation:
\begin{align}
	\catC^2 (\catL^2 - \catdH^2) - 4 \sinh^2\left(\frac{l\catC}{2}\right) = 0
\end{align}

This equation is solved numerically due to its transcendental nature. Once \catC{} is determined,
the catenary equation (\cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation}) is used to construct the \catenarymodel{}.

\section{Augmented catenary model}
\label{cm_augmented_catenary_model}

When the underwater cable is moved at sufficiently high speed, hydrodynamic forces become
non-negligible. Indeed, considering a constant drag coefficient, the standard drag force
formulation dampens the movement with an amplitude proportional to the square of the speed. This
effect is amplified by the added mass when significant accelerations are present. At equilibrium,
where all points on the cable move at the same velocity, they are subjected to identical drag
forces. Consequently, the total uniform acceleration of the cable is tilted, and its intensity
increased depending on the velocity. To account for this effect, two degrees of freedom were
proposed to the cable shape model, extending the \verticalmodel{}.

\subsection{Definition}
\label{cm_acm_definition}

The proposed augmented catenary model is based on intuitions while observing a cable's behavior
during during experiments: The cable would sway when the robot in the direction normal to the
cable's plane while staying in a plane while in steady state, with a relatively short out-of-plane
transition beforehand; and during in-plane motion, the cable would take on a shape similar to the
catenary curve, but in a tilted frame (\cref{cm_acm_picture_theta}). again with a short transition
beforehand.

	\caption{
		Picture of a cable during in-plane motion of the robot, the cable has a catenary-like shape in a
		tilted frame.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_picture_theta}
\end{figure}

This intuitive model will now be formalized by introducing two additional degrees of freedom to the
\catenarymodel{}.

\subsubsection{$\gamma$ degree of freedom}
\label{cm_acm_d_gamma_degree_of_freedom}

The first degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\gamma$ around the catenary axis \vect{e}, which is defined as the unit vector pointing from the
attachment point \fpoint{A}{v} to \fpoint{B}{v} (see \cref{cm_acm_d_gdof_schematic}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_gamma_schematic}
	\caption{
		\verticalmodel{} (blue) and \gammamodel{} (red) for $\gamma = \qty{20}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_d_gdof_schematic}
\end{figure}

This degree of freedom accounts for deformations induced by sway motions, in which the endpoints of
the cable deviate laterally, i.e., in the direction normal to the original vertical plane
\lplane{v}. The corresponding rotation is applied to both the frame \lframe{v} and the plane
\lplane{v}, resulting in the rotated frame \lframefull{\gamma} and the plane \lplanefullxz{\gamma},
respectively.

The catenary curve can be computed either directly within this rotated frame \lframe{\gamma} or,
equivalently, by applying the same rotation to the curve initially defined in the vertical frame
\lframe{v}, since the parameters of the curve remain unchanged under this transformation.

\subsubsection{$\theta$ degree of freedom}
\label{cm_acm_d_theta_degree_of_freedom}

The second degree of freedom introduced in the augmented model corresponds to a rotation of angle
$\theta$ around the normal to the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma} (see
\cref{cm_acm_d_tdof_schematic}). This transformation accounts for deformations induced by surge
motion, in which the endpoints of the cable move within the plane \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma},
depending on whether the $\gamma$ rotation has already been applied.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/augmented_catenary_theta_schematic}
	\caption{
		\gammamodel{} (red) and \thetagammamodel{} (green) for $\theta = \qty{30}{\degree}$.
	}
	\label{cm_acm_d_tdof_schematic}
\end{figure}

The \thetamodel{}'s curve can be computed directly in the rotated frame, or alternatively, it may
be derived by first computing a catenary curve in \lframe{v} or \lframe{\gamma} between the initial
point \point{A} and a virtual endpoint obtained by rotating \point{B} by $-\theta$ around
$\point{A}$ within \lplane{v} or \lplane{\gamma}, and then applying the inverse rotation to the
resulting curve. This procedure preserves the parameterization of the original catenary while
accounting for the inclination induced by surge dynamics.

\subsubsection{\thetagammamodel{}}
\label{cm_acm_d_augmented_catenary_model}

The degrees of freedom $\theta$ and $\gamma$ can be composed to define a \thetagammamodel{}, which
represents the cable's shape in more general configurations involving both sway and surge-induced
deformations. Two valid composition orders may be employed, depending on the chosen modeling
convention:

\begin{itemize}
	\item In the \emph{$\gamma$-then-$\theta$} composition, the $\gamma$ rotation is first applied to define
	      the frame \lframe{\gamma} and plane \lplane{\gamma}, thereby accounting for sway-induced
	      displacements of the cable endpoints. The \thetagammamodel{} is then constructed within this
	      rotated configuration by rotating around the normal to \lplane{\gamma}.
	\item In the \emph{$\theta$-then-$\gamma$} composition, the \thetamodel{} is first computed within the
	      initial vertical plane \lplane{v} by applying a surge-induced inclination. The resulting curve is
	      then rotated around the \vect{e}-axis by angle $\gamma$, yielding the final shape in the
	      \thetagammamodel{}.
\end{itemize}

In both cases, the final curve captures the combined effects of surge and sway motions while
relying on an analytically tractable formulation. For consistency in implementation, a fixed
composition order should be adopted. In the future, any point belonging to an \thetagammamodel{}
will be referred to as \lupoint{p}{\theta\gamma}.

\subsection{Experimental validation}
\label{cm_acm_experimental_validation}

The experimental validation was conducted on a dataset whose acquisition is detailed in
\cref{e_augmented_catenary_model_experimental_validation}. The dataset contains \num{129} recording
sequences spanning \num{8} cables being moved underwater by a robot. More precisely, the data
includes the shape of the cable with \scal{n} regularly spaced points along its length from end to
end. These points are referred to as \llpoint{p}{m}{k} in the following, where
\scal{k}~\inzeroton{} is the index of the point.

For each cable, the dataset includes sequences with varying experimental parameters:
\begin{itemize}
	\item the direction of the robot's motion: surge or sway,
	\item the speed of the robot: \qty{0.3}{\meter\per\second} or \qty{0.6}{\meter\per\second},
	\item the initial horizontal distance between the two attachment points: \qty{1.5}{\meter} or
	      \qty{2.0}{\meter}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Curvilinear discretization of the \catenarymodel{}}
\label{cm_acm_ev_curvilinear_discretization_of_the_catenary_model}

The catenary equation \cref{cm_cm_d_catenary_equation} is written as a function of \coord{x}{v},
which, when it is regularly discretized and the corresponding \coord{z}{v} is computed, does not
yield regularly spaced points along the length of the cable that can be compared to the measured
points \llpoint{p}{m}{k}. This is a consequence of the non-linear nature of the catenary equation.
To address this issue, the \thetagammamodel{} was discretized by computing varying steps of
\coord{x}{v} beforehand:

From the catenary equation, the infinitesimal signed arc-length \scal{ds} of the cable between the
origin (lowest point) and the point of interest is expressed as:
\begin{align}
	ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dz^2}
\end{align}

Since $\frac{dz}{dx} = \sinh\left(x\catC\right)$, it follows that:
\begin{align}
	ds & = \sqrt{dx^2 + \left(\sinh\left(x\catC\right) dx\right)^2} \\
	   & = \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\left(x\catC\right)} dx
\end{align}

Integrating over a given horizontal distance $\Delta x$ from the origin, the corresponding
arc-length $\Delta s$ is:
\begin{align}
	\Delta s & = \int_{0}^{\Delta x} \sqrt{1 + \sinh^2\left(x\catC\right)} dx \\
	         & = \frac{\sinh\left(\Delta x \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

This relation leads to the expression of the horizontal distance from the origin $\Delta x$ as a
function of the arc-length distance $\Delta s$ from the same origin:
\begin{align}
	\Delta x = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(\Delta s \catC\right)}{\catC}
	\label{cm_acm_ev_cdotcm_equation}
\end{align}

The length of cable from the curvilinear origin to \llpoint{p}{\theta\gamma}{0} and
\llpoint{p}{\theta\gamma}{n} are
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{s}{0} = -\frac{\sinh{\left(\left(\catD{} + \catdD\right)\catC{}\right)}}{\catC{}} \text{ and } \ldscal{s}{n} = \frac{\sinh{\left(\catD{}\catC{}\right)}}{\catC{}}
\end{align}

Then the steps of \coord{x}{v} are computed from \ldscal{s}{0} or \ldscal{s}{n} by adding --or
subtracting-- fractions of \catL{} and using \cref{cm_acm_ev_cdotcm_equation}.

\subsubsection{Accuracy metric}
\label{cm_acm_ev_accuracy_metric}

To quantify the achievable accuracy improvement provided by the \thetagammamodel{}, a minimization
procedure was employed to estimate the values of the augmentation parameters $\theta$ and $\gamma$.

The model accuracy is defined by the residual function:
\begin{align}
	\res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}(\theta, \gamma)}
	\label{cm_acm_ev_am_residual}
\end{align}

where $\fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta\gamma}{k}(\theta, \gamma)$ are the points corresponding to
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \thetagammamodel{} based on the attachment points
\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n} for a given pair of parameters $(\theta, \gamma)$.

The optimal parameters were obtained by solving the following minimization problem:
\begin{align}
	(\theta, \gamma) = \argmin_{(\theta, \gamma) \in [-\pi, \pi]^2} \res{\theta\gamma}(\theta, \gamma)
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Statistical results}
\label{cm_acm_ev_statistical_results}

To assess the performance of the \thetagammamodel{} in terms of accuracy, the accuracy defined in
\cref{cm_acm_ev_am_residual} was compared with the accuracy of the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{},
and \thetamodel{}:
\begin{align}
	\res{v}      & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}}      \\
	\res{\gamma} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k}} \\
	\res{\theta} & = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n} \norm{\fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} - \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k}}
\end{align}

where \fllpoint{p}{w}{v}{k}, \fllpoint{p}{w}{\gamma}{k}, and \fllpoint{p}{w}{\theta}{k} are the
points corresponding to \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{k} predicted by the \verticalmodel{}, \gammamodel{}, and
\thetamodel{} based on the attachment points \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{0} and \fllpoint{p}{w}{m}{n}.

The residuals for the four model variants are presented in \cref{cm_acm_ev_sr_cables_boxplot},
where each boxplot visually represents the distribution of errors across all experimental trials.
The median values, indicated by the central line in each box, reveal that the \thetagammamodel{}
consistently achieved lower residuals across all cable types, with median improvements ranging from
\qtyrange{10}{75}{\percent} compared to the \verticalmodel{}. The interquartile ranges (IQR) which
ranges from the first to the third quartile, represented by the width of each box, further
demonstrate that the \thetagammamodel{} not only reduced median error but also exhibited more
consistent performance with less variability in most cases.

A detailed examination of the residual distributions reveals several noteworthy patterns. Cables
\subref{e_acmev_es_weighted} and \subref{e_acmev_es_chain} exhibited the lowest overall residuals
for all models, with the \thetagammamodel{} achieving median errors below \qty{10}{\milli\meter}
for cable \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted} and approximately \qty{13}{\milli\meter} for cable
\subref{e_acmev_es_chain}. This superior performance is attributable to their physical
characteristics (\cref{e_acmev_es_cables_characteristics}), particularly their high flexibility and
significant negative buoyancy, which closely align with the fundamental assumptions of the catenary
model. In these cases, the gravitational forces dominate the cable's behavior, allowing the
theoretical shape to closely match the observed configuration.

Cables \subref{e_acmev_es_rope} and \subref{e_acmev_es_elastic}, which are also negatively buoyant
but to a lesser degree, demonstrated moderate residuals with the \thetagammamodel{} achieving
\qtyrange{30}{40}{\percent} improvement over the \verticalmodel{}. The floating cable
\subref{e_acmev_es_floating}, which exhibits positive buoyancy, presented a more challenging
modeling scenario, yet showed the most dramatic relative improvement when the \thetagammamodel{}
was applied. In this case, the median residual decreased from approximately \qty{80}{\milli\meter}
with the \verticalmodel{} to around \qty{45}{\milli\meter} with the \thetagammamodel{},
representing a \qty{44}{\percent} reduction in error. This substantial improvement can be
attributed to the additional degrees of freedom compensating for the upward buoyant forces that
significantly alter the cable's configuration from the traditional catenary shape.

For the stiffer cables \subref{e_acmev_es_coaxial}, \subref{e_acmev_es_four_pairs}, and
\subref{e_acmev_es_two_pairs}, the improvement in accuracy for the \thetagammamodel{} was present
but less pronounced, with error reductions ranging from \qtyrange{15}{25}{\percent} compared to the
\verticalmodel{}. This more modest enhancement is primarily due to the permanent deformations
resulting from coiling and their light or neutral buoyancy characteristics. The residual
distributions for these cables exhibit wider interquartile ranges, indicating greater variability
in model performance across different experimental conditions. In these cases, the weight is
insufficient to generate the ideal catenary shape, and the inherent stiffness creates resistive
forces that oppose the formation of a smooth curve.

The four models were further examined in greater detail, distinguishing between experiments
involving surge and sway motion. Cable \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted} was selected for detailed
analysis due to its lowest residuals across all models, and cable \subref{e_acmev_es_two_pairs} was
analyzed because it exhibited the lowest residual among cables with significant plastic
deformations. Detailed results for all other cables are provided in \cref{af_cable_modelling} for
completeness.

In \cref{cm_acm_ev_sr_cable_3_boxplot,cm_acm_ev_sr_cable_6_boxplot}, the distribution of model
residuals for cables \subref{e_acmev_es_two_pairs} and \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted} are presented
according to various experimental parameters. For both cables, the residuals demonstrate a clear
correlation with motion characteristics and cable tension. Specifically, slower motion consistently
produced smaller residuals across all models, with differences of \qtyrange{30}{50}{\percent}
between the \qty{0.3}{\meter\per\second} and \qty{0.6}{\meter\per\second} trials. This observation
aligns with hydrodynamic principles, as higher velocities generate greater drag forces that distort
the cable shape more significantly from the theoretical catenary form.

Additionally, experimental configurations with greater distance between attachment points (i.e.,
\qty{2.0}{\meter} versus \qty{1.5}{\meter}) yielded lower residuals for all models. This
improvement is attributed to the increased tension in the cable, which diminishes the influence of
localized stiffness and helps overcome internal resistive forces, allowing the cable to more
closely approximate the ideal catenary shape. The reduction in residuals with increased tension was
particularly pronounced for the stiffer cable \subref{e_acmev_es_two_pairs}, where the median error
decreased by approximately \qty{40}{\percent} when comparing the \qty{1.5}{\meter} and
\qty{2.0}{\meter} configurations.

A comparative analysis of model performance revealed distinctive patterns based on motion
direction. The \gammamodel{} displayed significantly higher accuracy for lateral sway motion
compared to forward surge motion, with improvements of \qtyrange{35}{65}{\percent} depending on the
cable type. This differential performance reflects the \gammamodel{}'s specific design to address
lateral deformations through rotation around the catenary axis. Conversely, the \thetamodel{}
demonstrated markedly better performance for surge motion compared to the \gammamodel{}, with
residuals reduced by \qtyrange{40}{70}{\percent} in surge trials, highlighting its effectiveness in
accounting for inclination effects induced by forward motion.

In all experimental configurations, the \thetagammamodel{} achieved the highest accuracy by
effectively combining the complementary strengths of both individual augmentations. The relative
improvement in accuracy was substantially greater for cable \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted}, where the
\thetagammamodel{} reduced residuals by up to \qty{85}{\percent} compared to the \verticalmodel{}
in certain configurations. This exceptional improvement for cable \subref{e_acmev_es_weighted} can
be attributed to its physical properties being most closely aligned with the assumptions of the
augmented catenary model, allowing the additional degrees of freedom to effectively capture the
deformations induced by hydrodynamic effects.

\section{Perspectives on augmentation parameters' dynamics}
\label{cm_perspectives_on_augmentation_parameters_dynamics}

Defining a dynamic model for the proposed degrees of freedom was identified as a nontrivial task,
primarily due to the intuitive construction of the \thetagammamodel{} and the absence of a
straightforward physics-based formulation. However, it is essential for the development of a
control method capable of accounting for a cable's shape. In practical applications, the shape of
the cables is often unavailable for the augmented parameters to be optimized upon.

\subsection{Physics-based model}
\label{cm_poapd_physics_based_model}

A proposed physical analogy relies on a pendulum mounted on a horizontally translating cart
(\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}), where the length of the pendulum may vary dynamically based
on catenary parameters.

\subsubsection{Derivation of the pendulum's dynamics}
\label{cm_poapd_pbm_derivation_of_the_pendulums_dynamics}

To describe the dynamics of the augmented catenary model with a moving cart pendulum. Let $\dot{x}$
be the speed of the moving cart $m_1$ its mass, $m_2$ the pendulum's mass, $\alpha$ its tilt angle
and finally $l=f(L, \dots)$ the length of the pendulum (\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/pendulum_derivation_schematic}
	\caption{Pendulum and parameters.}
	\label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}
\end{figure}

The angle $\alpha$ is then used in the augmented catenary model. The speed $\dot{x}$ corresponds to
the average of the two endpoints' speeds. While the mass $m_1$ is the mass of the robots attached
to the endpoints. If the tether forms a symmetrical catenary curve, its weight and drag can be
integrated to be applied to a point halfway between the the height of lowest point and of the
attachment points. Moreover, if the attachment points are close, this distance may be approximated
to be half of the tether length:
\begin{align}
	l = \frac{\catL}{4}
\end{align}

However this first approximation does not take into account cases like the attachment points being
spaced out or a tether that is not symmetrical. Some of these methods may be more feasible in a
real application than others. In any of these cases, the pendulum's length is time dependant as a
function of the robots' position:
\begin{align}
	l = f\left(t, L, \ldpoint{P}{0}(t), \ldpoint{P}{n}(t)\right)
\end{align}

with \ldpoint{p}{0} and \ldpoint{P}{n} the attachment points positions.

In any case the dynamics of the pendulum is derived in the next section with the criteria $l>0$
since $l=0$ would mean that the tether is perfectly taunt and out of the scope of this model.

Note that $l$ and $x$ \textbf{are considered as parameters and not variables}. Let $\vect{A} =
	\left(x, 0, 0\right)$ be the attach point of the support, the position of $m_2$ is $\vect{B} =
	\left(x + l \sin{\alpha}, 0, -l \cos{\alpha}\right)$ (\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_schematic}).

The speed of $m_2$ is:
\begin{align}
	\dot{\vect{B}} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha} \\
		0                                                          \\
		l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha} - \dot{l}\cos{\alpha}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

We use the shorthands $\cos{\ast} = \newcos{\ast}$ and $\sin{\ast} = \newsin{\ast}$. \trigsh{}

Kinetic energy is written:
\begin{align}
	E_k & = \frac{1}{2} m_1 \norm{\dot{\vect{A}}}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_1 \norm{\dot{\vect{B}}}^2 \nonumber                                                                                                              \\
	    & =\frac{1}{2}  \left(m_1 \dot{x}^2 + m_2 \left(\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha} \right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha} - \dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2\right) \right)
\end{align}

And potential energy is written:
\begin{align}
	E_p & = m_1 g \vect{A}\cdot\ldvect{z}{v} + m_2 g \vect{B}\cdot\ldvect{z}{v} \nonumber \\
	    & = - m_2gl\cos{\alpha}
\end{align}

The Lagrangian operator is:
\begin{align}
	L & = E_k - E_p \nonumber                                                                                                                                                                                                            \\
	  & = \frac{1}{2}  \left( m_1 \dot{x}^2 + m_2 \left(\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha} \right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha} - \dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2\right) \right) + m_2gl\cos{\alpha}
\end{align}

In addition, we have the non-conservative drag force:
\begin{align}
	\vec{F}_D & = - \frac{1}{2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c \norm{\dot{\vect{B}}}\dot{\vect{B}}     \nonumber                                                                                                 \\
	          & = - \frac{1}{2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c \sqrt{\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha}\right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha}-\dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha} \\
		0                                                          \\
		l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha} - \dot{l} \cos{\alpha}
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{align}

With the Lagrange principle:
\begin{align}
	\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\alpha}}
	 & = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \alpha}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{x}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{x}{v}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{y}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{y}{v}
	+ \frac{\partial \vect{B} \cdot \ldvect{z}{v}}{\partial \alpha} \ldvect{F}{D} \cdot \ldvect{z}{v} \label{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_lagrange}
\end{align}

Finally, substituting in~\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_dotpd_lagrange}:
\begin{align*}
	 & m_2l^2\ddot{\alpha} + 2m_2l\dot{l}\dot{\alpha} + m_2l\ddot{x} \cos{\alpha} - m_2l\dot{x} \dot{\alpha} \sin{\alpha} + m_2\dot{l}\dot{x}\cos{\alpha}                                                                                                                    \\
	 & = - m_2l\dot{\alpha}\dot{x}\sin{\alpha} + m_2\dot{x}\dot{l}\cos{\alpha} - m_2gl\sin{\alpha}                                                                                                                                                                           \\
	 & - \frac{1}{2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c \sqrt{\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha}\right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha}-\dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2}\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha}\right) l\cos{\alpha} \\
	 & - \frac{1}{2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c \sqrt{\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha}\right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha}-\dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2}\left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha} -\dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right) l\sin{\alpha}
\end{align*}
\begin{align}
	\Leftrightarrow~ & \ddot{\alpha} = -\frac{1}{l}\left( \ddot{x} \cos{\alpha} + g\sin{\alpha} + 2\dot{l}\dot{\alpha} + \frac{1}{2m_2} \rho_{w} A_c C_c \sqrt{\left(\dot{x} + l \dot{\alpha}\cos{\alpha} + \dot{l}\sin{\alpha}\right)^2 + \left(l \dot{\alpha}\sin{\alpha}-\dot{l} \cos{\alpha}\right)^2}\left(\dot{x}\cos{\alpha} + l\dot{\alpha}\right)\right)
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Accuracy and discussion}
\label{cm_poapd_pbm_aad_accuracy_and_discussion}

This formulation accounts for drag effects and serves as a conceptual foundation for the
development of surrogate or reduced-order dynamic models that approximate the evolution of $\theta$
and $\gamma$ under underwater motion. However, none of the attempted configurations yielded
satisfactory results (\cref{cm_poapd_pbm_aad_dynamics_cables_boxplot}).

\subsection{Data-based model}
\label{cm_poapd_data_based_model}

Another approach to determine a more accurate dynamic model involves the use of data-based methods
such as neural networks, model identification, etc. These methods will be explored in future work
to address the limitations of physics-based models and to realize the predictive accuracy of the
\thetagammamodel{}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{cm_conclusion}

This chapter presented a comprehensive approach to modeling underwater cables, focusing on
balancing computational efficiency and modeling accuracy for integration into control frameworks.
Starting from the \verticalmodel{}, the chapter introduced an \augmentedmodel{} with additional
degrees of freedom to account for hydrodynamic effects encountered during robot-induced cable
motion. Experimental validation demonstrated that the augmented model significantly improves
accuracy over the standard catenary, particularly in dynamic scenarios. While a physics-based
dynamic model for the augmentation parameters remains challenging, initial attempts and analogies
were discussed, and future work will explore data-driven approaches to further enhance predictive
capabilities. The developed models provide a solid foundation for robust cable-aware control
strategies in underwater robotics.

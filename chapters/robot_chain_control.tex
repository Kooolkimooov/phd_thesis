\chapter{Robot Chain Control}
\label{robot_chain_control}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}
\label{rcc_introduction}

This chapter examines the coordination of tethered underwater robot chains, in which a designated
leader is tasked with following a motion objective while the formation as a whole must respect the
mechanical and environmental constraints imposed by the tethers and by the operating environment.
In contrast to single-vehicle control, the presence of cables introduces strong coupling: seafloor
clearance, inter-robot spacing, and feasible cable shapes must all be preserved as the leader
maneuvers.

Building on the classical marine craft model (\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}) and the
catenary formulation introduced in \cref{cable_modelling}, a unified description of the chain is
first established in which tether forces explicitly enter each vehicle's dynamics
(\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}), indeed the cable is represented as a vertical catenary,
yielding expressions for horizontal and vertical tensions that can be consistently mapped into each
robot's body frame. This enables the definition of a concatenated chain state and a clear statement
of the constraints that must be enforced, including seafloor and surface clearance, inter-robot
distances, and actuator bounds.

On this foundation, three different control are evaluated (\cref{rcc_controllers}). The first is a
constrained \ac{mpc} that optimizes future inputs subject to coupled dynamics and catenary-induced
constraints. The second is a path planning layer that generates feasible waypoint sequences
subsequently tracked by a \ac{pid} controller. The third is a reactive approach derived from
earlier leader-follower visual servoing work, here adapted to rely on inter-robot pose measurements
rather than image features. Their performance is assessed in a numerical tank with varying
bathymetry and a mixed \ac{rov}/\ac{usv} chain (\cref{rcc_simulation}). Both qualitative outcomes
(trajectories, catenary clearances) and quantitative indicators (tracking errors, inter-robot
distances, speeds, computation times) are reported in order to reveal the trade-offs between
accuracy, constraint satisfaction, and computational feasibility.

In summary, this chapter contributes a compact chain model coupling vehicle dynamics with a
tractable catenary description; a unified set of geometry-consistent constraints; a comparative
study of three control schemes under identical conditions; and practical insights into the choice
and tuning of controllers depending on mission priorities and computational budgets.

\section{Robot Chain Modelling}
\label{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}

The world frame, denoted as \lframe{W} when required, is defined as an earth-fixed, right-handed
frame. A local \ac{enu} convention is adopted unless explicitly stated otherwise: the \vect{x}-axis
points east, the \vect{y}-axis points north, and the \vect{z}-axis points upward. The origin of
this frame is located at the water surface (\cref{rcc_rcm_frames}).

The body-fixed frame, denoted as \lframe{B} when required, is rigidly attached to the vehicle with
its origin at the centre of gravity. It is defined as a right-handed frame in which the
\vect{x}-axis points forward, the \vect{y}-axis points to the left, and the \vect{z}-axis points
upward (\cref{rcc_rcm_frames}).

\subsection{Single Robot Dynamics}
\label{rcc_rcm_single_robot_dynamics}

The dynamic model of a \ac{rov} is described by the classical six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body
model formulated for marine craft by \cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}:
\begin{align}
	\vect{\dot{\eta}} & = \matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) \vect{\nu} \label{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics} \\
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} \vect{\dot{\nu}} + \lrdmatr{M}{a} \ldvect{\dot{\nu}}{r}
	                  & + \lrdmatr{C}{rb}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) \vect{\nu}
	+ \left(\lrdmatr{C}{a}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right) + \matr{D}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right)\right) \ldvect{\nu}{r}
	+ \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) = \vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics}
\end{align}

In these equations:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\vect{\eta} = \left[\scal{x}, \scal{y}, \scal{z}, \scal{\phi}, \scal{\theta}, \scal{\psi}\right]^{\top}$ denotes the vehicle position and orientation in the world frame \lframe{W}.
	\item $\vect{\nu} = \left[\scal{u}, \scal{v}, \scal{w}, \scal{p}, \scal{q}, \scal{r}\right]^{\top}$ represents the linear and angular velocity in the body-fixed frame \lframe{B}.
	\item $\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ is the transformation matrix mapping the body-fixed frame to the world frame.
	\item \ldvect{\nu}{r} denotes the relative velocity between the vehicle and the surrounding water, accounting for current disturbances.
	\item \lrdmatr{M}{rb} and \lrdmatr{M}{a} are the rigid-body and added-mass inertia matrices, respectively.
	\item \lrdmatr{C}{rb} and \lrdmatr{C}{a} are the associated Coriolis matrices.
	\item \matr{D} is the hydrodynamic damping matrix.
	\item $\vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ represents the hydrostatic restoring force.
	\item \vect{\omega} accounts for external forces such as wind and wave loads or tether perturbations.
	\item \vect{\tau} is the control input.
\end{itemize}

Under the assumptions of irrotational underwater currents, absence of dynamic ballast systems, and
negligible Coriolis effects at the low to moderate speeds considered, the dynamic model was
simplified (\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}) to:
\begin{align}
	\matr{I} \vect{\dot{\nu}}
	+ \matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) \vect{\nu}
	+ \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)
	= \vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple}
\end{align}

Here, \matr{I} denotes the generalized inertia matrix and \matr{D} denotes the generalized damping
matrix.

Furthermore, \cref{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics,rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple} may be written into a
state-space representation:
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\lrdmatr{I}{6\times 6} & 0        \\
		0                      & \matr{I}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\vect{\dot{\eta}} \\
		\vect{\dot{\nu}}
	\end{bmatrix} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & \matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)  \\
		0 & - \matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\vect{\eta} \\
		\vect{\nu}
	\end{bmatrix}
	+
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		\vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau} - \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

The transformation matrix $\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ introduced in
\cref{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics} is partitioned as
\begin{align}
	\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right) & \lrdmatr{0}{3\times 3}                 \\
		\lrdmatr{0}{3\times 3}                      & \lrdmatr{R}{a}\left(\phi,\theta\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta},\scal{\psi}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is the rotation matrix that maps linear velocities from the body-fixed frame to the world ENU frame.
	\item $\lrdmatr{R}{a}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is the transformation that relates Euler angle rates to angular velocities in the body-fixed frame.
\end{itemize}

The rotation matrix \lrdmatr{R}{l} is obtained by composing three elementary rotations according to
the ZYX Euler angle convention (yaw-pitch-roll). In this convention, the orientation of the body
relative to the world frame is parameterised by:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \scal{\psi}: yaw, a rotation about the world \fvect{z}{W}-axis,
	\item \scal{\theta}: pitch, a rotation about the intermediate \fluvect{y}{W}{'}-axis (after the yaw rotation),
	\item \scal{\phi}: roll, a rotation about the body-fixed \fvect{x}{B}-axis (after the yaw and pitch rotations).
\end{itemize}

Each of these rotations is represented by a basic rotation matrix. \trigsh{}
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{x}}\left(\scal{\phi}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 0                 & 0                  \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}} & -\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\
		0 & \sin{\scal{\phi}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{y}}\left(\scal{\theta}\right) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\theta}}  & 0 & \sin{\scal{\theta}} \\
		0                    & 1 & 0                   \\
		-\sin{\scal{\theta}} & 0 & \cos{\scal{\theta}}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{z}}\left(\scal{\psi}\right) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} & -\sin{\scal{\psi}} & 0 \\
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} & \cos{\scal{\psi}}  & 0 \\
		0                 & 0                  & 1
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

The composition yielded
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta},\scal{\psi}\right) & =
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{z}}\left(\scal{\psi}\right)
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{y}}\left(\scal{\theta}\right)
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{x}}\left(\scal{\phi}\right)                        \\
	                                                                 & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\theta}}                                                         &
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}} - \sin{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} &
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} + \sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}   \\
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\theta}}                                                         &
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}} + \cos{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} &
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} - \cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}   \\
		-\sin{\scal{\theta}}                                                                          &
		\cos{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}                                                         &
		\cos{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\label{eq:rov_rotation_matrix_final}
\end{align}

The transformation \lrdmatr{R}{a} between body angular velocities and Euler angle rates was derived
systematically from the ZYX Euler parameterization. The instantaneous angular velocity vector is
expressed as a linear combination of the contributions from the Euler angle rates:
\begin{align}
	\vect{\omega} & = \dot{\phi}\fvect{x}{B} + \dot{\theta}\fluvect{y}{W}{'} + \dot{\psi}\fvect{z}{W}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_angle_composition}
\end{align}

Since the angular velocity must be expressed in the body-fixed frame, each axis is projected into
\lframe{B}:
\begin{align}
	\fvect{x}{B} & = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\fvect{y}{B} = \lrmatr{R}{\top}{x}\left(\phi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} =
	\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ \cos{\phi} \\ \sin{\phi} \end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\fvect{z}{B} = {\left(\lrdmatr{R}{y}\left(\theta\right)\lrdmatr{R}{x}\left(\phi\right)\right)}^{\top}
	\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} =
	\begin{bmatrix}-\sin{\theta} \\ \cos{\theta}\sin{\phi} \\ \cos{\theta}\cos{\phi} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

Substituting these into \cref{rcc_rcm_srd_angle_composition} and expressing them in the body frame,
the following relation was obtained:
\begin{align}
	\fvect{\omega}{B} & =
	\dot{\phi}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
	+ \dot{\theta}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \cos{\scal{\phi}} \\ \sin{\scal{\phi}} \end{bmatrix}
	+ \dot{\psi}\begin{bmatrix} -\sin{\scal{\theta}} \\ \cos{\scal{\theta}}\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\ \cos{\scal{\theta}}\cos{\scal{\phi}} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

By collecting terms, the relation between angular velocities and Euler angle rates was expressed as
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{p} \\ \scal{q} \\ \scal{r}
	\end{bmatrix} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 0                  & -\sin{\scal{\theta}}                 \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}}  & \cos{\scal{\theta}}\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\
		0 & -\sin{\scal{\phi}} & \cos{\scal{\theta}}\cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{\phi}} \\ \scal{\dot{\theta}} \\ \scal{\dot{\psi}}
	\end{bmatrix} =
	\lrmatr{R}{-1}{a}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{\phi}} \\ \scal{\dot{\theta}} \\ \scal{\dot{\psi}}
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\lrdmatr{R}{a}                   & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & \sin{\scal{\phi}}\tan{\scal{\theta}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}\tan{\scal{\theta}} \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}}                    & -\sin{\scal{\phi}}                   \\
		0 & \sin{\scal{\phi}}\cos{\scal{\theta}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}\cos{\scal{\theta}}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

This expression becomes singular at $\scal{\theta} = \pm{}\qty{90}{\degree}$, this is called gimbal
lock. This singularity is usually avoided in practice by the fact that the center of buoyancy is
above the center of gravity, which makes the pitch and roll converge to zero in steady state, and
limited actuation capabilities on these axes.

\trigft{}

The hydrostatic restoring vector is defined as
\begin{align}
	\vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\fldvect{f}{B}{g} + \fldvect{f}{B}{b} \\
		\fldvect{m}{B}{g} + \fldvect{m}{B}{b}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where the first three components represent the net forces due to gravity and buoyancy, and the last
three components represent the corresponding restoring moments.

In the world frame \lframe{W}, the gravitational and buoyancy forces are
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{f}{W}{g} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\ 0 \\ -\scal{m}\scal{g}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{1cm}
	\fldvect{f}{W}{b} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\ 0 \\ \ldscal{\rho}{w}\scal{g}\scal{v}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where \scal{m} is the vehicle mass, \scal{g} is the gravitational acceleration, \ldscal{\rho}{w} is
the water density, and \scal{v} is the submerged volume of the vehicle.

The rotation matrix \lrdmatr{R}{l} is used to express these forces in \lframe{B}:
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{f}{B}{g} & = \lrdmatr{R}{l}^{\top}(\phi,\theta,\psi) \fldvect{f}{W}{g} \\
	\fldvect{f}{B}{b} & = \lrdmatr{R}{l}^{\top}(\phi,\theta,\psi) \fldvect{f}{W}{b}
\end{align}

The corresponding restoring moments are obtained from the moment arms of the centre of gravity
$\ldvect{c}{g}=\left[\ldscal{c}{g,x}, \ldscal{c}{g,y}, \ldscal{c}{g,z}\right]^{\top}$ and the
centre of buoyancy $\ldvect{c}{b}=\left[\ldscal{c}{b,x}, \ldscal{c}{b,y},
		\ldscal{c}{b,z}\right]^{\top}$:
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{m}{B}{g} & = \ldvect{r}{g} \times \left( \lrmatr{R}{\top}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right)\fldvect{f}{W}{g} \right) \\
	\fldvect{m}{B}{b} & = \ldvect{r}{b} \times \left( \lrmatr{R}{\top}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right)\fldvect{f}{W}{b} \right)
\end{align}

The generalized inertia matrix \matr{I} is defined to represent the combined rigid-body and
added-mass effects:
\begin{align}
	\matr{I} = \lrdmatr{M}{rb} + \lrdmatr{M}{a}.
\end{align}

The rigid-body inertia matrix \lrdmatr{M}{rb} is expressed as
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		m \lrdmatr{I}{3\times 3}             & -m \matr{S}\left(\ldvect{c}{g}\right) \\
		m \matr{S}\left(\ldvect{c}{g}\right) & \lrdmatr{I}{g}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where \scal{m} denotes the vehicle mass, $\ldvect{c}{g}=\left[\ldscal{c}{g,x}, \ldscal{c}{g,y},
		\ldscal{c}{g,z}\right]^{\top}$ is the position vector of the centre of gravity relative to the
body-fixed origin, $\matr{S}\left(\vect{v}\right)$ denotes the skew-symmetric matrix constructed
from \vect{v}, and \lrdmatr{I}{g} is the moment of inertia matrix about the centre of gravity. The
off-diagonal blocks capture the coupling between translational and rotational dynamics. Finally,
the rigid-body inertia matrix is expanded as
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} = \begin{bmatrix}
		                  \scal{m}                   & 0                          & 0                          & 0                          & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z}  & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y} \\
		                  0                          & \scal{m}                   & 0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z} & 0                          & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x}  \\
		                  0                          & 0                          & \scal{m}                   & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y}  & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x} & 0                          \\
		                  0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z} & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y}  & \ldscal{I}{xx}             & -\ldscal{I}{xy}            & -\ldscal{I}{xz}            \\
		                  \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z}  & 0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x} & -\ldscal{I}{xy}            & \ldscal{I}{yy}             & -\ldscal{I}{yz}            \\
		                  -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y} & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x}  & 0                          & -\ldscal{I}{xz}            & -\ldscal{I}{yz}            & \ldscal{I}{zz}
	                  \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

with \ldscal{I}{xx}, \ldscal{I}{yy}, \ldscal{I}{zz} denoting the moments of inertia about the
body-fixed axes, and \ldscal{I}{xy}, \ldscal{I}{xz}, \ldscal{I}{yz} denoting the products of
inertia.

The added-mass inertia matrix \lrdmatr{M}{a} is defined analogously as a diagonal matrix:
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{a} & = \diag{\left(\ldscal{M}{a, u}, \ldscal{M}{a, v}, \ldscal{M}{a, w}, \ldscal{M}{a, p}, \ldscal{M}{a, q}, \ldscal{M}{a, r}\right)}
\end{align}

where the coefficients are usually determined experimentally.

The hydrodynamic damping matrix $\matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right)$ is typically partitioned into
linear and quadratic components:
\begin{align}
	\matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) & = \lrdmatr{D}{l} + \lrdmatr{D}{q}\left(\vect{\nu}\right).
\end{align}

These are in turn represented as diagonal matrices:
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{D}{l} & = \diag{\left( \ldscal{D}{l,u}, \ldscal{D}{l,v}, \ldscal{D}{l,w}, \ldscal{D}{l,p}, \ldscal{D}{l,q}, \ldscal{D}{l,r} \right)},                                          \\
	\lrdmatr{D}{q} & = \diag{\left( \ldscal{D}{q,u}\abs{u}, \ldscal{D}{q,v}\abs{v}, \ldscal{D}{q,w}\abs{w}, \ldscal{D}{q,p}\abs{p}, \ldscal{D}{q,q}\abs{q}, \ldscal{D}{q,r}\abs{r} \right)}
\end{align}

where the coefficients are also usually determined experimentally.

\subsection{Cable Modelling}
\label{rcc_rcm_cable_modelling}

The cable model used is the catenary model discussed in \cref{cable_modelling}, more specifically
the \verticalmodel{} (\cref{rcc_rcm_cm_d_catenary_curve}).This model is preferred over the
\augmentedmodel{} due to the fact that no satisfactory dynamics was available for its augmentation
parameters at the time of this work.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/vertical_catenary_schematic}
	\caption{
		Catenary curve of length \catL{} defined in the plane \lplane{v}. The construction parameters are
		the sag \catH{}, the elevation difference \catdH{}, and the horizontal span $l = 2\catD + \catdD$
		between attachment points \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}. Note that \catdD{} and \catdH{} are
		signed distances.
	}
	\label{rcc_rcm_cm_d_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

The coordinates of any point along the curve are defined in
\cref{cm_catenary_model,cm_cm_d_catenary_equation} as $\fpoint{P}{v} =
	\left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bound by the attachment points
$\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD-\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catH\right)$. The \coord{z}{v} coordinate of each point is given by
the standard catenary equation:
\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD-\catdD,~\catD\right]
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is the catenary parameter and governs the cable's curvature.

In the context of a chain of \acp{rov}, the mechanical effects of the cable on the robot must be
considered. To this end the horizontal tension force is by definition
(\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_change_of_variable})
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{h} = \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}
\end{align}

Furthermore, the vertical tension force is defined (\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_tension_equation}) as
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{v}\left(x\right) = \ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}\ldscal{L}{1}
	\label{rcc_rcm_cm_vertical_tension}
\end{align}

for a general point \ldpoint{p}{1} along the cable where \ldscal{L}{1} is the arc-length of the
cable between the origin \ldpoint{o}{v} and \ldpoint{p}{1}. In
\cref{cm_catenary_model,cm_cm_dotcm_horizontal_catenary_coordinate} \scal{x} is written as a
function of \ldscal{L}{1}
\begin{align}
	x = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(L_1 \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

Which is then substituted in \cref{rcc_rcm_cm_vertical_tension} to yield
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{v}\left(x\right) & = \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(\catC x\right)} \\
	                            & = \ldscal{T}{h}\sinh{\left(\catC x\right)}
\end{align}

The forces exerted by the cable on the vehicles are then
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{\omega}{v}{a} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\\ 0 \\ \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(-\catC\left(\catD+\catdD\right)\right)} \end{bmatrix} \hspace{1cm}
	\fldvect{\omega}{v}{b} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\\ 0 \\ \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(\catC\catD\right)} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

with \fldvect{\omega}{v}{a} the external perturbation force applied to the robot attached to
\point{a} and \fldvect{\omega}{v}{b} the force applied to the robot attached to \point{b}. It
should be noted that these forces are expressed in the catenary frame \lframe{v} and must be
transformed to the body-fixed frame \lframe{B} of each vehicle before being used in
\cref{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple}.

\subsection{Robot Chain Dynamics}
\label{rcc_rcm_robot_chain_dynamics}

The robot chain system (\cref{rcc_rcm_rcd_schematic}) is defined as a set of \scal{n} underwater
robots connected by tethers, where each robot follows the dynamics described in
\cref{rcc_rcm_single_robot_dynamics}. The state of the system is a concatenation of the state of
the robots
\begin{align}
	\vect{x} = \left[\ldvect{\eta}{0}, \ldvect{\nu}{0}, \dots, \ldvect{\eta}{n-1}, \ldvect{\nu}{n-1}\right]^{\top}
	\label{rcc_rcm_rcd_state_vector}
\end{align}

Similarly, its input is a concatenation of all actuations
\begin{align}
	\vect{u} = \left[\ldvect{\tau}{0}, \dots, \ldvect{\tau}{n-1}\right]^{\top}
	\label{rcc_rcm_rcd_actuation_vector}
\end{align}

Additionally, the perturbation vector for each robot is define as the sum of the perturbation
forces derived from the cable models described in \cref{rcc_rcm_cable_modelling} and associated
with the robot, applied at its center of mass. The robots at the ends of the chain only experience
the perturbation forces from a single cable while the other are subjected to the forces of two
cables.

\section{Controllers}
\label{rcc_controllers}

This section presents the control architectures that were developed to coordinate a chain of
tethered \acp{rov} such that the lead vehicle tracked its assigned motion objective while the group
respected the mechanical and environmental constraints introduced in
\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}. The controllers were required to regulate the coupled dynamics
under disturbances, enforce actuator limits, maintain safe inter-vehicle spacing and seafloor
clearance, and keep tether tensions and geometry within the feasible catenary envelope. Several
controllers were evaluated: an \ac{mpc} that formulated the coupled problem with explicit
constraints (\cref{rcc_c_model_predictive_control}), a path-planning layer that provided feasible
waypoints and references consistent with tether kinematics (\cref{rcc_c_path_planning}), and
lightweight reactive behaviors that handled fast safety overrides and unmodeled events
(\cref{rcc_c_reactive_control}).

\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{mpc}{long}}
\label{rcc_c_model_predictive_control}

\Acl{mpc} is a control strategy that explicitly incorporates the dynamics of
the system, prediction of future states, and operational constraints into a single optimization
problem. The central idea is that, at each time step, the system evolution is predicted forward
over a finite horizon, based on the current state and a sequence of future control inputs. An
optimization problem is then solved to select the sequence of inputs that minimizes a prescribed
cost while satisfying all imposed constraints. Only the first input of this optimal sequence is
applied, and the procedure is repeated at the next step with updated state information. This
mechanism allows the controller to continuously adapt to disturbances and modeling errors while
respecting safety and feasibility conditions (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_schematic}).

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/model_predictive_control_schematic}
	\caption{Schematic of the model predictive control algorithm. The diagram illustrates the state
		and actuation across past, present, and future instants, and shows multiple candidate trajectories
		for $\vect{x}$ and $\vect{u}$ over the prediction horizon, of which the optimal one is selected.}
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_schematic}
\end{figure}

Let us define a discrete-time control system with state \ldvect{x}{k} and control input
\ldvect{u}{k}, both expressed at time \scal{k}:
\begin{equation}
	\ldvect{x}{k+1} = f\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)
	\label{mcc_c_mpc_system}
\end{equation}

This system is obtained by integrating the robot chain dynamics described in
\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}.

The \ac{mpc} problem then consists of finding a sequence of control inputs
$\vect{u}=\left(\ldvect{u}{0}, \dots, \ldvect{u}{h}\right)$ over the horizon \mpch{} that minimizes
the cost
\begin{align}
	\vect{u} = & \argmin{\lures{h}{\top} \matr{P} \res{h}
	+ \frac{1}{\mpch{}}\sum_{k=0}^{\mpch{}-1}{\lures{k}{\top} \matr{Q} \res{k}
	+ \Delta \llvect{u}{\top}{k} \matr{R} \Delta \ldvect{u}{k} + o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)}}
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_equation}
\end{align}

where the first term penalizes the final deviation from the target state, the second term enforces
good tracking of the reference along the horizon, and the third term limits unnecessary variations
in the control input.

The optimization is solved subject to dynamic feasibility and a set of inequality constraints:
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldvect{u}{\text{min}}           \leq & ~\ldvect{u}{k}                                                           & ~\leq & ~\ldvect{u}{\text{max}} \nonumber         \\
	\Delta   \ldvect{u}{\text{min}}  \leq & ~\Delta\ldvect{u}{k}                                                     & ~\leq & ~\Delta\ldvect{u}{\text{max}} \nonumber   \\
	\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{i} - \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}                            & ~     & ~\nonumber                                \\
	                                      & ~\max{\left(\ldcoord{z}{w}{i}, \ldcoord{z}{w}{i+1}\right)}               & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} \nonumber \\
	\ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}         \leq  & ~\ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)}                                                & ~     & ~\nonumber                                \\
	                                      & ~\sqrt{\llscal{\catl{}}{2}{i(i+1)} + \Delta \llscal{\catH{}}{2}{i(i+1)}} & ~\leq & ~\ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} \nonumber        \\
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_constraints}
\end{alignat}

In \cref{rcc_c_mpc_equation,,rcc_c_mpc_constraints}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\res{k} = \ldvect{x}{k} - \llvect{x}{\text{target}}{k}$ is the state deviation from the target,
	\item $\Delta \ldvect{u}{k} = \ldvect{u}{k} - \ldvect{u}{k-1}$ is the incremental control input,
	\item \matr{P}, \matr{Q} and \matr{R} are positive semi-definite matrices weighting the final, state, and control costs, respectively,
	\item $o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)$ is a nonlinear objective term that should be minimized; in the robot chain context, this term can be used to define a preferred inter-robot distance,
	\item \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, \ldscal{\catdH{}}{i(i+1)} and \ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)} are parameters of the catenary model, the sag as well as the vertical and horizontal inter-robot distances respectively, representing the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
	\item $\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$, $\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$ are the bounds of the control input,
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}} is the known elevation of the seafloor which neither the robot nor the cable should cross,
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} is the known elevation of the water surface which the robot cannot physically cross,
	\item \ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} is the maximum allowable distance between two consecutive robots in the chain based on the tether length,
	\item \ldscal{dh}{\text{min}} is the minimum allowable horizontal distance between consecutive robots, both to ensure separation and to avoid a singularity in the catenary model when \catl{} is zero.
\end{itemize}

If the robot is operated in a constrained environment such as a pool, additional box constraints
could be imposed on the position:
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldcoord{x}{w}{\text{pool}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{x}{w}{i} & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{x}{w}{\text{pool}} \nonumber \\
	\ldcoord{y}{w}{\text{pool}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{y}{w}{i} & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{y}{w}{\text{pool}}
\end{alignat}

Once the optimal control input is found, the first element of the sequence is applied to the
system, and the optimization problem is solved again at the next time step. In order to reduce the
optimization time, \vect{u} may be considered constant, piecewise constant on a fraction of the
horizon, or parameterized as a polynomial function over the horizon.

\subsection{Path Planning}
\label{rcc_c_path_planning}

A path planning problem was formulated to generate feasible trajectories for the robots while
respecting the tether kinematics and the constraints of the chain. The resulting trajectory is then
used with a simple \ac{pid} controller on the pose of the system. The path planning optimization
problem consists of finding a sequence of waypoints $\vect{p}=\left(\ldvect{x}{0}, \dots,
	\ldvect{x}{n}\right)$ over a finite horizon \mpch{} in order to minimize the distance of the path
to the desired pose of the robots while enforcing kinematic feasibility constraints:
\begin{align}
	\vect{p} = & \argmin{\lures{h}{\top} \matr{P} \res{h}
	+ \frac{1}{\mpch{}}\sum_{k=0}^{\mpch{}-1}{\lures{k}{\top} \matr{Q} \res{k}  + o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)}}
	\label{rcc_c_pp_cost}
\end{align}

subject to
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}} \leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{i} - \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}                            & ~     & \label{rcc_c_pp_constraints} \nonumber    \\
	\leq                                 & ~\max{\left(\ldcoord{z}{w}{i}, \ldcoord{z}{w}{i+1}\right)}               & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} \nonumber \\
	\ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}         \leq & ~\ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)}                                                & ~     & ~\nonumber                                \\
	                                     & ~\sqrt{\llscal{\catl{}}{2}{i(i+1)} + \Delta \llscal{\catH{}}{2}{i(i+1)}} & ~\leq & ~\ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} \nonumber        \\
	                                     & ~\norm{\ldvect{\nu}{i}}                                                  & ~\leq & ~\ldvect{\nu}{\text{max}}
\end{alignat}

where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\res{k} = \ldvect{x}{k} - \llvect{x}{\text{target}}{k}$ denotes the state deviation from the target,
	\item \matr{P} and \matr{Q} are positive semi-definite matrices weighting the final and state costs, respectively,
	\item $o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)$ is a nonlinear objective term that should be minimized; in the robot chain context, this term can be used to define a preferred inter-robot distance,
	\item \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, \ldscal{\catdH{}}{i(i+1)} and \ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)} are parameters of the catenary model, the sag as well as the vertical and horizontal inter-robot distances respectively, representing the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}}, \ldscal{dp}{\text{max}}, \ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}, and \ldscal{dh}{\text{max}} are geometrical bounds ensuring safe separation, catenary feasibility, and environment clearance,
	\item \ldvect{\nu}{\text{max}} is the maximum allowable velocity of each robot.
\end{itemize}

Once the optimal waypoint sequence was found, it is passed to a controller as a reference
trajectory. The optimization problem, or a subset of it (starting from the closest or current point
in the reference trajectory, for example) may be solved again according to different criteria such
as time, tracking error, etc; this is called replanning.

\subsection{Reactive Control}
\label{rcc_c_reactive_control}

In addition to the path planning scheme, a reactive control strategy was implemented to regulate
the tether geometry on a peer-to-peer basis. This method is based on the catenary-based control law
proposed in \cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2017}, originally formulated as a visual
servoing algorithm. In their work, the tether is modeled as a catenary, and the control objective
is to regulate its shape parameters by acting on the motion of the follower robot with respect to
the leader. The visual servoing loop estimates the tether features directly from the image plane
and closes the loop by minimizing the feature error through the catenary interaction matrix.

In our framework, we replace the visual estimation step with direct pose localization of the
robots. The relative positions and orientations of neighboring robots in the chain are measured,
allowing the catenary parameters between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1} to be explicitly estimated.
The reactive controller then commands the catenary sag parameter \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, which
represents the vertical deflection of the tether, as the primary regulation variable. The control
law is expressed as
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{v}{i+1} = - \lambda \lrumatr{L}{\dagger} \left(
	\vect{s} - \luvect{s}{\text{target}}
	\right)
	\label{rcc_c_rc_law}
\end{align}

where \ldvect{v}{i+1} is the velocity of robot \scal{i+1}, \matr{L} is the interaction matrix (see
\cite{moreiraVisualServoingDeformable2019} for details) relating the catenary shape parameters and
their derivatives to the relative motion of the robots, $\scal{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^{+}$ is a
control gain, and \ldvect{s}{i(i+1)} is feature vector such that
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{s}{i(i+1)} = \left[\frac{\ldscal{H}{i(i+1)}}{\ldscal{H}{\text{max}}}, \sin{\scal{\alpha}}, \frac{\ldscal{\Delta H}{i(i+1)} + \ldscal{\Delta H}{max}}{2\ldscal{\Delta H}{max}}\right]
\end{align}

with \ldscal{H}{i(i+1)} being the sag of the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
\ldscal{H}{\text{max}} the maximum sag allowed, \scal{\alpha} is the angle between robot
\scal{i+1}'s \vect{x} axis projected in the horizontal plane and the vector going from robot
\scal{i+1} to robot \scal{i} also projected in the horizontal plane, \ldscal{\Delta H}{i(i+1)}
being the vertical distance between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1}, and \ldscal{\Delta
	H}{\text{max}} the maximum absolute vertical distance allowed.

This peer-to-peer formulation enables each robot to locally and concurrently regulate their tether
with the preceding robot all the way up to the leader robot which performs a task, ensuring that
the global chain maintains feasible catenary geometries without over-constraining the system.

\section{Simulation}
\label{rcc_simulation}

The controllers is assessed through the analysis of a scenario comprising four tethered robots
($\scal{n}=4$ in \cref{rcc_rcm_rcd_state_vector,rcc_rcm_rcd_actuation_vector}). The experiment
takes place in a numerical tank with varying depth, and infinite width and length
(\cref{rcc_s_mpc_ss_tank}). The tethers are assumed to be negatively buoyant with a known fixed
length as discussed in \cref{cable_modelling}. The leader robot as well as the two first follower
robots are considered as \acp{rov} while the fourth robot is modelled as an \ac{usv} in order to
represent the link to a surface control vehicle.

The leader robot is tasked with tracking a reference trajectory, while the two follower robots must
ensure that the constraints are not violated. It is imperative that they maintain a safe distance
from one another since proximity could result in contact with the floor or other obstacles.

To test the system's ability to cope with these constraints, we considered a trajectory in which
the leading robot passes over a raised peak in which the tether would be entangled without proper
consideration of the tether shape. This allowed us to check that, even in this critical case, the
following robots were able to position themselves so that the cables did not touch the seafloor.

The leader reference trajectory is a sine of amplitude \qty{3}{\m} along the \ldvect{z}{w}-axis
centered on a depth of \qty{2.5}{\m} and a smooth curve from \qty{2}{\m} to \qty{-3}{\m} and back
on the \ldvect{x}{w}-axis with speed equal to zero at both ends (\cref{rcc_s_mpc_ss_tank}). The
reference on the \ldvect{y}{w}-axis as well as the orientation is constant and equal to zero.
During this planned trajectory, the leader is closest to the seafloor at times \qty{10}{\s},
\qty{25}{\s} and \qty{40}{\s} and passes over the peak at times \qty{16}{\s} and \qty{34}{\s}.

The leader robot initial state is $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{0}=[2,0,1, 0, 0, 0]$ and the two follower
robots'states are $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{1}=[2.5,0,1, 0, 0, 0]$ and $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{2}=[3,0,1, 0, 0,
	0]$. The \ac{usv} is positioned at $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{3}=[3.5,0,0,0,0,\pi/2]$.

\subsubsection{Implementation}
\label{rcc_s_mpc_implementation}

The simulation (\citeurl{filliungPympc_usage2025}) integrates the robots chain dynamics introduced
in \cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling} and follows the scenario outlined previously. The simulation is
run with a time step of \qty{0.1}{\second} for \num{500} steps, i.e. \qty{50}{\second}. The
\ac{mpc} horizon is \num{10} steps, i.e. \qty{1}{\second}. The problem is solved with the
\texttt{scipy.minimize} function in Python with a tolerance of \num{1e-6} and a maximum number of
iterations set to \num{100}.

A recovery procedure was implemented for cases in which the \ac{mpc} failed to converge to a
solution. In this procedure, the optimization tolerance was progressively increased until either
convergence was achieved -at which point the tolerance was reduced again- or the tolerance reached
a predefined maximum value.

The matrices \matr{P} and \matr{Q} (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_equation}) are defined in such a way that the
position of the leader is assigned a weight of \num{10} and its orientation a weight of \num{1}.
The orientation of the other robots is also assigned a weight of \num{1}, while their position is
not weighted, leaving them free on these axes. Matrix \matr{R} (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_equation}) has a
weight of zero on linear actuation and a weight of \num{1} on angular actuation with the exception
of the orientation of the \ac{usv}. These tuned values are obtained on a trial-and-error basis. The
objective term $o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)$ aims to minimize the robots' linear
speed and make the inter-robot distances as close to \qty{1.5}{\m} as possible. This term is
weighted as \num{0.01}.

\subsection{Path Planning}
\label{rcc_s_path_planning}

\subsubsection{Implementation}
\label{rcc_s_pp_implementation}

The simulation (\citeurl{filliungPympc_usage2025}) was implemented by integrating the robot chain
dynamics introduced in \cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling} and by following the previously described
scenario. It was executed with a time step of \qty{0.1}{\second} over \num{500} steps,
corresponding to a simulated duration of \qty{50}{\second}. The optimization problem was solved
using the \texttt{scipy.minimize} function in Python.

A recovery procedure was introduced for cases in which the optimization failed to converge. In this
procedure, the optimization tolerance was progressively increased until convergence was achieved,
at which point the tolerance was reduced again. If no convergence occurred, the tolerance was
capped at a predefined maximum value.

Two distinct simulations were performed using different replanning strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item In the first configuration, the path was optimized at every step for the following \num{10} steps,
	      corresponding to \qty{1}{\second}. The tolerance was set to \num{1e-3} and the maximum number of
	      iterations to \num{100}. The matrices \matr{P} and \matr{Q} (\cref{rcc_c_pp_cost}) were defined
	      such that the leader's position was assigned a weight of \num{10} and its orientation a weight of
	      \num{1}. The orientation of the follower robots was also weighted with \num{1}, while their
	      positions remained unweighted. The objective term $o\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right)$
	      penalized the robots' linear speed and aimed to maintain inter-robot distances close to
	      \qty{1.5}{\m}. This term was weighted with a factor of \num{0.01}.
	\item In the second configuration, the path was replanned every \num{10} steps or whenever the leader's
	      tracking error exceeded \qty{1}{\m}. The optimization horizon covered the following \num{20} steps,
	      corresponding to \qty{2}{\second}, with a tolerance of \num{1e-5} and a maximum of \num{100}
	      iterations. The weighting matrices \matr{P} and \matr{Q} were defined as in the first
	      configuration, and the same inter-robot objective was applied. In this case, however, the objective
	      term was weighted with a factor of \num{20.0}.
\end{enumerate}

These parameter values were determined by trial and error. Additionally, the constraints introduced
in \cref{rcc_c_pp_constraints} were imposed and are summarized in
\cref{rcc_s_pp_i_constrains_values}.

\paragraph{First Configuration}
\label{rcc_s_pp_r_first_configuration}

The trajectories of the robots (\cref{rcc_s_pp_r_fc_poses}) show that the leader tracked the
planned path tightly in the horizontal plane: errors along \ldvect{x}{w} and \ldvect{y}{w} remained
bounded within a few decimeters (see also \cref{rcc_s_pp_r_fc_error}). In contrast, the
\ldvect{z}{w}-axis response exhibited a marked loss of performance whenever the reference underwent
a steep variation. Instead of brief overshoots followed by rapid convergence, the vertical error
accumulated over several seconds, leading to a sustained bias before partial recovery. Analysis of
the error logs confirmed a peak Euclidean tracking error norm of \qty{2.49}{\m} at \qty{15.9}{\s},
dominated by the vertical component (\qty{2.19}{\m}). The RMS errors were limited to \qty{0.16}{\m}

concealed a substantial degradation in vertical control authority.

Two mechanisms explain this behavior. First, the two-stage architecture (periodic path optimization
followed by low-level \ac{pid} tracking) introduced an intrinsic delay: the predictive layer
generated waypoints at discrete replanning instants, while the inner loop responded continuously
but without preview. This excited the derivative component of the \ac{pid} controller and induced a
lagging integral accumulation, producing a quasi wind-up effect in the vertical channel.

\paragraph{Discussion and Comparison of the two Configurations}

A comparison of the two configurations highlights a trade-off between tracking quality, constraint
robustness, and computational feasibility. The first configuration achieved accurate horizontal
tracking with only marginal clearance infringements, but persistent vertical errors accumulated due
to the lag introduced by the two-step control architecture: the planner provided new waypoints only
at discrete instants, while the inner \ac{pid} loop reacted continuously but without preview,
compounding phase delay in the vertical channel. The second configuration reduced the overall RMS
tracking error norm and improved average spatial coherence, yet it also produced severe clearance
violations in the leading cable and frequent distance constraint breaches. Most critically, the
sporadic replanning events in the second configuration imposed computational demands far beyond
real-time feasibility, with solve times several orders of magnitude larger than the horizon length.
Taken together, these results indicate that while both approaches can generate feasible paths, the
first configuration provides a more balanced compromise between accuracy and practicality, whereas
the second configuration requires additional refinement -particularly in clearance handling, solver
efficiency, and mitigation of control lag- before it can be deployed in real-time scenarios.

\subsection{Reactive Control}
\label{rcc_s_reactive_control}

\subsubsection{Implementation}
\label{rcc_s_rc_implementation}

The simulation (\citeurl{filliungPympc_usage2025}) was implemented by integrating the robot chain
dynamics introduced in \cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling} and by following the previously described
scenario. It was executed with a time step of \qty{0.1}{\second} over \num{500} steps,
corresponding to a simulated duration of \qty{50}{\second}. Owing to the reactive nature of the
controller described in \cref{rcc_c_rc}, the leader was controlled by a simple \ac{pid} regulator
with proportional, integral, and derivative gains of \num{100}, \num{10}, and \num{6500}
respectively in the \vect{x}-\vect{y} plane. In the \vect{z} direction, the gains were set to
\num{100}, \num{10}, and \num{5000}. The angular control used proportional, integral, and
derivative gains of \num{0.1}, \num{0}, and \num{0}. The follower robots were driven by the visual
servoing controller, with a gain matrix weighting horizontal speed by \num{0.1}, vertical speed by
\num{3}, and angular speed by \num{0.005}. The maximum allowable sag \ldscal{H}{\text{max}} and
maximum vertical offset \ldscal{\Delta H}{\text{max}} were set to half of the cable length, i.e.
\qty{1.5}{\m}.

Different target features were assigned to each follower. For Robot~1, following the leader, the
target was set to $\left[0.9, 0.0, 0.75\right]$, keeping it above the leader and limiting cable
contact with the seafloor. For Robot~2, the target was $\left[0.5, 0.0, 0.25\right]$, maintaining
elevation above Robot~1. For Robot~3, the target was $\left[0.9, 0.75, 0.75\right]$, ensuring it
remained above Robot~2.

A recovery procedure was introduced for cases in which the pseudo-inverse of the interaction matrix
could not be computed, for example when inter-robot configurations reduced its rank. In such cases,
the output twist was set to zero until the next computation step.

All parameter values were selected by trial and error.

stretching and accumulated control lag pushed the configuration beyond feasible bounds, at which
point the reactive law no longer provided restoring action. Once constraints were exceeded, the
closed-loop dynamics diverged rapidly, underscoring the structural limitation of a purely reactive
strategy when applied to multi-robot chains subject to coupled kinematic and clearance
requirements.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter proposed a unified framework for modeling and controlling a chain of tethered
underwater robots and compared three control strategies on a common scenario with varying
bathymetry. The chain model combines a simplified marine craft dynamics with a vertical catenary
representation to inject physically consistent tether forces into each vehicle's equations of
motion. This enabled a principled definition of safety and feasibility constraints: seafloor and
surface clearance, inter-robot spacing consistent with tether length, and actuator bounds.

The comparative study highlighted a clear performance hierarchy. The constrained \ac{mpc}
maintained the best overall constraint discipline and the smallest tracking residuals for the
leader (typical errors below decimeter scale except during planned peak crossings), and preserved
comfortable cable clearances for deeper positions. Its principal limitation was computational:
median solve times were on the order of the prediction horizon and heavy-tailed spikes occasionally
exceeded it, which threatens real-time feasibility without additional engineering, code
optimization, solver-friendly model and constraints formulation.

The path-planning plus \ac{pid} scheme offered good horizontal tracking and acceptable behavior
when replanned frequently with short horizons, yet it exhibited vertical lag that translated into
clearance and spacing stress around steep bathymetric transitions. A more sparse, event-triggered
replanning reduced average tracking error but produced significant clearance violations in the
leading span and prohibitively long solve times during replans, making this setting unsuitable for
real-time deployment in its current form.

The reactive catenary-based visual servoing controller regulated local tether shape effectively at
the start and kept large safety margins while the configuration remained feasible, but it lacked
guarantees once constraints became active. Without prediction, accumulated lag and formation
stretching eventually pushed the system beyond feasible geometry, after which the closed loop
diverged.

In practice, if constraint satisfaction is paramount and computation allows, \ac{mpc} is the most
reliable option among those tested. When computation is limited, a hierarchical design becomes
appealing: a fast planner that respects catenary geometry to provide previewed references, a
reactive layer for rapid local shaping, and a lightweight safety mechanism that enforces minimum
clearance and spacing (e.g., via control barrier functions) regardless of the nominal controller.

Promising directions for future work include: distributed or hierarchical MPC to reduce per-step
solve time; SQP with warm starts; adaptive weighting and horizon scheduling near terrain features;
extension to the \augmentedmodel{} proposed in \cref{cable_modelling} when a dynamic representation
of its parameters is available. Preliminary experiments were conducted with a reduced chain
configuration in a water tank (see \cref{e_robot_chain_control_experiment}) but more work remains
to be done to validate the approach further.

\section{Underwater robot chain modelization}

\subsection{Frames definition}

In accordance with the SNAME~\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011} convention, the world reference
frame, designated as $w$, is aligned with the NED convention: $\mathbf{x}_w$-axis is oriented
towards the North, $\mathbf{y}_w$-axis towards the East and the $\mathbf{z}_w$-axis is oriented
downwards, perpendicular to the Earth's surface. The origin $\mathbf{O}_w$ of the reference frame
is placed on the surface at a fixed point in the navigation zone. It is assumed that the navigation
zone is locally flat and that the reference frame is a Galilean reference frame. The
$\mathbf{z}_w$-coordinate directly represents the immersion depth of the vehicle
(\cref{fig:frames}).

The robot frame is a Cartesian frame affixed to the robot body whose origin is at the centre of
mass. The $\mathbf{x}_r$-axis points forward, the $\mathbf{z}_r$-axis points downward, and the
$\mathbf{y}_r$-axis is oriented to the right.

Henceforth, the frame in which a coordinate is expressed will be indicated by means of an upper
left index. For the sake of readability and when there's no ambiguity, this notation is not used in
certain equations.

\subsection{Single robot dynamics}\label{sec:robot_dynamics}

The state of an underwater robot is given by
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{\eta},\mathbf{\nu} \right]^T
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{\eta} = \left[{}^wx, {}^wy, {}^wz, {}^w\phi, {}^w\theta,
		{}^w\psi\right]^T$ is the pose of the robot in the world frame and
$\mathbf{\nu} =\left[{}^ru, {}^rv, {}^rw, {}^rp, {}^rq,{}^rr\right]^T$ is the
linear and rotational velocity of the robot in the body frame. The robot
dynamics are~\cite{creuze_robots_2014, fossen_handbook_2011}:
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\text{Id} & 0          \\
		0         & \mathbf{I}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\dot{\mathbf{\eta}} \\
		\dot{\mathbf{\nu}}
	\end{bmatrix} & =
	\begin{bmatrix*}[l]
		0 & \mathbf{J}\left(\mathbf{\eta}\right) \\
		0 & -\mathbf{D}
	\end{bmatrix*}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\mathbf{\eta} \\
		\mathbf{\nu}
	\end{bmatrix} +
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		\mathbf{\tau} - \mathbf{s}\left(\eta\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{J}\left(\mathbf{\eta}\right)$ is the transformation matrix from
the world frame to the body frame:
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{J}\left(\eta\right) & = \begin{bmatrix}
		                                \mathbf{R}^w_r\left(\eta\right) & \mathbf{0}_{3\times3}       \\
		                                \mathbf{0}_{3\times3}           & \mathbf{T}\left(\eta\right)
	                                \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
with $\mathbf{R}^w_r\left(\eta\right)$ is the rotation matrix that represents
the orientation of the robot in the NED reference frame,
$\mathbf{T}\left(\eta\right)$ is defined as:
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{T}\left(\eta\right) & =\begin{bmatrix}
		                               1 & \sin\phi \tan\theta   & \cos\phi \tan\theta   \\
		                               0 & \cos\phi              & -\sin\phi             \\
		                               0 & \sin\phi / \cos\theta & \cos\phi / \cos\theta \\
	                               \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

$\mathbf{I}$ is the inertial matrix
\begin{align*}
	 & \mathbf{I}=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!                                       % \\
	 & \begin{bmatrix}
		   m         & 0         & 0         & 0         & {}^rz_mm  & -{}^ry_mm \\
		   0         & m         & 0         & -{}^rz_mm & 0         & {}^rx_mm  \\
		   m         & 0         & m         & {}^ry_mm  & -{}^rx_mm & 0         \\
		   0         & -{}^rz_mm & {}^ry_mm  & I_x       & -I_{xy}   & -I_{xz}   \\
		   {}^rz_mm  & 0         & -{}^rx_mm & -I_{xy}   & I_y       & -I_{yz}   \\
		   -{}^ry_mm & {}^rx_mm  & 0         & -I_{xz}   & -I_{yz}   & I_z
	   \end{bmatrix}\notag
\end{align*}
with $I_x$, $I_y$ and $I_z$ the moments of inertia, $I_{xy}$, $I_{yz}$ and
$I_{xz}$ the products of inertia, $m$ the robot's mass and $\left[{}^rx_m,
		{}^ry_m, {}^rz_m\right]^T$ its center of mass. $\mathbf{D} = \diag{\left(D_u,
		D_v, D_w, D_p, D_q, D_r\right)}$ is the hydrodynamic drag matrix,
$\mathbf{\tau}$ is the control input for each degree of freedom and
$\mathbf{s}\left(\eta\right)$ is the hydrostatic force i.e.~weight and
buoyancy.

\subsection{Chain dynamics}

The robot chain system is defined as a set of $n$ underwater robots connected by tethers, where
each robot follows the dynamics described in \cref{sec:robot_dynamics}. The state of the system is
a concatenation of the state of the robots
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{x} = \left[\mathbf{\eta}_1, \mathbf{\nu}_1, \dots, \mathbf{\eta}_n, \mathbf{\nu}_n\right]^T
\end{align}

Similarly, its input is a concatenation of all actuations
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{u} = \left[\mathbf{\tau}_1, \dots, \mathbf{\tau}_n\right]^T
\end{align}

\subsection{Cable model}\label{sec:cable_model}

The shape of a homogeneous hanging cable of length $L$, subjected only to its own weight and
buoyancy, is modelled as a catenary curve. It is expressed in the catenary frame $c$
(\cref{fig:catenary}) by the following expression:
\begin{align}
	{}^cz = \frac{\cosh{({}^cx\text{ }C)} - 1}{C} &  & \forall C \in \mathbb{R}^*_+
	\label{eq:catenary}
\end{align}

The catenary parameter $C$ is derived from the horizontal distance $l$ and the vertical distance
$\Delta H$ between the two endpoints as well as the length $L$ of the curve.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\input{figures/catenary}
	\caption{Catenary curve of length $L$ between two consecutive robots $i$ and $i+1$}
	\label{fig:catenary}
\end{figure}

The sag $H$ is given by the solution of~\cref{equ:sag} and used to determine the vertical component
of the lowest point of the catenary~\cite{druptInertialmeasurementbasedCatenaryShape2022}. Let
$H_{i(i+1)}$ denote the sag between robots $i$ and $i+1$ then, the elevation of the lowest point of
the catenary is $H_{i(i+1)} + {}^wz_i$.%between robots $i$ and $i+1$ 
\begin{equation}\label{equ:sag}
	0  = aH^2 + bH + C \text{; }
	\begin{cases}
		a  = 4C^2\left(\Delta H^2 - L^2\right)  % \notag                     
		\\
		b  = 4C\left(\Delta H^2 - L^2\right)(C\Delta H + 2)   %\notag 
		\\
		c  = \left(C\left(L^2 - \Delta H^2\right) - 2\Delta H\right)^2
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\section{Model predictive controller}
\label{sec:control}

Let~\cref{equ:discrete_sys} be a discrete-time control system with state $\mathbf{x}_k$ and control
input $\mathbf{u}_k$, both expressed at time $k$.
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k) \label{equ:discrete_sys}
\end{equation}

A model predictive control problem consists of finding a sequence of control inputs $\mathbf{u}=
	\left(\mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_h\right)$ over a \emph{future} time horizon $h$ in order to
minimize some prescribed cost function~(\cref{fig:mpc}). In addition to the aforementioned dynamic
constraint~\cref{equ:discrete_sys}, it is also possible to impose algebraic constraints on the
variables associated with the problem. In the case of the present paper, it takes the form:
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{u} = & \argmin{\left(\mathbf{x}_h^T \mathbf{P} \mathbf{x}_h + \frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}{\mathbf{x}_k^T \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x}_k + \Delta \mathbf{u}_k^T \mathbf{R} \Delta \mathbf{u}_k}\right)}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
	\text{subject to } \Delta   \mathbf{u}_{\text{min}} \leq\Delta\mathbf{u}_k & \leq\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}\notag \\
	H_{i(i+1)} + {}^wz_i                                                       & \leq z_{\text{floor}}\notag             \\ \sqrt{l_{i(i+1)}^2 +
	\Delta H_{i(i+1)}^2}                                                       & \leq d_{\text{max}}\notag               \\ dh_{\text{min}}\leq
	l_{i(i+1)}                                                                 & \leq dh_{\text{max}}\notag              \\ \norm{\nu_{i}} & \leq
	   \nu_{\text{max}}\notag
\end{align}
where $\Delta \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{u}_k - \mathbf{u}_{k-1}$ and $\mathbf{P}$,
$\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ are positive semi-definite matrices weighting the
final, state and control costs, respectively; $\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and
$\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$, $\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and
$\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$ are the bounds of the control input.

Additionally, in the context of the robot chain, we introduce specific constraints. These
constraints are imposed on the state of the system such that the robots and the tether are not
compromised. The cable's sag is obtained as described in \cref{sec:cable_model}. Using this
information, the position of lowest point of the tether is constrained given information on the
environment. Furthermore, the distance between two consecutive robots is constrained by the length
$L$ of the tether with safety margins. Finally, the speed of the robots is constrained as a safety
measure.

Once the optimal control input is found, the first control input is applied to the system and the
optimization problem is solved again at the next time step.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\input{figures/mpc}
	\caption{Schematic of the model predictive control algorithm}
	\label{fig:mpc}
\end{figure}

In order to speed up the optimization time, $\mathbf{u}$ may be peace-wise constant over the
horizon. In this paper we consider a constant control input
\begin{align}
	\mathbf{u}_i = \mathbf{u}_j \forall i, j \in \{0,\hdots, h\}^2
\end{align}

\section{Simulation}

\subsection{Setup}

The controller is assessed through the analysis of a scenario comprising four tethered robots
($n=4$). The experiment takes place in a numerical tank with a depth of 4 meters, and infinite
width and length (\cref{fig:three_d_view}). The tethers are assumed to be negatively buoyant with a
known fixed length~\cite{druptValidityCatenaryModel2022}. In previous works, the chain of ROVs move
freely~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2017,talkeCatenaryTetherShape2018,laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.
Whereas in this scenario, the fourth robot \textit{considered as a fixed point} in order to
represent the link to a surface control station. Consequently, the operational range the rest of
the chain system is constrained.

The leader robot is tasked with tracking a reference trajectory, while the two follower robots must
ensure that the constraints are not violated. It is imperative that they maintain a safe distance
from one another since proximity could result in contact with the floor or other obstacles. To
prevent the follower robots from spinning, their orientation is controlled to remain aligned with
that of the leader (\cref{tab:constraints}).

To test the system's ability to cope with these constraints, we tested a trajectory in which the
leading robot touches the ground. This allowed us to check that, even in this critical case, the
following robots were able to position themselves so that the cables did not touch the ground. The
leader reference trajectory is a sine of amplitude \qty{3.}{\m} along the $\mathbf{z}_w$-axis
centered on a depth of \qty{2.5}{\m} (\cref{fig:pose_z_2}) and a smooth curve from
${}^wx=$\qty{2}{\m} to ${}^wx=$\qty{-3}{\m} and back with speed equal to zero at both ends
(\cref{fig:pose_x_2}). the reference on $\mathbf{y}_w$-axis as well as the orientation is constant
and equal to zero. The leader robot initial state is $\eta_0=[2,0,-1, 0, 0, 0]$ and the two
follower robots'states are $\eta_1=[2.5,0,-1, 0, 0, 0]$ and $\eta_2=[3,0,-1, 0, 0, 0]$. The fixed
point is positioned at $[3.5,0,0,0,0,0]$.

\subsection{Implementation}

The simulation integrates the robots dynamics introduced in \cref{sec:robot_dynamics}. The
simulation is run with a time step of \qty{0.01}{\second} for 2000 steps, \text{i.e.}
\qty{20}{\second}. The MPC horizon is 25 steps, \text{i.e.} \qty{.25}{\second}. The problem is
solved with the \texttt{scipy.minimize} function in Python with a tolerance of \num{1e-3}.

The matrices $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ are defined in such a way that the position of the
leader is assigned a weight of 10 and its orientation a weight of 5. The orientation of the other
robots is also assigned a weight of 5, while their position is not weighted, leaving them free on
these axes. Matrix $\mathbf{R}$ has a weight of zero on linear actuation and a weight of \num{1e3}
on angular actuation. No matrix weights on the fixed point.

In order to differentiate the simulation from the model employed in the MPC prediction horizon,
their integration methodologies diverge. The simulation utilizes a Runge-Kutta integration of order
4~\cite{ralstonRungeKuttaMethodsMinimum1962}, whereas the MPC prediction employs an Euler-explicit
scheme. The shape of each cable is deduced from the position of its attachment points using the
catenary equation \cref{eq:catenary}.

The code is available at {\small\url{https://github.com/LaboratoireCosmerTOULON/rov_chain_mpc/}}.

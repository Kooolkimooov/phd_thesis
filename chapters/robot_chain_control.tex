\section{Robot Chain Modelling}
\label{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}

The world frame, denoted as \lframe{W} when required, is defined as an earth-fixed, right-handed
frame. A local \ac{enu} convention is adopted unless explicitly stated otherwise: the \vect{x}-axis
points east, the \vect{y}-axis points north, and the \vect{z}-axis points upward. The origin of
this frame is located at the water surface (\cref{rcc_rcm_frames}).

The body-fixed frame, denoted as \lframe{B} when required, is rigidly attached to the vehicle with
its origin at the centre of gravity. It is defined as a right-handed frame in which the
\vect{x}-axis points forward, the \vect{y}-axis points to the left, and the \vect{z}-axis points
upward (\cref{rcc_rcm_frames}).

\subsection{Single Robot Dynamics}
\label{rcc_rcm_single_robot_dynamics}

The dynamic model of a \ac{rov} is described by the classical six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body
model formulated for marine craft~\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}:
\begin{align}
	\vect{\dot{\eta}} & = \matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) \vect{\nu} \label{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics} \\
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} \vect{\dot{\nu}} + \lrdmatr{M}{a} \ldvect{\dot{\nu}}{r}
	                  & + \lrdmatr{C}{rb}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) \vect{\nu}
	+ \left(\lrdmatr{C}{a}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right) + \matr{D}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right)\right) \ldvect{\nu}{r}
	+ \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) = \vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics}
\end{align}

In these equations:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\vect{\eta} = \left[\scal{x}, \scal{y}, \scal{z}, \scal{\phi}, \scal{\theta}, \scal{\psi}\right]^{\top}$ denotes the vehicle position and orientation in the world frame \lframe{W}.
	\item $\vect{\nu} = \left[\scal{u}, \scal{v}, \scal{w}, \scal{p}, \scal{q}, \scal{r}\right]^{\top}$ represents the linear and angular velocity in the body-fixed frame \lframe{B}.
	\item $\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ is the transformation matrix mapping the body-fixed frame to the world frame.
	\item \ldvect{\nu}{r} denotes the relative velocity between the vehicle and the surrounding water, accounting for current disturbances.
	\item \lrdmatr{M}{rb} and \lrdmatr{M}{a} are the rigid-body and added-mass inertia matrices, respectively.
	\item \lrdmatr{C}{rb} and \lrdmatr{C}{a} are the associated Coriolis matrices.
	\item \matr{D} is the hydrodynamic damping matrix.
	\item $\vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ represents the hydrostatic restoring force.
	\item \vect{\omega} accounts for external forces such as wind and wave loads.
	\item \vect{\tau} is the control input.
\end{itemize}

Under the assumptions of irrotational underwater currents, absence of dynamic ballast systems, and
negligible Coriolis effects at the low to moderate speeds considered, the dynamic model was
simplified to~\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}:
\begin{align}
	\matr{I} \vect{\dot{\nu}}
	+ \matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) \vect{\nu}
	+ \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)
	= \vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple}
\end{align}

Here, \matr{I} denotes the generalized inertia matrix and \matr{D} denotes the generalized damping
matrix.

Furthermore, \cref{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics,rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple} were combined into a
state-space representation:
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\lrdmatr{I}{6\times 6} & 0        \\
		0                      & \matr{I}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\vect{\dot{\eta}} \\
		\vect{\dot{\nu}}
	\end{bmatrix} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & \matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)  \\
		0 & - \matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\vect{\eta} \\
		\vect{\nu}
	\end{bmatrix}
	+
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		\vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau} - \vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

The transformation matrix $\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right)$ introduced in
\cref{rcc_rcm_srd_kinematics} is partitioned as
\begin{align}
	\matr{T}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right) & \lrdmatr{0}{3\times 3}                 \\
		\lrdmatr{0}{3\times 3}                      & \lrdmatr{R}{a}\left(\phi,\theta\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where:
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta},\scal{\psi}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is the rotation matrix that maps linear velocities from the body-fixed frame to the world ENU frame.
	\item $\lrdmatr{R}{a}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta}\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}$ is the transformation that relates Euler angle rates to angular velocities in the body-fixed frame.
\end{itemize}

The rotation matrix \lrdmatr{R}{l} is obtained by composing three elementary rotations according to
the ZYX Euler angle convention (yaw-pitch-roll). In this convention, the orientation of the body
relative to the world frame is parameterised by:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \scal{\psi}: yaw, a rotation about the world \fvect{z}{W}-axis,
	\item \scal{\theta}: pitch, a rotation about the intermediate \fluvect{y}{W}{'}-axis (after the yaw rotation),
	\item \scal{\phi}: roll, a rotation about the body-fixed \fvect{x}{B}-axis (after the yaw and pitch rotations).
\end{itemize}

Each of these rotations is represented by a basic rotation matrix. \trigsh{}
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{x}}\left(\scal{\phi}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 0                 & 0                  \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}} & -\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\
		0 & \sin{\scal{\phi}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{y}}\left(\scal{\theta}\right) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\theta}}  & 0 & \sin{\scal{\theta}} \\
		0                    & 1 & 0                   \\
		-\sin{\scal{\theta}} & 0 & \cos{\scal{\theta}}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{z}}\left(\scal{\psi}\right) =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} & -\sin{\scal{\psi}} & 0 \\
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} & \cos{\scal{\psi}}  & 0 \\
		0                 & 0                  & 1
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

The composition yielded
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{R}{l}\left(\scal{\phi},\scal{\theta},\scal{\psi}\right) & =
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{z}}\left(\scal{\psi}\right)
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{y}}\left(\scal{\theta}\right)
	\lrdmatr{R}{\vect{x}}\left(\scal{\phi}\right)                        \\
	                                                                 & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\theta}}                                                         &
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}} - \sin{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} &
		\cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} + \sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}   \\
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\theta}}                                                         &
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}} + \cos{\scal{\psi}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} &
		\sin{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}} - \cos{\scal{\psi}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}   \\
		-\sin{\scal{\theta}}                                                                          &
		\cos{\scal{\theta}} \sin{\scal{\phi}}                                                         &
		\cos{\scal{\theta}} \cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\label{eq:rov_rotation_matrix_final}
\end{align}

The transformation \lrdmatr{R}{a} between body angular velocities and Euler angle rates was derived
systematically from the ZYX Euler parameterization. The instantaneous angular velocity vector is
expressed as a linear combination of the contributions from the Euler angle rates:
\begin{align}
	\vect{\omega} & = \dot{\phi}\fvect{x}{B} + \dot{\theta}\fluvect{y}{W}{'} + \dot{\psi}\fvect{z}{W}
	\label{rcc_rcm_srd_angle_composition}
\end{align}

Since the angular velocity must be expressed in the body-fixed frame, each axis is projected into
\lframe{B}:
\begin{align}
	\fvect{x}{B} & = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 0 \\ 0\end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\fvect{y}{B} = \lrmatr{R}{\top}{x}\left(\phi\right)\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} =
	\begin{bmatrix}0 \\ \cos{\phi} \\ \sin{\phi} \end{bmatrix} \hspace{.5cm}
	\fvect{z}{B} = {\left(\lrdmatr{R}{y}\left(\theta\right)\lrdmatr{R}{x}\left(\phi\right)\right)}^{\top}
	\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} =
	\begin{bmatrix}-\sin{\theta} \\ \cos{\theta}\sin{\phi} \\ \cos{\theta}\cos{\phi} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

Substituting these into \cref{rcc_rcm_srd_angle_composition} and expressing them in the body frame,
the following relation was obtained:
\begin{align}
	\fvect{\omega}{B} & =
	\dot{\phi}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
	+ \dot{\theta}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \cos{\scal{\phi}} \\ \sin{\scal{\phi}} \end{bmatrix}
	+ \dot{\psi}\begin{bmatrix} -\sin{\scal{\theta}} \\ \cos{\scal{\theta}}\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\ \cos{\scal{\theta}}\cos{\scal{\phi}} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

By collecting terms, the relation between angular velocities and Euler angle rates was expressed as
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{p} \\ \scal{q} \\ \scal{r}
	\end{bmatrix} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & 0                  & -\sin{\scal{\theta}}                 \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}}  & \cos{\scal{\theta}}\sin{\scal{\phi}} \\
		0 & -\sin{\scal{\phi}} & \cos{\scal{\theta}}\cos{\scal{\phi}}
	\end{bmatrix}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{\phi}} \\ \scal{\dot{\theta}} \\ \scal{\dot{\psi}}
	\end{bmatrix} =
	\lrmatr{R}{-1}{a}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\scal{\dot{\phi}} \\ \scal{\dot{\theta}} \\ \scal{\dot{\psi}}
	\end{bmatrix} \\
	\lrdmatr{R}{a}                   & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		1 & \sin{\scal{\phi}}\tan{\scal{\theta}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}\tan{\scal{\theta}} \\
		0 & \cos{\scal{\phi}}                    & -\sin{\scal{\phi}}                   \\
		0 & \sin{\scal{\phi}}\cos{\scal{\theta}} & \cos{\scal{\phi}}\cos{\scal{\theta}}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

This expression becomes singular at $\scal{\theta} = \pm{}\qty{90}{\degree}$ (gimbal lock).

\trigft{}

The hydrostatic restoring vector is defined as
\begin{align}
	\vect{s}\left(\vect{\eta}\right) & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\fldvect{f}{B}{g} + \fldvect{f}{B}{b} \\
		\fldvect{m}{B}{g} + \fldvect{m}{B}{b}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where the first three components represent the net forces due to gravity and buoyancy, and the last
three components represent the corresponding restoring moments.

In the world frame \lframe{W}, the gravitational and buoyancy forces are
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{f}{W}{g} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\ 0 \\ -\scal{m}\scal{g}
	\end{bmatrix} \hspace{1cm}
	\fldvect{f}{W}{b} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\ 0 \\ \ldscal{\rho}{w}\scal{g}\scal{v}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where \scal{m} is the vehicle mass, \scal{g} is the gravitational acceleration, \ldscal{\rho}{w} is
the water density, and \scal{v} is the submerged volume of the vehicle.

The rotation matrix \lrdmatr{R}{l} is used to express these forces in \lframe{B}:
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{f}{B}{g} & = \lrdmatr{R}{l}^{\top}(\phi,\theta,\psi) \fldvect{f}{W}{g} \\
	\fldvect{f}{B}{b} & = \lrdmatr{R}{l}^{\top}(\phi,\theta,\psi) \fldvect{f}{W}{b}
\end{align}

The corresponding restoring moments are obtained from the moment arms of the centre of gravity
$\ldvect{c}{g}=\left[\ldscal{c}{g,x}, \ldscal{c}{g,y}, \ldscal{c}{g,z}\right]^{\top}$ and the
centre of buoyancy $\ldvect{c}{b}=\left[\ldscal{c}{b,x}, \ldscal{c}{b,y},
		\ldscal{c}{b,z}\right]^{\top}$:
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{m}{g} & = \ldvect{r}{g} \times \left( \lrmatr{R}{\top}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right)\fldvect{f}{W}{g} \right) \\
	\ldvect{m}{b} & = \ldvect{r}{b} \times \left( \lrmatr{R}{\top}{l}\left(\phi,\theta,\psi\right)\fldvect{f}{W}{b} \right)
\end{align}

The generalized inertia matrix \matr{I} is defined to represent the combined rigid-body and
added-mass effects:
\begin{align}
	\matr{I} = \lrdmatr{M}{rb} + \lrdmatr{M}{a}.
\end{align}

The rigid-body inertia matrix \lrdmatr{M}{rb} is expressed as
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} & =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		m \lrdmatr{I}{3\times 3}             & -m \matr{S}\left(\ldvect{c}{g}\right) \\
		m \matr{S}\left(\ldvect{c}{g}\right) & \lrdmatr{I}{g}
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

where \scal{m} denotes the vehicle mass, $\ldvect{c}{g}=\left[\ldscal{c}{g,x}, \ldscal{c}{g,y},
		\ldscal{c}{g,z}\right]^{\top}$ is the position vector of the centre of gravity relative to the
body-fixed origin, $\matr{S}\left(\cdot\right)$ denotes the skew-symmetric matrix operator, and
\lrdmatr{I}{g} is the moment of inertia matrix about the centre of gravity. The off-diagonal blocks
capture the coupling between translational and rotational dynamics. Finally, the rigid-body inertia
matrix is expanded as
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} = \begin{bmatrix}
		                  \scal{m}                   & 0                          & 0                          & 0                          & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z}  & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y} \\
		                  0                          & \scal{m}                   & 0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z} & 0                          & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x}  \\
		                  0                          & 0                          & \scal{m}                   & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y}  & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x} & 0                          \\
		                  0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z} & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y}  & \ldscal{I}{xx}             & -\ldscal{I}{xy}            & -\ldscal{I}{xz}            \\
		                  \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, z}  & 0                          & -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x} & -\ldscal{I}{xy}            & \ldscal{I}{yy}             & -\ldscal{I}{yz}            \\
		                  -\scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, y} & \scal{m} \ldscal{c}{g, x}  & 0                          & -\ldscal{I}{xz}            & -\ldscal{I}{yz}            & \ldscal{I}{zz}
	                  \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

with \ldscal{I}{xx}, \ldscal{I}{yy}, \ldscal{I}{zz} denoting the moments of inertia about the
body-fixed axes, and \ldscal{I}{xy}, \ldscal{I}{xz}, \ldscal{I}{yz} denoting the products of
inertia.

The added-mass inertia matrix \lrdmatr{M}{a} is defined analogously as a diagonal matrix:
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{M}{a} & = \diag{\left(\ldscal{M}{a, u}, \ldscal{M}{a, v}, \ldscal{M}{a, w}, \ldscal{M}{a, p}, \ldscal{M}{a, q}, \ldscal{M}{a, r}\right)}
\end{align}

where the coefficients are usually determined experimentally.

The hydrodynamic damping matrix $\matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right)$ is typically partitioned into
linear and quadratic components:
\begin{align}
	\matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) & = \lrdmatr{D}{l} + \lrdmatr{D}{q}\left(\vect{\nu}\right).
\end{align}

These are in turn represented as diagonal matrices:
\begin{align}
	\lrdmatr{D}{l} & = \diag{\left( \ldscal{D}{l,u}, \ldscal{D}{l,v}, \ldscal{D}{l,w}, \ldscal{D}{l,p}, \ldscal{D}{l,q}, \ldscal{D}{l,r} \right)},                                          \\
	\lrdmatr{D}{q} & = \diag{\left( \ldscal{D}{q,u}\abs{u}, \ldscal{D}{q,v}\abs{v}, \ldscal{D}{q,w}\abs{w}, \ldscal{D}{q,p}\abs{p}, \ldscal{D}{q,q}\abs{q}, \ldscal{D}{q,r}\abs{r} \right)}
\end{align}

where the coefficients are also usually determined experimentally.

\subsection{Cable Modelling}
\label{rcc_rcm_cable_modelling}

The cable model used is the catenary model discussed in \cref{cable_modelling}, more specifically
the \verticalmodel{} (\cref{rcc_rcm_cm_d_catenary_curve}).This model is preferred over the
\augmentedmodel{} due to the fact that no satisfactory dynamics was available for its augmentation
parameters at the time of this work.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/vertical_catenary_schematic}
	\caption{
		Catenary curve of length \catL{} defined in the plane \lplane{v}. The construction parameters are
		the sag \catH{}, the elevation difference \catdH{}, and the horizontal span $l = 2\catD + \catdD$
		between attachment points \fpoint{A}{v} and \fpoint{B}{v}. Note that \catdD{} and \catdH{} are
		signed distances.
	}
	\label{rcc_rcm_cm_d_catenary_curve}
\end{figure}

The coordinates of any point along the curve are defined in
\cref{cm_catenary_model,cm_cm_d_catenary_equation} as $\fpoint{P}{v} =
	\left(\coord{x}{v},~0,~\coord{z}{v}\right) \in \lframe{v}$, bound by the attachment points
$\fpoint{A}{v}=\left(-\catD-\catdD,~0,~\catH+\catdH\right)$ and
$\fpoint{B}{v}=\left(\catD,~0,~\catH\right)$. The \coord{z}{v} coordinate of each point is given by
the standard catenary equation:
\begin{align}
	\coord{z}{v} = \frac{\cosh{(\coord{x}{v}~\catC)} - 1}{\catC}, \quad \coord{x}{v} \in \left[-\catD-\catdD,~\catD\right]
\end{align}

Here, $\catC \in \mathbb{R}^*_+$ is the catenary parameter and governs the cable's curvature.

In the context of a chain of \acp{rov}, the mechanical effects of the cable on the robot are of
interest. To this end the horizontal tension force is by definition
(\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_change_of_variable})
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{h} = \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}
\end{align}

Furthermore, the vertical tension force is defined (\cref{cm_cm_dotcm_tension_equation}) as
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{v}\left(x\right) = \ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}\ldscal{L}{1}
	\label{rcc_rcm_cm_vertical_tension}
\end{align}

for a general point \ldpoint{p}{1} along the cable where \ldscal{L}{1} is the arc-length of the
cable between the origin \ldpoint{o}{v} and \ldpoint{p}{1}. In
\cref{cm_catenary_model,cm_cm_dotcm_horizontal_catenary_coordinate} \scal{x} is written as a
function of \ldscal{L}{1}
\begin{align}
	x = \frac{\sinh^{-1}\left(L_1 \catC\right)}{\catC}
\end{align}

Which is then substituted in \cref{rcc_rcm_cm_vertical_tension} to yield
\begin{align}
	\ldscal{T}{v}\left(x\right) & = \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(\catC x\right)} \\
	                            & = \ldscal{T}{h}\sinh{\left(\catC x\right)}
\end{align}

The forces exerted by the cable on the vehicles are then
\begin{align}
	\fldvect{\omega}{v}{i} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\\ 0 \\ \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(-\catC\left(\catD+\catdD\right)\right)} \end{bmatrix} \hspace{1cm}
	\fldvect{\omega}{v}{i+1} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\\ 0 \\ \frac{\ldscal{\lambda}{m}\scal{g}}{\catC{}}\sinh{\left(\catC\catD\right)} \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

with \fldvect{\omega}{v}{i} the external perturbation force applied to the robot attached to
\point{a} and \fldvect{\omega}{v}{i+1} the force applied to the robot attached to \point{b}. It
should be noted that these forces are expressed in the catenary frame \lframe{v} and must be
transformed to the body-fixed frame \lframe{B} of each vehicle before being used in
\cref{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple}.

with \fldvect{\omega}{v}{i} the external perturbation force applied to the robot attached to
\point{a} and \fldvect{\omega}{v}{i+1} the force applied to the robot attached to \point{b}. It
should be noted that these forces are expressed in the catenary frame \lframe{v} and must be
transformed to the body-fixed frame \lframe{B} of each vehicle before being used in
\cref{rcc_rcm_srd_dynamics_simple}.

\subsection{Robot Chain Dynamics}
\label{rcc_rcm_robot_chain_dynamics}

The robot chain system (\cref{rcc_rcm_rcd_schematic}) is defined as a set of \scal{n} underwater
robots connected by tethers, where each robot follows the dynamics described in
\cref{rcc_rcm_single_robot_dynamics}. The state of the system is a concatenation of the state of
the robots
\begin{align}
	\vect{x} = \left[\ldvect{\eta}{1}, \ldvect{\nu}{1}, \dots, \ldvect{\eta}{n}, \ldvect{\nu}{n}\right]^{\top}
	\label{rcc_rcm_rcd_state_vector}
\end{align}

Similarly, its input is a concatenation of all actuations
\begin{align}
	\vect{u} = \left[\ldvect{\tau}{1}, \dots, \ldvect{\tau}{n}\right]^{\top}
	\label{rcc_rcm_rcd_actuation_vector}
\end{align}

Additionally, the perturbation vector for each robot is define as the perturbation forces derived
from the cable model described in \cref{rcc_rcm_cable_modelling}. The robots at the ends of the
chain only experience the perturbation forces from a single cable while the other are subjected to
the forces of two cables.

\section{Controllers}
\label{rcc_controllers}

This section presents the control architectures that were developed to coordinate a chain of
tethered \acp{rov} such that the lead vehicle tracked its assigned motion objective while the group
respected the mechanical and environmental constraints introduced in
\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}. The controllers were required to regulate the coupled dynamics
under disturbances, enforce actuator limits, maintain safe inter-vehicle spacing and seafloor
clearance, and keep tether tensions and geometry within the feasible catenary envelope. Several
controllers were evaluated: an \ac{mpc} that formulated the coupled problem with explicit
constraints (\cref{rcc_c_model_predictive_control}), a path-planning layer that provided feasible
waypoints and references consistent with tether kinematics (\cref{rcc_c_path_planning}), and
lightweight reactive behaviors that handled fast safety overrides and unmodeled events
(\cref{rcc_c_reactive_control}).

\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{mpc}{long}}
\label{rcc_c_model_predictive_control}

\Ac{mpc} is a control strategy that explicitly incorporates the dynamics of
the system, prediction of future states, and operational constraints into a single optimization
problem. The central idea is that, at each time step, the system evolution is predicted forward
over a finite horizon, based on the current state and a sequence of future control inputs. An
optimization problem is then solved to select the sequence of inputs that minimizes a prescribed
cost while satisfying all imposed constraints. Only the first input of this optimal sequence is
applied, and the procedure is repeated at the next step with updated state information. This
mechanism allows the controller to continuously adapt to disturbances and modeling errors while
respecting safety and feasibility conditions (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_schematic}).

	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/model_predictive_control_schematic}
	\caption{Schematic of the model predictive control algorithm. The diagram illustrates the state
		and actuation across past, present, and future instants, and shows multiple candidate trajectories
		for $\vect{x}$ and $\vect{u}$ over the prediction horizon, of which the optimal one is selected.}
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_schematic}
\end{figure}

Let \cref{mcc_c_mpc_system} define a discrete-time control system with state \ldvect{x}{k} and
control input \ldvect{u}{k}, both expressed at time \scal{k}:
\begin{equation}
	\ldvect{x}{k+1} = f\left(\ldvect{x}{k}, \ldvect{u}{k}\right).
	\label{mcc_c_mpc_system}
\end{equation}

This system is obtained by integrating the robot chain dynamics described in
\cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}.

The \ac{mpc} problem then consists of finding a sequence of control inputs
$\vect{u}=\left(\ldvect{u}{0}, \dots, \ldvect{u}{h}\right)$ over the horizon \mpch{} that minimizes
the cost
\begin{align}
	\vect{u} = & \argmin{\lures{h}{\top} \matr{P} \res{h}
	+ \frac{1}{\mpch{}}\sum_{k=0}^{\mpch{}-1}{\lures{k}{\top} \matr{Q} \res{k}
	+ \Delta \llvect{u}{\top}{k} \matr{R} \Delta \ldvect{u}{k}}},
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_equation}
\end{align}

where the first term penalizes the final deviation from the target state, the second term enforces
good tracking of the reference along the horizon, and the third term limits unnecessary variations
in the control input.

The optimization is solved subject to dynamic feasibility and a set of inequality constraints:
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldvect{u}{\text{min}}           \leq & ~\ldvect{u}{k}                                                           & ~\leq & ~\ldvect{u}{\text{max}}                \\
	\Delta   \ldvect{u}{\text{min}}  \leq & ~\Delta\ldvect{u}{k}                                                     & ~\leq & ~\Delta\ldvect{u}{\text{max}} \notag   \\
	\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{i} - \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}                            & ~     & ~\notag                                \\
	                                      & ~\max{\left(\ldcoord{z}{w}{i}, \ldcoord{z}{w}{i+1}\right)}               & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} \notag \\
	                                      & ~\sqrt{\llscal{\catl{}}{2}{i(i+1)} + \Delta \llscal{\catH{}}{2}{i(i+1)}} & ~\leq & ~\ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} \notag        \\
	\ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}          \leq & ~\ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)}                                                & ~     & \notag                                 \\
	                                      & ~\norm{\ldvect{\nu}{i}}                                                  & ~\leq & ~\ldvect{\nu}{\text{max}} \notag
	\label{rcc_c_mpc_constraints}
\end{alignat}

where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\res{k} = \ldvect{x}{k} - \llvect{x}{\text{target}}{k}$ is the state deviation from the target,
	\item $\Delta \ldvect{u}{k} = \ldvect{u}{k} - \ldvect{u}{k-1}$ is the incremental control input,
	\item \matr{P}, \matr{Q} and \matr{R} are positive semi-definite matrices weighting the final, state, and control costs, respectively,
	\item \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, \ldscal{\catdH{}}{i(i+1)} and \ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)} are parameters of the catenary model representing the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
	\item $\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$, $\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{min}}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{u}_{\text{max}}$ are the bounds of the control input,
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}} is the known elevation of the seafloor which neither the robot nor the cable should cross,
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} is the known elevation of the water surface which the robot cannot physically cross,
	\item \ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} is the maximum allowable distance between two consecutive robots in the chain based on the tether length,
	\item \ldscal{dh}{\text{min}} is the minimum allowable distance between consecutive robots, both to ensure separation and to avoid a singularity in the catenary model when \catl{} is zero.
\end{itemize}

If the robot is operated in a constrained environment such as a pool, additional box constraints
could be imposed on the position:
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldcoord{x}{w}{\text{pool}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{x}{w}{i} & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{x}{w}{\text{pool}}        \\
	\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{pool}}  \leq & ~\ldcoord{y}{w}{i} & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{y}{w}{\text{pool}} \notag
\end{alignat}

Once the optimal control input is found, the first element of the sequence is applied to the
system, and the optimization problem is solved again at the next time step. In order to reduce the
optimization time, \vect{u} may be considered constant, piecewise constant, or parameterized as a
polynomial function over the horizon.

\subsection{Path Planning}
\label{rcc_c_path_planning}

A path planning problem was formulated to generate feasible trajectories for the robots while
respecting the tether kinematics and the constraints of the chain. The resulting trajectory is then
used with a simple controller (\ac{pid}) to move the system. The path planning optimization problem
consists of finding a sequence of waypoints $\vect{p}=\left(\ldvect{x}{0}, \dots,
	\ldvect{x}{n}\right)$ over a finite horizon \mpch{} in order to minimize the distance of the path
to the desired pose of the robots while enforcing kinematic feasibility constraints
(\cref{rcc_c_pp_constraints}):
\begin{align}
	\vect{p} = & \argmin{\lures{h}{\top} \matr{P} \res{h}
	+ \frac{1}{\mpch{}}\sum_{k=0}^{\mpch{}-1}{\lures{k}{\top} \matr{Q} \res{k}}}
	\label{rcc_c_pp_cost}
\end{align}

subject to
\begin{alignat}{3}
	\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}} \leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{i} - \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}                            & ~     & \label{rcc_c_pp_constraints}           \\
	\leq                                 & ~\max{\left(\ldcoord{z}{w}{i}, \ldcoord{z}{w}{i+1}\right)}               & ~\leq & ~\ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{surface}} \notag \\
	                                     & ~\sqrt{\llscal{\catl{}}{2}{i(i+1)} + \Delta \llscal{\catH{}}{2}{i(i+1)}} & ~\leq & ~\ldscal{dp}{\text{max}} \notag        \\
	\ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}         \leq & ~\ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)}                                                & ~\leq & ~\ldscal{dh}{\text{max}} \notag        \\
	                                     & ~\norm{\ldvect{p}{i+1}-\ldvect{p}{i}}                                    & ~\leq & ~\ldvect{d}{\text{max}} \notag
\end{alignat}

where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $\res{k} = \ldvect{x}{k} - \llvect{x}{\text{target}}{k}$ denotes the state deviation from the target,
	\item \matr{P} and \matr{Q} are positive semi-definite matrices weighting the final and state costs, respectively,
	\item \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, \ldscal{\catdH{}}{i(i+1)}, and \ldscal{\catl{}}{i(i+1)} are the catenary model parameters representing the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
	\item $\mathbf{p}_{\text{min}}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\text{max}}$ are bounds on feasible waypoints,
	\item $\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\text{min}}$ and $\Delta\mathbf{r}_{\text{max}}$ are bounds on the increments between path parameters,
	\item \ldcoord{z}{w}{\text{seafloor}}, \ldscal{dp}{\text{max}}, \ldscal{dh}{\text{min}}, and \ldscal{dh}{\text{max}} are geometrical bounds ensuring safe separation, catenary feasibility, and seafloor clearance.
\end{itemize}

Once the optimal waypoint sequence was found, it is passed to the controller as a reference, and
the optimization problem was solved again at the next planning step.

\subsection{Reactive Control}
\label{rcc_c_reactive_control}

In addition to the path planning scheme, a reactive control strategy was implemented to regulate
the tether geometry on a peer-to-peer basis. This method is based on the catenary-based control law
proposed in \cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2017}, originally formulated as a visual
servoing algorithm. In their work, the tether is modeled as a deformable catenary, and the control
objective is to regulate its shape parameters by acting on the motion of the follower robot with
respect to the leader. The visual servoing loop estimates the tether features directly from the
image plane and closes the loop by minimizing the feature error through the catenary interaction
matrix.

In our framework, we replace the visual estimation step with direct pose localization of the
robots. The relative positions and orientations of neighboring robots in the chain are measured,
allowing the catenary parameters between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1} to be explicitly estimated.
The reactive controller then commands the catenary sag parameter \ldscal{\catH{}}{i(i+1)}, which
represents the vertical deflection of the tether, as the primary regulation variable. The control
law is expressed as
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{v}{i+1} = - \lambda \lrumatr{L}{\dagger} \left(
	\vect{s} - \luvect{s}{\text{target}}
	\right)
	\label{rcc_c_rc_law}
\end{align}

where \ldvect{v}{i+1} is the twist of robot \scal{i+1} relative to robot \scal{i}, \matr{L} is the
interaction matrix relating the tether shape derivatives to the relative motion of the robots,
$\scal{\lambda} \in \mathbb{R}^{+}$ is a control gain, and \ldvect{s}{i(i+1)} is feature vector
such that
\begin{align}
	\ldvect{s}{i(i+1)} = \left[\frac{\ldscal{H}{i(i+1)}}{\ldscal{H}{\text{max}}}, \sin{\scal{\alpha}}\right]
\end{align}

with \ldscal{H}{i(i+1)} being the sag of the tether between robots \scal{i} and \scal{i+1},
\ldscal{H}{\text{max}} the maximum sag allowed, and \scal{\alpha} is the angle between robot
\scal{i+1}'s \vect{x} axis projected in the horizontal plane and the vector going from robot
\scal{i+1} to robot{i} also projected in the horizontal plane.

This peer-to-peer formulation enables each robot to locally and concurrently regulate their tether
with the preceding robot all the way up to the leader robot which performs a task, ensuring that
the global chain maintains feasible catenary geometries without over-constraining the system.

\subsubsection{Simulation Setup}
\label{rcc_s_mpc_simulation_setup}

The controller is assessed through the analysis of a scenario comprising four tethered robots
($\scal{n}=4$ in \cref{rcc_rcm_rcd_state_vector,rcc_rcm_rcd_actuation_vector}). The experiment
takes place in a numerical tank with varying depth, and infinite width and length
(\cref{rcc_s_mpc_ss_tank}). The tethers are assumed to be negatively buoyant with a known fixed
length as discussed in \cref{cable_modelling}. The leader robot as well as the two first follower
robots are considered as \acp{rov} while the fourth robot is modelled as an \ac{usv} in order to
represent the link to a surface control vehicle.

The leader robot is tasked with tracking a reference trajectory, while the two follower robots must
ensure that the constraints are not violated. It is imperative that they maintain a safe distance
from one another since proximity could result in contact with the floor or other obstacles. To
prevent the follower robots from spinning, their orientation is controlled to remain aligned with
that of the leader (\cref{rcc_s_mpc_i_constrains_values}).

To test the system's ability to cope with these constraints, we considered a trajectory in which
the leading robot passes over a raised peak in which the tether would be entangled without proper
constraints on the tether shape. This allowed us to check that, even in this critical case, the
following robots were able to position themselves so that the cables did not touch the seafloor.
The leader reference trajectory is a sine of amplitude \qty{3.}{\m} along the \ldvect{z}{w}-axis
centered on a depth of \qty{2.5}{\m} and a smooth curve from \qty{2}{\m} to \qty{-3}{\m} and back
on the \ldvect{x}{w}-axis with speed equal to zero at both ends (\cref{rcc_s_mpc_ss_tank}). The
reference on the \ldvect{y}{w}-axis as well as the orientation is constant and equal to zero. The
leader robot initial state is $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{0}=[2,0,1, 0, 0, 0]$ and the two follower
robots'states are $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{1}=[2.5,0,1, 0, 0, 0]$ and $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{2}=[3,0,1, 0, 0,
	0]$. The \ac{usv} is positioned at $\fldvect{\eta}{w}{3}=[3.5,0,0,0,0,\pi/2]$.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{rcc_s_mpc_implementation}

The simulation (\citeurl{filliungPympc_usage2025}) integrates the robots chain dynamics introduced
in \cref{rcc_robot_chain_modelling}. The simulation is run with a time step of \qty{0.1}{\second}
for \num{500} steps, i.e. \qty{20}{\second}. The MPC horizon is \num{5} steps, i.e.
\qty{.5}{\second}. The problem is solved with the \texttt{scipy.minimize} function in Python with a
tolerance of \num{1e-6} and a maximum number of iterations set to \num{100}.

The matrices \matr{P} and \matr{Q} (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_equation}) are defined in such a way that the
position of the leader is assigned a weight of \num{50} and its orientation a weight of \num{1}.
The orientation of the other robots is also assigned a weight of \num{1}, while their position is
not weighted, leaving them free on these axes. Matrix \matr{R} (\cref{rcc_c_mpc_equation}) has a
weight of zero on linear actuation and a weight of \num{1} on angular actuation with the exception
of the orientation of the \ac{usv}.

Additionally, the constraints defined in \cref{rcc_c_mpc_constraints} are set and listed in
\cref{rcc_s_mpc_i_constrains_values}.

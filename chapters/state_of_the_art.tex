\section{Introduction}

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art concerning the
control of chains of underwater robots interconnected via tethers, operating within known but
constrained environments. Two primary scientific challenges are addressed throughout this thesis:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The safe and reliable control of underwater robots navigating through constrained environments;
	\item The incorporation of tether dynamics—specifically, their shape and the forces they exert—into the
	      overall control framework.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, this chapter begins by reviewing developments in unmanned underwater robotics in
\cref{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}, emphasizing the various types of \acp{uuv}. Research
related to cables used in underwater robotics is then examined
(\cref{sota_cables_in_underwater_robotics}), with a focus on cable shape estimation and management
techniques. Finally, control strategies for underwater robots are assessed in
\cref{sota_robot_control}, with particular attention given to tethered configurations and their
specific control challenges.

\section{\Glsentrylongpl{uuv}}
\label{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}

Unmanned underwater robots are essential for a broad range of applications, including marine
research, environmental monitoring, and underwater exploration. These robotic platforms are
generally categorized into two primary classes according to their operational modes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Utilizing tethers, \acp{rov} maintain a physical link with a surface vessel, thereby enabling
	      high-bandwidth, real-time communication and control, teleoperation, and continuous power supply;
	\item Operating independently, \acp{auv} rely on preprogrammed missions, limited communications, and
	      onboard sensors for navigation and data acquisition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{rov}}
\label{sota_uuv_remotely_operated_vehicles}

Remotely operated vehicles \acp{rov} are widely deployed in marine science and industrial
operations. These systems are typically equipped with cameras, environmental sensors, and robotic
manipulators, facilitating tasks such as underwater inspection, maintenance, and deep-sea
exploration. \Acp{rov} are highly maneuverable and are capable of operating at substantial depths,
thus allowing them to perform missions deemed hazardous or impractical for human divers. Their
operations are continuously supervised and controlled by human operators from the surface, which
limits their range to the tether length.

To ensure reliable high-bandwidth communication, \acp{rov} are connected to surface vessels via
tethers. This configuration is necessitated by the high absorption of electromagnetic waves in
water, which precludes conventional wireless communication over distances exceeding a few meters.

	\inputtikz{figures/water_absorption_spectrum}
	\caption{

		vapor (\cite{rothmanHITRAN2004Molecular2005}), the dominant contributor to atmospheric absorption.

As illustrated in \cref{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum}, the absorption coefficient of water
decreases with increasing wavelength. Thus, \ac{elf} electromagnetic waves (in the
\qtyrange{3}{300}{\hertz} range) can propagate farther underwater. However, as discussed
in~\cite{altgeltWorldsLargestRadiostation2005}, such frequencies would require impractically large
antennas --on the order of kilometers-- and high transmission power, rendering them unsuitable for
use in mobile underwater platforms.

According to~\cite{christROVManualUser2013}, \acp{rov} are classified by size and functionality
into three main categories:

\begin{itemize}	\item Work-class \acp{rov} (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_victor_6000}) exceed \qty{1000}{\kilo\gram} and are
	      designed for complex tasks such as subsea construction, maintenance, and repair. These systems are
	      typically equipped with hydraulic-powered thrusters and manipulators, in addition to a
	      comprehensive suite of sensors. Work-class \acp{rov} can operate at depths greater than
	      \qty{3000}{\meter} and are launched from specialized surface support vessels
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}). Their tethers are designed to deliver power to the
	      robot over great distances and require substantial cable management infrastructure aboard the host
	      vessel due to their weight and length.
\end{itemize}

	      \acp{rov}.
\end{itemize}

	      \ac{rov} relies on an internal battery for power.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{auv}}
\label{sota_uuv_autonomous_underwater_vehicles}

An \ac{auv} is an untethered robotic platform capable of operating without real-time communication
(\cref{sota_uuv_auv_asterix}). Although data exchange is possible via acoustic channels, it remains
highly constrained in range and bandwidth (\cite{creuzeRobotsMarinsSousmarins2014}). \Ac{auv} are
typically programmed prior to deployment with mission objectives and rely on onboard sensors,
control algorithms, and occasional communication upon surfacing to accomplish tasks such as
bathymetric mapping, search-and-rescue operations, environmental monitoring, sample collection, and
seabed surveys (\cite{bechlioulisRobustFormationControl2019, merciSimulatorUnderwaterGlider2023}).

The physical configuration of an \ac{auv} is determined by its operational requirements. However,
most are torpedo-shaped \acp{auv} that are optimized for long-range and extended-duration missions.
Their streamlined form minimizes hydrodynamic drag, enabling efficient travel over large distances.
Propulsion may be achieved using conventional thrusters, or, in the case of gliders, through a
buoyancy-driven system. The latter allows vertical movement by adjusting overall density and
internal mass distribution. Horizontal motion is then generated by hydrofoils that convert vertical
displacement into forward glide (\cite{meyerGliderTechnologyOcean2016}).

\subsection{Other vehicles}
\label{sota_uuv_other_vehicles}

Several underwater vehicle designs exist beyond conventional \ac{rov} or \ac{auv} architectures.
These platforms are intended to improve maneuverability, energy efficiency, or mission
adaptability. Notable examples include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Biomimetic vehicles, which are inspired by aquatic organisms and aim to replicate their propulsion
	      methods using flexible fins, undulating body motions, or flapping appendages. These systems
	      generate minimal hydrodynamic disturbance, thereby enhancing stealth and environmental
	      compatibility. Examples include the RoboTuna, presented
	      in~\cite{triantafyllouEfficientSwimmingMachine1995}. The humanoid Ocean One K
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_ocean_one_k}) also exhibits biomimetic traits through its human-like structure
	      and dexterous manipulation capabilities.

	\item Reconfigurable vehicles, which may be adaptable in multiple ways. Some systems allow for thruster
	      repositioning to optimize hydrodynamic performance and energy usage depending on specific mission
	      requirements~\cite{fagundesgasparotoAdvancesReconfigurableVectorial2021}. Others, such as Hybrid
	      \acp{rov}, are capable of operating either in autonomous mode or under remote control. These
	      vehicles generally rely on onboard batteries and a lightweight fiber-optic tether, in contrast to
	      standard work-class \acp{rov}. The Ariane vehicle, developed by \ac{ifremer}, exemplifies this
	      class with its ability to switch to autonomous operation when
	      required~\cite{raugelOperationalScientificCapabilities2019}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Robot control}
\label{sota_robot_control}

This section reviews control strategies developed for unmanned robotic systems operating with
tether constraints. Emphasis is placed on methods that account for dynamic coupling with the
environment, constraints imposed by tethers, and the challenges of limited sensing and delayed
feedback. The discussion is structured according to the robot’s configuration: tethered,
untethered, and tethered underwater systems.

\subsection{Tethered robots}
\label{sota_rc_tethered}

Control of tethered robots is confronted with distinct challenges owing to the intricate
interactions among the vehicle, its tether, and the surrounding environment. The tether introduces
additional dynamic elements that are incorporated into the control architecture, including tether
tension, hydrodynamic or aerodynamic drag, oscillatory modes, and risks of entanglement or
collisions with obstacles.

Although the current work is focused on underwater robots, analogous control challenges are
observed in other domains such as aerial robotics, wherein tethers may also be utilized not only
for power delivery, data transmission, or mechanical stabilization but also as active elements in
cooperative manipulation tasks. For instance, in~\cite{dantonioCatenaryRobotDesign2021}, a catenary
robot --consisting of two \acp{uav} connected by a flexible cable-- was designed to autonomously
locate and transport hook-shaped objects. In that system, the cable's position, orientation, and
span were actively regulated in real time to execute manipulation tasks without prior physical
attachment. These observations illustrate that tether dynamics are harnessed to provide
functionality rather than merely being compensated for.

Recent advances in tethered aerial systems have demonstrated sophisticated control architectures
for multi-vehicle configurations. Systems of tethered multicopters featuring chains of drones
connected by elastic tethers have been developed, where tether lengths can be dynamically adjusted
through controlled winches~\cite{fagianoSystemsTetheredMulticopters2017}. These systems employ
hierarchical control approaches that account for the elastic properties of tethers and their
ability to transfer only traction loads, providing both extended runtime and operational
flexibility for various applications.

The modeling and control of tethered drone systems has evolved to include novel mechanical
configurations such as pulley-gimbal mechanisms that allow drones to move freely along tethers
while maintaining precise position measurement~\cite{kosarnovskyStringTetheredDrones2019}. These
systems can be conceptualized as robotic arms where each tether section acts as a variable-length
link and each drone serves as a joint actuator, enabling complex coordinated motions through
combined geometric and linear control approaches.

For multi-tethered aerial systems, specialized state estimation techniques have been developed to
minimize sensor requirements while maintaining full observability. Nonlinear observers based on
high-gain and Luenberger observer designs can successfully estimate the complete system state from
minimal sensor configurations, such as two accelerometers and encoders or
inclinometers~\cite{tognonNonlinearObserverControl2015}. These observers enable precise control of
both Cartesian position and internal tether forces in bi-tethered multi-aerial robot systems.

Fundamental properties of tethered aerial systems have been thoroughly characterized, revealing
differential flatness with respect to specific output pairs: the elevation of the link and attitude
of the vehicle, or the elevation of the link and longitudinal link
force~\cite{tognonDynamicsControlEstimation2017}. This characterization enables the design of
almost globally convergent nonlinear controllers and observers that can track smooth time-varying
trajectories using only onboard accelerometers and gyroscopes, demonstrating robustness against
nonlinear cross-coupling effects, parameter deviations, and measurement noise.

In the field of space robotics, tether systems have been investigated for their potential in
momentum exchange, orbital transfer, and energy harvesting. Electrodynamic tethers, for instance,
have been proposed as a means of deorbiting satellites or generating artificial gravity. These
systems introduce complex control requirements related to tether deployment, tension regulation,
and oscillation suppression in a microgravity environment. The deployment and retrieval of long
tethers also entail the challenge of stabilizing multi-body dynamics in the absence of aerodynamic
damping~\cite{modiDeploymentDynamicsTether1979,huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}.

Advanced control strategies for tethered space systems address critical challenges such as tether
tangling around uncooperative tumbling targets. Energy-based control approaches utilizing the
system's passivity properties have been developed, where potential energy is constructed in terms
of tension torque to steer target angular velocity toward the tether
direction~\cite{wangAntitangleControlTethered2019}. These methods employ sliding mode controllers
optimized through model predictive control to satisfy motion constraints while incorporating
adaptive weighting mechanisms for enhanced tangle-avoidance capabilities.

Dexterous tethered space robot systems have demonstrated feasibility for geostationary orbit space
debris removal missions, combining lightweight design with cost-effective operation. These systems
integrate vision-based pose measurement with coordinated control design to achieve precise
manipulation of uncooperative targets in space environments~\cite{huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}.
The coordination between the tethered robot and its base platform requires sophisticated control
algorithms that account for the coupling effects introduced by the tether connection while
maintaining mission objectives.

\subsection{Underwater robots}

Control of underwater robots requires sophisticated algorithms that can handle the complex,
nonlinear dynamics of underwater systems while managing uncertainties and disturbances. The
underwater environment presents unique control challenges including hydrodynamic effects, buoyancy
variations, ocean currents, and limited sensing capabilities.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) has emerged as a particularly effective approach for underwater
robot control due to its ability to systematically handle constraints while optimizing performance.
\cite{Wei_2023} provides a comprehensive review of MPC-based motion planning and control for
autonomous marine vehicles, highlighting how recent advances in communication technologies,
perception capabilities, and optimization algorithms have enhanced the guidance and control
capabilities of underwater systems.

\cite{Yan_2020} demonstrates the application of model predictive control for trajectory tracking of autonomous underwater vehicles in the presence of external disturbances, showing the effectiveness of this approach for handling environmental uncertainties.

For underactuated underwater robots, specialized control approaches have been developed.
\cite{Guo_2022} presents a neural network-based nonsingular terminal sliding mode controller with
prescribed performance for target tracking. This approach uses radial basis function neural
networks combined with terminal sliding mode control to handle model uncertainties and external
disturbances, achieving finite-time convergence with prescribed performance bounds.

Advanced control strategies have also been developed for 3D path following scenarios.
\cite{Vadapalli_2023} presents a robust state feedback optimal control strategy using a
backstepping approach that handles hydrodynamic parameter variations through a polytopic approach.
The control strategy integrates both horizontal and vertical dynamics to achieve 3D path following
while maintaining robust behavior under uncertain hydrodynamic parameters.

Benchmark simulation environments have been developed to facilitate control algorithm development
and validation. \cite{Benzon_2022} presents an open-source benchmark simulator for the BlueROV2
underwater robot, implemented in Simulink and experimentally validated. This simulator includes
comprehensive models of vehicle kinematics, hydrodynamics, thruster dynamics, and environmental
effects, providing a standardized platform for evaluating control algorithms.

\subsection{Underwater tethered robots}

The control of underwater tethered robots combines the challenges of underwater robotics with the
additional complexity introduced by tether dynamics. These systems must account for cable forces,
tether-induced disturbances, and the coupling between robot motion and cable configuration.

Recent research has explored various approaches to address these challenges. \cite{Su_2024}
presents SPH-DEM modeling techniques for cable-controlled ROVs, focusing on underwater mobility and
path planning. This work demonstrates the importance of considering cable dynamics in the planning
and control of tethered underwater vehicles.

For multi-robot scenarios, coordination becomes even more complex when tethers are involved.
\cite{Tymoshyn_2023} addresses the intelligent control of groups of transport underwater robots
using a coordinator robot, which provides insights into managing multiple tethered vehicles
operating in uncertain environments.

The development of cooperative control strategies for underwater robotic systems has also advanced
significantly. \cite{Cai_2023} explores cooperative artificial intelligence approaches for
underwater robotic swarms, which can inform the development of control strategies for chains of
tethered vehicles where coordination between multiple robots is essential.

Navigation in human-populated environments presents additional challenges for autonomous systems.
\cite{Fiasché_2023} develops a universal social force model combined with parametrized MPC for
autonomous robot navigation, which could be adapted for underwater tethered robots operating in
environments with multiple agents or obstacles.

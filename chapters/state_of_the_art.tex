\chapter{State of the art}
\label{state_of_the_art}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art concerning the
control of chains of underwater robots interconnected via tethers, operating within known but
constrained environments. Two primary scientific challenges are addressed throughout this thesis:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The safe and reliable control of underwater robots navigating through constrained environments;
	\item The incorporation of tether dynamics—specifically, their shape and the forces they exert—into the
	      overall control framework.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, this chapter begins by reviewing developments in unmanned underwater robotics in
\cref{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}, emphasizing the various types of \acp{uuv}. Research
related to cables used in underwater robotics is then examined
(\cref{sota_cables_in_underwater_robotics}), with a focus on cable shape estimation and management
techniques. Finally, control strategies for underwater robots are assessed in
\cref{sota_robot_control}, with particular attention given to tethered configurations and their
specific control challenges.

\section{\Glsentrylongpl{uuv}}
\label{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}

Unmanned underwater robots are essential for a broad range of applications, including marine
research, environmental monitoring, and underwater exploration. These robotic platforms are
generally categorized into two primary classes according to their operational modes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Utilizing tethers, \acp{rov} maintain a physical link with a surface vessel, thereby enabling
	      high-bandwidth, real-time communication and control, teleoperation, and continuous power supply;
	\item Operating independently, \acp{auv} rely on preprogrammed missions, limited communications, and
	      onboard sensors for navigation and data acquisition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{rov}}
\label{sota_uuv_remotely_operated_vehicles}

Remotely operated vehicles \acp{rov} are widely deployed in marine science and industrial
operations. These systems are typically equipped with cameras, environmental sensors, and robotic
manipulators, facilitating tasks such as underwater inspection, maintenance, and deep-sea
exploration. \Acp{rov} are highly maneuverable and are capable of operating at substantial depths,
thus allowing them to perform missions deemed hazardous or impractical for human divers. Their
operations are continuously supervised and controlled by human operators from the surface, which
limits their range to the tether length.

To ensure reliable high-bandwidth communication, \acp{rov} are connected to surface vessels via
tethers. This configuration is necessitated by the high absorption of electromagnetic waves in
water, which precludes conventional wireless communication over distances exceeding a few meters.

As illustrated in \cref{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum}, the absorption coefficient of water
decreases with increasing wavelength. Thus, \ac{elf} electromagnetic waves (in the
\qtyrange{3}{300}{\hertz} range) can propagate farther underwater. However, as discussed
in~\cite{altgeltWorldsLargestRadiostation2005}, such frequencies would require impractically large
antennas --on the order of kilometers-- and high transmission power, rendering them unsuitable for
use in mobile underwater platforms.

According to~\cite{christROVManualUser2013}, \acp{rov} are classified by size and functionality
into three main categories:

\begin{itemize}	\item Work-class \acp{rov} (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_victor_6000}) exceed \qty{1000}{\kilo\gram} and are
	      designed for complex tasks such as subsea construction, maintenance, and repair. These systems are
	      typically equipped with hydraulic-powered thrusters and manipulators, in addition to a
	      comprehensive suite of sensors. Work-class \acp{rov} can operate at depths greater than
	      \qty{3000}{\meter} and are launched from specialized surface support vessels
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}). Their tethers are designed to deliver power to the
	      robot over great distances and require substantial cable management infrastructure aboard the host
	      vessel due to their weight and length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{auv}}
\label{sota_uuv_autonomous_underwater_vehicles}

An \ac{auv} is an untethered robotic platform capable of operating without real-time communication
(\cref{sota_uuv_auv_asterix}). Although data exchange is possible via acoustic channels, it remains
highly constrained in range and bandwidth (\cite{creuzeRobotsMarinsSousmarins2014}). \Ac{auv} are
typically programmed prior to deployment with mission objectives and rely on onboard sensors,
control algorithms, and occasional communication upon surfacing to accomplish tasks such as
bathymetric mapping, search-and-rescue operations, environmental monitoring, sample collection, and
seabed surveys (\cite{bechlioulisRobustFormationControl2019, merciSimulatorUnderwaterGlider2023}).

The physical configuration of an \ac{auv} is determined by its operational requirements. However,
most are torpedo-shaped \acp{auv} that are optimized for long-range and extended-duration missions.
Their streamlined form minimizes hydrodynamic drag, enabling efficient travel over large distances.
Propulsion may be achieved using conventional thrusters, or, in the case of gliders, through a
buoyancy-driven system. The latter allows vertical movement by adjusting overall density and
internal mass distribution. Horizontal motion is then generated by hydrofoils that convert vertical
displacement into forward glide (\cite{meyerGliderTechnologyOcean2016}).

\subsection{Other vehicles}
\label{sota_uuv_other_vehicles}

Several underwater vehicle designs exist beyond conventional \ac{rov} or \ac{auv} architectures.
These platforms are intended to improve maneuverability, energy efficiency, or mission
adaptability. Notable examples include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Biomimetic vehicles, which are inspired by aquatic organisms and aim to replicate their propulsion
	      methods using flexible fins, undulating body motions, or flapping appendages. These systems
	      generate minimal hydrodynamic disturbance, thereby enhancing stealth and environmental
	      compatibility. Examples include the RoboTuna, presented
	      in~\cite{triantafyllouEfficientSwimmingMachine1995}. The humanoid Ocean One K
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_ocean_one_k}) also exhibits biomimetic traits through its human-like structure
	      and dexterous manipulation capabilities.

	\item Reconfigurable vehicles, which may be adaptable in multiple ways. Some systems allow for thruster
	      repositioning to optimize hydrodynamic performance and energy usage depending on specific mission
	      requirements~\cite{fagundesgasparotoAdvancesReconfigurableVectorial2021}. Others, such as Hybrid
	      \acp{rov}, are capable of operating either in autonomous mode or under remote control. These
	      vehicles generally rely on onboard batteries and a lightweight fiber-optic tether, in contrast to
	      standard work-class \acp{rov}. The Ariane vehicle, developed by \ac{ifremer}, exemplifies this
	      class with its ability to switch to autonomous operation when
	      required~\cite{raugelOperationalScientificCapabilities2019}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Cables in underwater robotics}
\label{sota_cables_in_underwater_robotics}

Cable modeling is a foundational aspect of underwater robotics, as the dynamic behavior of tethers
and umbilicals directly impacts the performance, safety, and operational envelope of remotely
operated and autonomous underwater vehicles. Accurate cable models are essential for simulation,
design, and real-time estimation tasks, enabling the prediction of cable shape, tension, and
interaction with the environment under a variety of operational scenarios. The complexity of
underwater cable dynamics requires specialized modeling approaches that can capture the coupling
between the cable, vehicle, and fluid environment while maintaining computational efficiency for
real-time applications.

\subsection{Cable shape estimation}

A wide range of modeling approaches have been developed to capture the complex mechanics of
underwater cables. Lumped-mass and rigid-flexible multibody models are commonly used to represent
the cable as a series of interconnected masses and springs, allowing for efficient simulation of
large deformations and dynamic coupling with the
vehicle~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
These models are particularly effective for real-time applications, as they balance computational
efficiency with physical fidelity. The lumped-mass approach has been extensively applied in towing
systems, where the cable is discretized into rigid links connected by fictitious elastic joints
that account for bending and elevation movements~\cite{dasilvagomesNewDynamicModel2021}.

\Acp{fem} have emerged as powerful tools for underwater cable modeling,
providing high-fidelity representations of cable dynamics including bending stiffness, compression,
and hydrodynamic effects. The application of Euler-Bernoulli beam theory enables the simulation of
low-tension scenarios and accounts for cable bending, which is critical for operations involving
slack or contact with obstacles~\cite{o.aFiniteElementCablemodel2018}. The Galerkin finite element
method, combined with advanced time integration schemes, allows for the accurate
temporal evolution of cable states. Experimental validation in ocean tank environments has
demonstrated the effectiveness of these models in replicating real-world cable behavior.

Advanced numerical techniques have been developed to address specific challenges in underwater
cable dynamics. Marine cables, primarily designed to support axial loads, experience significant
bending effects during low-tension applications such as umbilical modeling for ROVs or during slack
events~\cite{palmInfluenceBendingStiffness2020}. The implementation of bending stiffness using
rotation-free, nested local Discontinuous Galerkin methods into existing Lax-Friedrichs-type
solvers has shown exponential convergence and improved accuracy in capturing snap load events in
deep water tethers.

For cables subject to complex loading conditions, geometrically exact beam formulations have been
employed to capture large displacements, large rotations, and cross-sectional deformation effects.
These approaches are particularly relevant for aerial and underwater towed systems where
aerodynamic or hydrodynamic loads are computed on each element and then
integrated~\cite{dehadraiTransientPlanarDynamics2022}. The inclusion of damage effects and strain
localization in rope response modeling has been addressed through nonlinear finite element methods
that account for helical structure and axial-bending, axial-torsional, and bending-torsional
interactions~\cite{beltranEvaluationCoupledEffect2020}.

Kirchhoff rod theory has been applied to model deep-sea umbilical cables under complex sea
conditions, with differential quadrature and Newmark methods used for spatial and temporal
discretization~\cite{chenDynamicCharacteristicsDeepsea2021}. This approach has revealed that
amplitude amplification rates are directly proportional to ocean current velocity and mother ship
motion amplitude, while being inversely proportional to the period of motion. The results indicate
that umbilical cable length significantly affects dynamic response, with amplitude amplification
reaching maximum values at specific cable lengths around 3000 meters.

State estimation and cable shape reconstruction are fundamental to the operation of both tethered
and untethered underwater robotic systems. Inertial-measurement-based techniques have been proposed
for estimating the catenary shape of submerged cables by employing \acp{imu} affixed along the
cable length. These sensors provide local tangent estimations, allowing the reconstruction of the
overall cable profile with high fidelity~\cite{druptInertialmeasurementbasedCatenaryShape2022}.
Compared to vision-based approaches, this method exhibits increased robustness in environments with
limited visibility and occlusions, and extends the feasible estimation range. Recent developments
have demonstrated IMU-based monitoring of buoy-ballast systems through comprehensive cable dynamics
simulation frameworks, providing three-dimensional orientation and curvature amplitude information
for enhanced operational safety~\cite{peraudIMUbasedMonitoringBuoyBallast2024}. Additional
sensor-based technologies, such as ShapeTape, have been demonstrated for underwater cable shape
detection and dynamic tracking~\cite{xuUnderwaterCableShape2016}.

Recent advances in sensing technologies have further enhanced cable modeling capabilities. Fiber
optic shape sensors and distributed optical fiber systems provide real-time, continuous tracking of
the three-dimensional shape and position of cables, even in the absence of visual
contact~\cite{florisFiberOpticShape2021}. These sensors offer advantages such as intrinsic safety,
flexibility, and resistance to harsh underwater conditions, making them suitable for integration
into modern robotic systems.

Visual-based approaches have been developed for cable shape estimation in underwater environments.
Catenary-based visual servoing schemes utilize parameterized catenary models to estimate
three-dimensional tether shape parameters through nonlinear optimization procedures adapted from
Gauss-Newton algorithms~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These methods demonstrate
robustness even in situations of tether partial visibility and strong disturbances, proving
reliable for real-time applications.

Multi-body formulations and rigid-flexible coupling models have been developed to simulate the
interaction among the vehicle, the tether, and associated tether management
systems~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
These models enable accurate prediction of tether behavior under dynamic conditions, such as
deep-sea deployment, reeling, and fluid-structure interaction. The incorporation of \ac{ancf} and
mixed-coordinate methods has allowed for the realistic simulation of large deformations, nonlinear
coupling, and deployment dynamics.

\subsection{Cable managment}

Effective cable management is crucial for underwater robotic operations, as tethers and umbilicals
pose significant challenges including entanglement risks, unpredictable shapes, and complex
interactions with the environment. Various strategies have been developed to address these
challenges, ranging from passive self-management systems to active control approaches.

Passive self-management systems represent an innovative approach to cable management in underwater
robotics. The addition of moving ballasts and buoys to ROV umbilicals enables the creation of
predictable cable shapes while avoiding
self-entanglement~\cite{vielSelfManagementROVUmbilical2022}. These elements move freely along the
umbilical to maintain cable tension without requiring motorized systems, making the approach easily
adaptable to existing ROVs with minimal constraints on navigation. Experimental validation in pool
environments has demonstrated the effectiveness of this method, with fast computational models
providing real-time feedback to operators.

Advanced self-management techniques have been developed for different operational scenarios. Three
distinct umbilical configurations have been proposed to optimize performance for specific mission
types: near-surface exploration, sea exploration, and diving in the presence of large
obstacles~\cite{vielSelfmanagementUmbilicalROV2022}. The consideration of environmental factors
such as waves and currents in these models enables robust performance across varied operational
conditions.

More recent developments in cable management have introduced general three-dimensional models that
can estimate umbilical shape regardless of force orientation applied to management
elements~\cite{vielSelfmanagementROVUmbilical2024}. This advancement allows for the consideration
of underwater currents, passive or motorized elements on the tether, and the presence or absence of
tether management systems (TMS). The flexibility of these models supports various umbilical
configurations optimized for different environmental conditions including near-surface, seafloor,
near-wall, and deep-dive operations.

Traditional tether management systems have evolved from simple mechanical winches to sophisticated
active control systems. Dynamic mooring models have been implemented to provide operators with
real-time analysis of cable behavior under hydrodynamic effects and tension
variations~\cite{aaseModelingControlCatenary2018}. These systems incorporate safety considerations
through tension constraints that prevent line breaking and alarm systems that notify operators when
vessels exceed operational limits of anchor points.

Cable routing and manipulation represent emerging areas in underwater robotics, with applications
extending to intervention tasks. Robotic cable routing frameworks utilize spatial representations
to model cable-environment interactions, enabling high-level path planning from initial to target
configurations~\cite{jinRoboticCableRouting2022}. These systems incorporate multiple manipulation
primitives learned from human demonstration, allowing for efficient cable configuration to planned
intermediate states using consistent spatial representations.

For systems employing multiple interconnected vehicles, specialized cable management strategies
have been developed. Smart cables equipped with relative position tracking capabilities eliminate
the need for additional positioning sensors while providing active buoyancy
control~\cite{yuArmlessUnderwaterManipulation2013}. This approach enables the use of small,
deployable agent \acp{rov} as end effectors connected to main vehicles through flexible smart
cables, significantly enhancing manipulation capabilities in constrained underwater environments.

The integration of cable management with vehicle systems represents a critical aspect of modern
underwater robotics. Hybrid systems that combine cable length adjustment mechanisms with vehicle
propulsion systems have demonstrated effective operational
capabilities~\cite{wuTrajectoryFollowingTethered2019}. These integrated approaches recognize that
cable length adjustment primarily affects vertical positioning, while propeller operation dominates
horizontal positioning.

Simulation platforms, such as the UUV Simulator based on Gazebo, have been developed to support the
modeling and analysis of underwater cables in multi-robot and intervention scenarios. These tools
incorporate hydrodynamic effects, thruster models, and external disturbances, enabling
comprehensive evaluation of cable-robot-environment
interactions~\cite{manhaesUUVSimulatorGazebobased2016}. Specialized simulation environments have
been created for specific underwater vehicle classes, with the BlueROV2 simulation model
implemented in Simulink™ providing a benchmark platform for algorithm development and
validation~\cite{vonbenzonOpenSourceBenchmarkSimulator2022}.

Comprehensive hydrodynamic models have been developed that couple the main body of underwater
robots, umbilical cables, and propulsion systems into integrated simulation
environments~\cite{wuIntegratedHydrodynamicsControl2018}. These models solve Navier-Stokes
equations using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) techniques while incorporating cable dynamics
based on finite difference methods. Experimental validation remains crucial for cable model
development and verification, with ocean tank experiments using real ROV systems providing
essential validation data for finite element cable models~\cite{o.aFiniteElementCablemodel2018}.
Field validation studies have demonstrated the importance of bending stiffness in cable dynamics,
particularly for shorter tether lengths and snap load
events~\cite{palmInfluenceBendingStiffness2020}.

In summary, the state of the art in underwater cable modeling encompasses a spectrum of methods
ranging from lumped-mass and multibody approaches to high-fidelity finite element and sensor-driven
models. These advances, combined with innovative management strategies and comprehensive simulation
platforms, provide the foundation for reliable design, real-time estimation, and safe operation in
underwater robotic applications. The field continues to evolve with increasing emphasis on
real-time computational efficiency, multi-physics coupling, and integration with modern sensing
technologies.

\subsection{Robot state estimation}

Robot state estimation in underwater environments presents unique challenges due to the limitations
of traditional sensing modalities and the complex dynamics of tethered systems. The underwater
environment severely restricts the use of conventional positioning systems such as GPS, requiring
specialized approaches for accurate localization and state estimation of underwater vehicles and
their associated tethers.

For positioning applications, innovative approaches have been developed to estimate \ac{rov}
position through umbilical shape observation. By equipping the umbilical with moving ballasts and
buoys to create predictable shapes with straight line segments, simple mathematical models can be
established that enable position estimation through angle measurements at cable
ends~\cite{vielROVLocalizationBased2023}. This approach transforms the traditionally problematic
cable into a positioning asset.

For tethered underwater systems, state estimation becomes particularly complex due to the coupling
between vehicle dynamics and cable behavior. Multi-body formulations have been developed to
estimate the combined state of the vehicle and tether system, accounting for the dynamic
interactions between these components~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021}. These
approaches recognize that accurate vehicle state estimation requires understanding of the tether's
influence on the overall system dynamics.

Sensor fusion techniques have been developed to combine information from multiple onboard sensors
for robust state estimation. Standard low-cost sensors including cameras, depth sensors, and
\acp{imu} are commonly integrated to provide comprehensive state
information~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. The fusion of these heterogeneous
sensor modalities enables robust estimation even in challenging underwater conditions with limited
visibility and external disturbances.

Vision-based state estimation approaches have shown particular promise for underwater robotics.
These methods utilize cameras to extract geometric information about the robot's environment and
its own configuration. For tethered systems, visual servoing techniques can simultaneously estimate
both robot pose and tether shape parameters through nonlinear optimization procedures adapted from
Gauss-Newton algorithms~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. Such approaches
demonstrate robustness in situations of partial visibility and strong disturbances, proving
reliable for real-time applications.

Innovative positioning methods have been developed that exploit the tether itself as a sensing
element. By equipping umbilicals with moving ballasts and buoys to create predictable shapes,
simple mathematical models can be established that enable ROV position estimation through angle
measurements at cable ends~\cite{vielROVLocalizationBased2023}. This approach transforms the
traditionally problematic cable into a positioning asset, providing an alternative to conventional
underwater positioning systems.

Advanced filtering and estimation techniques have been applied to handle the uncertainties and
disturbances inherent in underwater environments. Robust state estimation methods have been
developed to address issues related to sensor noise, hydrodynamic disturbances, and actuator
degradation~\cite{songRobustStateEstimation2023}. These approaches often incorporate hybrid
techniques combining robust filtering with sliding-window estimation to improve system resilience
and autonomy under uncertain operational conditions.

For multi-robot systems, distributed state estimation approaches have been investigated to enable
cooperative localization and formation control. These methods allow groups of underwater vehicles
to maintain prescribed formations and track reference trajectories while sharing state information
to improve overall estimation accuracy~\cite{khalajiLyapunovBasedFormationControl2020}. The
distributed nature of these approaches enhances robustness and mission coherence in multi-agent
underwater operations.

Parent-child robot configurations present unique state estimation challenges, where large parent
vehicles transport and control smaller tethered child robots. Mathematical modeling of these
systems considers the hydrodynamic coupling between the vehicles, requiring specialized estimation
approaches that account for the nonlinear time delays and complex dynamics
involved~\cite{kimParentchildUnderwaterRobotbased2023}. Complementary sensing methods are often
employed based on the observation distance and operational requirements.

The integration of state estimation with cable shape reconstruction has emerged as a critical
capability for tethered underwater systems. Real-time estimation of both vehicle states and cable
configuration enables more effective mission planning and execution. These combined estimation
approaches often utilize parameterized models of cable behavior, allowing for efficient real-time
computation while maintaining sufficient accuracy for control applications.

Experimental validation of state estimation approaches remains crucial for underwater robotics
applications. Tank experiments and field trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of various
estimation techniques under realistic operational conditions, including scenarios with current
disturbances, wave effects, and varying visibility conditions. These validation studies provide
essential data for model calibration and performance assessment of state estimation algorithms in
practical underwater environments.

\section{Robot control}
\label{sota_robot_control}

This section reviews control strategies developed for unmanned robotic systems operating with
tether constraints. Emphasis is placed on methods that account for dynamic coupling with the
environment, constraints imposed by tethers, and the challenges of limited sensing and delayed
feedback. The discussion is structured according to the robot's configuration: tethered,
untethered, and tethered underwater systems.

\subsection{Tethered robots}
\label{sota_rc_tethered}

The control of tethered robots has historically encountered distinct challenges due to the complex
interactions among the vehicle, its tether, and the surrounding environment. The tether introduces
additional dynamic components into the control architecture, including tension variability, drag,
oscillatory behavior, and risks of entanglement or collision with obstacles.

Although this study primarily focuses on underwater robots, similar control challenges are
encountered in aerial robotics, where tethers may be used not only for energy delivery, data
transmission, or mechanical stabilization, but also as active elements in cooperative manipulation
tasks. For example, a catenary robot --comprising two \acp{uav} connected by a flexible cable-- was
designed to autonomously locate and transport hook-shaped
objects~\cite{dantonioCatenaryRobotDesign2021}. In that system, the cable's position, orientation,
and span were regulated in real time to facilitate manipulation tasks without requiring prior
mechanical attachment. Such applications demonstrate that tether dynamics can be exploited as
functional assets, rather than merely compensated for.

Recent advancements in tethered aerial systems have enabled the development of multi-vehicle
configurations featuring dynamic tether management. In particular, systems comprising chains of
multicopters connected via elastic tethers have been implemented, where tether lengths are actively
adjusted using controlled winches~\cite{fagianoSystemsTetheredMulticopters2017}. These
architectures employ hierarchical control frameworks that consider the elastic nature of the
tethers and their unidirectional load-bearing characteristics, thereby enhancing endurance and task
flexibility across various operational domains.

The modeling and control of tethered aerial vehicles have evolved to incorporate novel mechanical
arrangements, such as pulley-gimbal mechanisms. These configurations allow \acp{uav} to move freely
along tethers while enabling accurate position tracking~\cite{kosarnovskyStringTetheredDrones2019}.
The overall system can be conceptualized as a robotic manipulator, wherein each tether segment
functions as a variable-length link and each \ac{uav} acts as an articulated joint actuator. This
perspective facilitates complex coordinated motion through hybrid geometric and linear control
strategies.

For systems involving multiple tethers, specialized state estimation techniques have been proposed
to reduce sensor dependency while preserving observability. Nonlinear observer designs, including
high-gain and Luenberger observers, have been shown to accurately estimate full system states using
minimal instrumentation --such as dual accelerometers, encoders, or
inclinometers~\cite{tognonNonlinearObserverControl2015}. These observers support precise regulation
of both Cartesian position and internal tether forces in bi-tethered multi-agent systems.

Fundamental properties of tethered aerial robots have been analytically characterized, revealing
differential flatness with respect to specific output pairs. Notable flat outputs include
combinations such as the link elevation and vehicle attitude, or link elevation and longitudinal
tension~\cite{tognonDynamicsControlEstimation2017}. This characterization supports the formulation
of almost globally convergent nonlinear controllers and observers that are capable of tracking
smooth, time-varying trajectories using only onboard accelerometers and gyroscopes. These
approaches offer robustness against nonlinear coupling effects, parameter uncertainties, and sensor
noise.

In the domain of space robotics, tethers have been investigated for applications in momentum
exchange, orbital transfer, and energy harvesting. Electrodynamic tethers, for instance, have been
explored as mechanisms for satellite deorbiting or artificial gravity generation. These systems
impose unique control challenges involving deployment dynamics, tension regulation, and oscillation
suppression in microgravity environments. The absence of atmospheric damping further complicates
the stabilization of long, flexible tethers during deployment and
retrieval~\cite{modiDeploymentDynamicsTether1979,huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}.

Advanced control methodologies have been formulated to address critical issues such as tether
entanglement around uncooperative, tumbling targets. Energy-based control frameworks exploiting the
system’s passivity properties have been proposed, wherein potential energy is expressed in terms of
tether torque to direct the target’s angular velocity toward the tether
axis~\cite{wangAntitangleControlTethered2019}. These strategies frequently incorporate sliding mode
controllers optimized via model predictive control, and integrate adaptive weighting mechanisms to
enhance entanglement avoidance.

Dexterous tethered space robots have demonstrated feasibility for missions involving geostationary
orbit debris removal. Such systems typically combine lightweight construction with cost-efficient
operational paradigms. Integrated vision-based pose estimation and coordinated control designs have
enabled precise manipulation of non-cooperative targets in
orbit~\cite{huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}. These control schemes must account for the coupling
introduced by the tether, while ensuring that mission objectives are reliably satisfied through
sophisticated coordination between the robotic platform and its tethered base.

\subsection{Underwater robots}
\label{sota_rc_underwater}

The control of underwater robots entails distinctive challenges arising from the nonlinear,
uncertain, and dynamic characteristics of the aquatic environment. These challenges include
hydrodynamic forces, buoyancy variability, unpredictable ocean currents, limited sensory input, and
the presence of static or dynamic obstacles. To address these issues, a wide array of modeling,
estimation, and control strategies has been developed in recent years.

Underwater manipulation has been advanced through the integration of deployable agent vehicles
connected via smart, flexible tethers. In place of conventional rigid manipulator arms, small and
highly maneuverable agent remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) have been utilized as mobile
end-effectors. The smart cable in this configuration provides relative position tracking and active
buoyancy control, enabling fine manipulation and monitoring with minimal structural modifications
to the primary platform~\cite{yuArmlessUnderwaterManipulation2013}. This paradigm demonstrates the
feasibility of distributed manipulation architectures tailored for cluttered or constrained
underwater environments.

Robust control approaches remain essential to ensure stable and reliable underwater operation.
\Acp{nmpc} schemes have been formulated to navigate vehicles in constrained workspaces, accounting
for environmental obstacles, boundary limitations, thruster saturation, and ocean
currents~\cite{heshmati-alamdariRobustPredictiveControl2020}. To mitigate the influence of
unmodeled hydrodynamic phenomena and reduce tracking errors, sliding mode control has been combined
with Gaussian process regression, producing data-driven compensatory
mechanisms~\cite{limaSlidingModeControl2020}. Additionally, adaptive neural network-based
controllers optimized using genetic algorithms have demonstrated resilience against parametric
uncertainty and external disturbances, particularly in the control of underwater
manipulators~\cite{salloomAdaptiveNeuralNetwork2020}.

As multi-agent operations gain prominence in subsea applications, formation control and cooperative
mission planning have become critical. Lyapunov-based control frameworks have been employed to
ensure that groups of \acp{auv} maintain prescribed formations and track reference trajectories,
thereby enhancing robustness and mission coherence~\cite{khalajiLyapunovBasedFormationControl2020}.
Concurrently, advances in robust state estimation and fault diagnosis have addressed issues related
to sensor noise, hydrodynamic disturbances, and actuator degradation. Notably, hybrid techniques
combining robust filtering with sliding-window estimation have been developed to improve system
resilience and autonomy under uncertain operational
conditions~\cite{songRobustStateEstimation2023}.

\subsection{Underwater tethered robots}
\label{sota_rc_underwater_tethered}

A broad spectrum of control strategies has been formulated to address the unique challenges
associated with underwater tethered robotic systems, wherein dynamic coupling among the vehicle,
the tether, and the environment imposes significant influence on stability and maneuverability.
Early research on tether management systems laid foundational principles for the safe deployment
and retrieval of umbilicals, focusing on mechanical reliability and operational
robustness~\cite{abelUnderwaterVehicleTether1994}.

Subsequent developments introduced advanced dynamic models that incorporate vehicle-tether
interactions, enabling precise analysis and controller synthesis. Notably, lumped-mass models and
rigid-flexible multibody formulations have been employed to capture the complex motion of tethered
systems under various environmental and operational
conditions~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
These models support the design of control algorithms capable of compensating for disturbances
arising from cable tension fluctuations, hydrodynamic loads, and oscillatory tether-induced forces.

Robust and adaptive control techniques have been integrated to ensure reliable trajectory tracking
and position regulation in uncertain and time-varying environments. Controllers based on sliding
mode and \ldscal{L}{1} adaptive architectures have shown strong resilience against model
uncertainties and environmental disturbances, including during manipulator operation and under
fluctuating cable
lengths~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,maaloufNovelApplicationMultivariable2012}. Empirical
evaluations indicate that incorporating tether force feedback at the termination point, along with
adaptive gain tuning in real time, substantially enhances control performance and disturbance
rejection capabilities.

In parallel, hydrodynamic-aware control strategies have been explored to explicitly incorporate
fluid-structure interaction into control laws. \Ac{cfd}-informed models, supported by experimental
validation, have demonstrated that understanding the coupled influence of the tether, vehicle
geometry, and flow dynamics can be leveraged to optimize maneuverability and energy efficiency in
strong current environments~\cite{wuManeuverabilityHydrodynamicsTethered2021}.

Hybrid and dual-mode systems have introduced new requirements for flexible control architectures.
Platforms such as \ac{ifremer}'s Ariane operate in both tethered and autonomous configurations,
necessitating controllers capable of mode switching and environmental
adaptation~\cite{brignoneFirstSeaTrials2015}. Further, dual-robot systems --such as the MEDUSA
architecture-- combine aerial and underwater agents linked via a tether, expanding the control
domain to include inter-vehicle coordination for deployment, sampling, and retrieval
tasks~\cite{debruynMEDUSAMultiEnvironmentDualRobot2020}.

Sensor-driven control strategies have also been developed to regulate tether geometry and maintain
relative positioning in multi-agent configurations. Catenary-based visual servoing algorithms have
employed onboard cameras and inertial measurement units to estimate and control the
three-dimensional shape of the tether in real time. These systems support stable coordination even
in scenarios involving visual occlusion and external
disturbances~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.

Overall, the literature indicates that effective control of underwater tethered robotic systems
relies on the integration of high-fidelity dynamic modeling, adaptive and robust control methods,
and sensor-based feedback architectures. These elements collectively address the nonlinear,
coupled, and uncertain dynamics that characterize tethered marine robotics and enable reliable
operation in complex environments.

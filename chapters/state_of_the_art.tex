\chapter{State of the art}
\label{state_of_the_art}

\minitoc{}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art concerning the
control of chains of underwater robots interconnected via tethers, operating within known but
constrained environments. Two primary scientific challenges are addressed throughout this chapter:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The safe and reliable control of underwater robots navigating through constrained environments;
	\item The incorporation of tether dynamics—specifically, their shape and the forces they exert—into the
	      overall control framework.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, this chapter begins by reviewing developments in unmanned underwater robotics in
\cref{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}, emphasizing the various types of \acp{uuv}. Research
related to cables used in underwater robotics is then examined
(\cref{sota_cables_in_underwater_robotics}), with a focus on cable shape estimation and management
techniques. Finally, control strategies for underwater robots are assessed in
\cref{sota_robot_control}, with particular attention given to tethered configurations and their
specific control challenges.

\section{\Glsentrylongpl{uuv}}
\label{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}

Unmanned underwater robots are essential for a broad range of applications, including marine
research, environmental monitoring, and underwater exploration. These robotic platforms are
generally categorized into two primary classes according to their operational modes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Utilizing tethers, \acp{rov} maintain a physical link with a surface vessel, thereby enabling
	      high-bandwidth, real-time communication and control, teleoperation, and continuous power supply;
	\item Operating independently, \acp{auv} rely on preprogrammed missions, limited communications, and
	      onboard sensors for navigation and data acquisition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{rov}}
\label{sota_uuv_remotely_operated_vehicles}

Remotely operated vehicles \acp{rov} are widely deployed in marine science and industrial
operations. These systems are typically equipped with cameras, environmental sensors, and robotic
manipulators, facilitating tasks such as underwater inspection, maintenance, and deep-sea
exploration. \Acp{rov} are highly maneuverable and are capable of operating at substantial depths,
thus allowing them to perform missions deemed hazardous or impractical for human divers. Their
operations are continuously supervised and controlled by human operators from the surface, which
limits their range to the tether length.

To ensure reliable high-bandwidth communication, \acp{rov} are connected to surface vessels via
tethers. This configuration is necessitated by the high absorption of electromagnetic waves in
water, which precludes conventional wireless communication over distances exceeding a few meters.

As illustrated in \cref{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum}, the absorption coefficient of water
decreases with increasing wavelength. Thus, \ac{elf} electromagnetic waves (in the
\qtyrange{3}{300}{\hertz} range) can propagate farther underwater. However, as discussed
in~\cite{altgeltWorldsLargestRadiostation2005}, such frequencies would require impractically large
antennas --on the order of kilometers-- and high transmission power, rendering them unsuitable for
use in mobile underwater platforms.

According to~\cite{christROVManualUser2013}, \acp{rov} are classified by size and functionality
into three main categories:

\begin{itemize}	\item Work-class \acp{rov} (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_victor_6000}) exceed \qty{1000}{\kilo\gram} and are
	      designed for complex tasks such as subsea construction, maintenance, and repair. These systems are
	      typically equipped with hydraulic-powered thrusters and manipulators, in addition to a
	      comprehensive suite of sensors. Work-class \acp{rov} can operate at depths greater than
	      \qty{3000}{\meter} and are launched from specialized surface support vessels
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}). Their tethers are designed to deliver power to the
	      robot over great distances and require substantial cable management infrastructure aboard the host
	      vessel due to their weight and length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\Glsentrylongpl{auv}}
\label{sota_uuv_autonomous_underwater_vehicles}

An \ac{auv} is an untethered robotic platform capable of operating without real-time communication
(\cref{sota_uuv_auv_asterix}). Although data exchange is possible via acoustic channels, it remains
highly constrained in range and bandwidth (\cite{creuzeRobotsMarinsSousmarins2014}). \Ac{auv} are
typically programmed prior to deployment with mission objectives and rely on onboard sensors,
control algorithms, and occasional communication upon surfacing to accomplish tasks such as
bathymetric mapping, search-and-rescue operations, environmental monitoring, sample collection, and
seabed surveys (\cite{bechlioulisRobustFormationControl2019, merciSimulatorUnderwaterGlider2023}).

The physical configuration of an \ac{auv} is determined by its operational requirements. However,
most are torpedo-shaped \acp{auv} that are optimized for long-range and extended-duration missions.
Their streamlined form minimizes hydrodynamic drag, enabling efficient travel over large distances.
Propulsion may be achieved using conventional thrusters, or, in the case of gliders, through a
buoyancy-driven system. The latter allows vertical movement by adjusting overall density and
internal mass distribution. Horizontal motion is then generated by hydrofoils that convert vertical
displacement into forward glide (\cite{meyerGliderTechnologyOcean2016}).

\subsection{Other vehicles}
\label{sota_uuv_other_vehicles}

Several underwater vehicle designs exist beyond conventional \ac{rov} or \ac{auv} architectures.
These platforms are intended to improve maneuverability, energy efficiency, or mission
adaptability. Notable examples include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Biomimetic vehicles, which are inspired by aquatic organisms and aim to replicate their propulsion
	      methods using flexible fins, undulating body motions, or flapping appendages. These systems
	      generate minimal hydrodynamic disturbance, thereby enhancing stealth and environmental
	      compatibility. Examples include the RoboTuna, presented
	      in~\cite{triantafyllouEfficientSwimmingMachine1995}. The humanoid Ocean One K
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_ocean_one_k}) also exhibits biomimetic traits through its human-like structure
	      and dexterous manipulation capabilities.

	\item Reconfigurable vehicles, which may be adaptable in multiple ways. Some systems allow for thruster
	      repositioning to optimize hydrodynamic performance and energy usage depending on specific mission
	      requirements~\cite{fagundesgasparotoAdvancesReconfigurableVectorial2021}. Others, such as Hybrid
	      \acp{rov}, are capable of operating either in autonomous mode or under remote control. These
	      vehicles generally rely on onboard batteries and a lightweight fiber-optic tether, in contrast to
	      standard work-class \acp{rov}. The Ariane vehicle, developed by \ac{ifremer}, exemplifies this
	      class with its ability to switch to autonomous operation when
	      required~\cite{raugelOperationalScientificCapabilities2019}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Systems modelling}

\subsection{\Ac{uuv} modelling}

The modelling of \acp{uuv} is a critical aspect of underwater robotics, as it provides the basis
for simulation and model based control. It is however a relatively straightforward rigid body model
as described in~\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011} for any seafaring vessel:
\begin{align}
	\dot{\eta}                                                                                             & = \matr{T}\left(\eta\right)\nu \label{sota_uuv_modeling_kinematics}                                                                                                                                        \\
	\lrdmatr{M}{rb} \dot{\nu} + \lrdmatr{M}{a} \ldvect{\dot{\nu}}{r} + \lrdmatr{C}{rb}\left(\nu\right) \nu & + \left(\lrdmatr{C}{a}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right) + \matr{D}\left(\ldvect{\nu}{r}\right)\right) \ldvect{\nu}{r} + s\left(\vect{\eta}\right) + \vect{b} = \omega + \tau \label{sota_uuv_modeling_dynamics}
\end{align}

where \vect{\eta} is the vehicle's position and orientation in the world frame, \vect{\nu} is its
linear and angular velocity in its own frame, \ldvect{\nu}{r} is the relative velocity of the
vehicle and the water, taking into account the underwater currents, \lrdmatr{M}{rb} is the inertia
matrix, \lrdmatr{M}{a} is the added mass matrix, \lrdmatr{C}{rb} and \lrdmatr{C}{a} are their
corresponding Coriolis matrices, \matr{D} is the drag matrix, \scal{s} is the hydrostatic force,
\vect{b} is the constant ballast force, \ldvect{\tau}{\text{wind}} is the wind force,
\ldvect{\tau}{\text{waves}} is the wave force, and \vect{\tau} is the control force. The
transformation matrix \matr{T} links the vehicle and world frames.

With the assumption that the water current is irrotational and the vehicle is operated underwater
without ballasts, \cref{sota_uuv_modeling_dynamics} can be simplified
(\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}) to:
\begin{align}
	\matr{I}\dot{\nu} + \left(\matr{C}\left(\vect{\nu}\right) + \matr{D}\left(\vect{\nu}\right)\right) \vect{\nu} + s\left(\vect{\eta}\right) = \omega + \tau \label{sota_uuv_modeling_dynamics_simple}
\end{align}

With \matr{I} being the inertia matrix, \matr{C} the Coriolis matrix, and \matr{D} the drag matrix.

furthermore, \cref{sota_uuv_modeling_kinematics,sota_uuv_modeling_dynamics_simple} can be expressed
as a state-space system with $\vect{x} = \left[\vect{\eta}, \vect{\nu}\right]^T$:
\begin{align}
	\begin{bmatrix}
		\lrdmatr{I}{d} & 0        \\
		0              & \matr{I}
	\end{bmatrix} \vect{\dot{x}} =
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 & \matr{T}             \\
		0 & -\matr{C} - \matr{D}
	\end{bmatrix} \vect{x} +
	\begin{bmatrix}
		0 \\
		\vect{\omega} + \vect{\tau} - s\left(\vect{\eta}\right)
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

\subsection{Tether modelling}

Tether modeling is a foundational aspect of underwater robotics using \acp{rov}, as the dynamic
behavior of tethers and umbilicals directly impacts the performance, safety, and operational
envelope of the vehicles. Accurate cable models are essential for simulation, design, and real-time
estimation tasks, enabling the prediction of cable shape, tension, and interaction with the
environment under a variety of operational scenarios. The complexity of underwater cable dynamics
requires specialized modeling approaches that can capture the coupling between the cable, vehicle,
and fluid environment while maintaining computational efficiency for potential real-time
applications.

The accurate modeling of underwater cable shape remains central to effective tethered robotic
operations, as the mechanical behavior of cables directly affects vehicle dynamics, energy
consumption, and mission safety. A diverse range of modeling techniques has been developed to
represent the physical characteristics of submerged cables across varying operational regimes.

Classical modeling approaches include lumped-mass and nodal coordinate formulations, which
approximate cables as a series of interconnected rigid bodies linked via elastic joints. These
models facilitate simulation of large deformations and dynamic coupling with the
vehicle~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
The lumped-mass framework has seen widespread application in underwater towing systems, where
discrete segments capture both bending and elevation
dynamics~\cite{dasilvagomesNewDynamicModel2021}. These methods may be used to compute the shape of
a tether as well as the forces it exerts on its attachment points, however, in applied cases, they
are mostly used to compute forces. The computational time of nodal coordinate formulation can be as
low as \qty{1}{\second} (\cite{westinEfficientSemiimplicitNumerical2023}).

\Acp{fem} have emerged as high-fidelity alternatives for underwater cable
simulation. By incorporating Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, \acp{fem} enable accurate modeling of bending
stiffness and low-tension dynamics—conditions typical in slack cable operations or obstacle contact
scenarios~\cite{o.aFiniteElementCablemodel2018}. Galerkin-based time integration schemes have also been
employed to resolve the temporal evolution of cable states with high resolution, and experimental
ocean tank trials have confirmed their predictive capability~\cite{palmInfluenceBendingStiffness2020}.
These enhancements account for the transition between axial and bending-dominated regimes, which is
critical in applications such as \ac{rov} umbilicals or low-tension cable deployment.

For cables undergoing large deformations, geometrically exact beam theories has been employed to
model displacement, rotation, and cross-sectional deformation in a unified
framework~\cite{dehadraiTransientPlanarDynamics2022}. Such formulations are especially applicable
to aerial or underwater towed systems, where distributed hydrodynamic or aerodynamic loads are
integrated across cable segments. Additionally, nonlinear \acp{fem} have been extended to
incorporate structural degradation effects, including damage localization and strain-induced
coupling among axial, torsional, and bending modes~\cite{beltranEvaluationCoupledEffect2020}.

An alternative to dynamic cable modeling has been proposed through the use of an ellipsoid tether
model, which provides a conservative geometric envelope around the cable based solely on its
attachment points and length~\cite{vielEllipsoidTetherModel2024a}. This model enables guaranteed
collision avoidance without requiring knowledge of the tether's shape or dynamics. Initially
developed for multi-\ac{rov} scenarios, it integrates efficiently with potential field methods and
tether length management, offering a lightweight and scalable solution for real-time applications
in cluttered environments.

Beyond modeling, real-time estimation of cable shape has been pursued through various sensing
strategies. Inertial-measurement-based methods have been developed, wherein \acp{imu} affixed along
the cable length estimate local tangents, enabling accurate reconstruction of the global cable
profile using a simple catenary shape~\cite{druptInertialmeasurementbasedCatenaryShape2022}.
Compared to vision-based techniques, these approaches exhibit superior robustness under occlusions
and low-visibility conditions, while extending the spatial range of shape reconstruction.

Catenary-based visual servoing algorithms estimate three-dimensional cable geometry by fitting
parameterized catenary models to visual and inertial data using nonlinear optimization algorithms
derived from the Gauss-Newton method~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These
systems demonstrate resilience to partial visibility and high environmental disturbance, making
them suitable for cooperative multi-robot scenarios.

Recent advances have demonstrated the utility of \ac{imu} networks for monitoring buoy-ballast
systems through dynamic simulation frameworks. These systems provide orientation and curvature
amplitude data, supporting safer and more accurate tether
deployment~\cite{peraudIMUbasedMonitoringBuoyBallast2024}. Complementary sensor modalities, such as
ShapeTape, have also been employed for dynamic tracking of cable shape in underwater
conditions~\cite{xuUnderwaterCableShape2016}.

Effective cable management remains a critical requirement in underwater robotic operations, as
tethers and umbilicals introduce complexities including entanglement risks, unpredictable geometry,
and intricate interactions with the environment. A variety of strategies have been developed to
address these challenges, ranging from passive self-regulation to active control frameworks.

Passive self-management strategies have provided a low-complexity yet effective solution for
regulating umbilical geometry. The incorporation of free-moving ballasts and buoys along the tether
enables tension maintenance and predictable shape formation without requiring powered
actuation~\cite{vielSelfManagementROVUmbilical2022}. This approach facilitates integration into
existing ROV platforms with minimal modification while preserving navigational flexibility.
Experimental tests conducted in controlled pool environments have validated the method's efficacy,
supported by lightweight computational models capable of providing real-time operator feedback.

Specialized umbilical configurations have been proposed to suit diverse mission profiles. For
example, specific arrangements have been designed for near-surface surveys, seafloor exploration,
and navigation through obstacle-dense areas~\cite{vielSelfmanagementUmbilicalROV2022}. These
strategies consider hydrodynamic forces from waves and currents, enabling adaptability across
varying operational conditions.

More recently, general-purpose three-dimensional models have been developed to predict umbilical
shape under arbitrary external forces and tether
configurations~\cite{vielSelfmanagementROVUmbilical2024}. These models account for the presence of
currents, passive and motorized elements on the tether, and the optional inclusion of \acp{tms}.
Configurations suited for shallow water, deep-sea, near-wall, and cluttered environments can be
simulated with high flexibility. 

Validation through experimental data remains a key aspect of cable modeling. Ocean tank experiments
using real \ac{rov} systems have been conducted to verify the accuracy of finite element cable
models~\cite{o.aFiniteElementCablemodel2018}. Field validation has emphasized the importance of
bending stiffness in short-tether dynamics and in capturing snap load
effects~\cite{palmInfluenceBendingStiffness2020}.

For tethered \acp{rov}, innovative methods have been proposed that utilize the umbilical cable as a
passive sensing element. For instance, by using the \ac{imu}-based estimation of the cable's shape
presented in \cite{druptInertialmeasurementbasedCatenaryShape2022} the vehicle's position can be
inferred. 

Subsequent developments introduced advanced dynamic models that incorporate vehicle-tether
interactions, enabling precise analysis and controller synthesis. Notably, lumped-mass models and
rigid-flexible multibody formulations have been employed to capture the complex motion of tethered
systems under various environmental and operational
conditions~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
These models support the design of control algorithms capable of compensating for disturbances
arising from cable tension fluctuations, hydrodynamic loads, and oscillatory tether-induced forces.

Fiber-optic shape sensing technologies, including distributed fiber Bragg grating systems, have
enabled continuous three-dimensional cable shape estimation in harsh underwater
environments~\cite{florisFiberOpticShape2021}. These sensors provide intrinsic safety, immunity to
electromagnetic interference, and adaptability for long-duration deployment, making them ideal for
integration with tethered robotic platforms.

\subsection{Coupled modelling}

In multi-vehicle systems, cable management has been further refined through the use of smart cables
with integrated sensing. These cables offer relative positioning capabilities and active buoyancy
control without the need for separate localization
devices~\cite{yuArmlessUnderwaterManipulation2013}. This has enabled deployment of small, agile
ROVs as manipulative end-effectors tethered to a primary platform, enhancing the operational range
and dexterity in constrained environments.

Simulation platforms have been developed to analyze cable-robot-environment interactions. The
\ac{uuv} Simulator, based on the Gazebo framework, supports modeling of hydrodynamic effects,
thruster dynamics, and environmental disturbances~\cite{manhaesUUVSimulatorGazebobased2016}. In
parallel, class-specific simulators such as the BlueROV2 model implemented in
Simulink\texttrademark{} offer benchmarks for control algorithm development and
validation~\cite{vonbenzonOpenSourceBenchmarkSimulator2022}.

Integrated simulation environments have been constructed to model coupled dynamics between
\acp{rov}, tethers, and propulsion systems. \Ac{cfd}-based methods have been employed to solve
Navier-Stokes equations, with cable behavior modeled using finite difference
techniques~\cite{wuIntegratedHydrodynamicsControl2018}. These coupled hydrodynamic models offer
detailed insights into flow-structure interactions.

Finally, multi-body dynamic simulations have been used to assess the interaction among the vehicle,
tether, and tether management systems, particularly in deep-sea deployment and reeling
contexts~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021,htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022,banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}.
Incorporation of \acp{ancf} and mixed-coordinate methods allows for precise modeling of nonlinear
coupling effects and large deformations during real-time mission operations.

Alternatively, by incorporating mobile ballasts and buoys along the cable to enforce
predictable geometries composed of straight-line segments, simple geometric models can be
constructed. These allow for ROV position estimation through angular measurements at the tether
endpoints~\cite{vielROVLocalizationBased2023}. In this configuration, the umbilical itself becomes
a source of position feedback, transforming a traditional liability into an estimation asset.

In tethered configurations, state estimation is particularly complex due to the tight dynamic
coupling between the vehicle and the cable. Multi-body dynamic models have been developed to
estimate the combined state of vehicle-tether systems, accounting for the transmission of forces,
tether deformation, and fluid interaction effects~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021}.
These models recognize that accurate localization and velocity estimation depend on modeling the
tether's influence on overall vehicle motion.


Sensor fusion techniques may be employed to enhance estimation robustness by integrating
heterogeneous sensor inputs. Common combinations include \acp{imu}, depth sensors, and visual
sensors, with fusion frameworks designed to mitigate the limitations of each
modality~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These systems operate effectively under
adverse conditions such as turbidity, current disturbances, or low lighting.

Vision-based estimation methods have demonstrated particular efficacy in underwater robotics.
Through the use of onboard cameras, geometric and kinematic information about the robot's pose and
its surrounding environment can be extracted. In tethered scenarios, visual servoing approaches
have been employed to simultaneously estimate the vehicle pose and tether shape by optimizing
parameterized catenary models through nonlinear least-squares methods adapted from Gauss-Newton
algorithms~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These techniques remain effective even
with partial cable visibility or environmental perturbations.

To improve estimation accuracy under uncertainty, advanced filtering and hybrid observer designs
have been introduced. Robust state estimation algorithms have been developed to address sensor
noise, actuator degradation, and hydrodynamic disturbances~\cite{songRobustStateEstimation2023}.
Techniques combining high-gain filtering with sliding-window estimators have been shown to improve
system resilience in real-time operations under unpredictable conditions.

Distributed state estimation has been explored in the context of cooperative multi-robot underwater
systems. These methods allow multiple \acp{auv} to share state information while maintaining
individual observability, supporting collaborative behaviors such as formation keeping and
coordinated path tracking~\cite{khalajiLyapunovBasedFormationControl2020}. The decentralized
structure of these algorithms enhances robustness to communication delays and sensor failures.

Parent-child robotic configurations, where a large carrier vehicle deploys one or more smaller
tethered agents, introduce additional complexities. Estimation frameworks for such systems must
account for nonlinear hydrodynamic coupling, tether forces, and the time delays introduced by
flexible link dynamics~\cite{kimParentchildUnderwaterRobotbased2023}. Hybrid approaches have been
developed that combine mechanical models with complementary sensing strategies adapted to the
specific range and resolution requirements of the task.

For systems involving multiple tethers, specialized state estimation techniques have been proposed
to reduce sensor dependency while preserving observability. Nonlinear observer designs, including
high-gain and Luenberger observers, have been shown to accurately estimate full system states using
minimal instrumentation --such as dual accelerometers, encoders, or
inclinometers~\cite{tognonNonlinearObserverControl2015}. These observers support precise regulation
of both Cartesian position and internal tether forces in bi-tethered multi-agent systems.

Integration of state estimation with cable shape reconstruction has emerged as a critical
capability for real-time control in tethered systems. Joint estimation frameworks utilize
parameterized cable models in conjunction with vehicle state estimation to provide a unified
understanding of the robot-cable configuration. These models support both motion planning and
disturbance rejection by maintaining sufficient real-time accuracy while ensuring computational
tractability.

Experimental validation remains an essential aspect of estimator development for underwater
robotics. Controlled tank experiments and open-water field trials have been conducted to evaluate
algorithm performance under realistic disturbances such as wave-induced motion, visibility
reduction, and flow turbulence. These studies provide indispensable calibration data and allow
benchmarking of estimation algorithms in operational environments.

\section{Control}

\subsection{\Ac{uuv} control}

The control of underwater robots entails distinctive challenges arising from the nonlinear,
uncertain, and dynamic characteristics of the aquatic environment. These challenges include
hydrodynamic forces, buoyancy variability, unpredictable ocean currents, limited sensory input, and
the presence of static or dynamic obstacles. To address these issues, a wide array of modeling,
estimation, and control strategies has been developed in recent years.

Underwater manipulation has been advanced through the integration of deployable agent vehicles
connected via smart, flexible tethers. In place of conventional rigid manipulator arms, small and
highly maneuverable agent remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) have been utilized as mobile
end-effectors. The smart cable in this configuration provides relative position tracking and active
buoyancy control, enabling fine manipulation and monitoring with minimal structural modifications
to the primary platform~\cite{yuArmlessUnderwaterManipulation2013}. This paradigm demonstrates the
feasibility of distributed manipulation architectures tailored for cluttered or constrained
underwater environments.

Robust control approaches remain essential to ensure stable and reliable underwater operation.
\Acp{nmpc} schemes have been formulated to navigate vehicles in constrained workspaces, accounting
for environmental obstacles, boundary limitations, thruster saturation, and ocean
currents~\cite{heshmati-alamdariRobustPredictiveControl2020}. To mitigate the influence of
unmodeled hydrodynamic phenomena and reduce tracking errors, sliding mode control has been combined
with Gaussian process regression, producing data-driven compensatory
mechanisms~\cite{limaSlidingModeControl2020}. Additionally, adaptive neural network-based
controllers optimized using genetic algorithms have demonstrated resilience against parametric
uncertainty and external disturbances, particularly in the control of underwater
manipulators~\cite{salloomAdaptiveNeuralNetwork2020}.

As multi-agent operations gain prominence in subsea applications, formation control and cooperative
mission planning have become critical. Lyapunov-based control frameworks have been employed to
ensure that groups of \acp{auv} maintain prescribed formations and track reference trajectories,
thereby enhancing robustness and mission coherence~\cite{khalajiLyapunovBasedFormationControl2020}.
Concurrently, advances in robust state estimation and fault diagnosis have addressed issues related
to sensor noise, hydrodynamic disturbances, and actuator degradation. Notably, hybrid techniques
combining robust filtering with sliding-window estimation have been developed to improve system
resilience and autonomy under uncertain operational
conditions~\cite{songRobustStateEstimation2023}.


\subsection{Tethered \ac{uuv} control}

A broad spectrum of control strategies has been formulated to address the unique challenges
associated with underwater tethered robotic systems, wherein dynamic coupling among the vehicle,
the tether, and the environment imposes significant influence on stability and maneuverability.
Early research on tether management systems laid foundational principles for the safe deployment
and retrieval of umbilicals, focusing on mechanical reliability and operational
robustness~\cite{abelUnderwaterVehicleTether1994}.

Although this study primarily focuses on underwater robots, similar control challenges are
encountered in aerial robotics, where tethers may be used not only for energy delivery, data
transmission, or mechanical stabilization, but also as active elements in cooperative manipulation
tasks. For example, a catenary robot --comprising two \acp{uav} connected by a flexible cable-- was
designed to autonomously locate and transport hook-shaped
objects~\cite{dantonioCatenaryRobotDesign2021}. In that system, the cable's position, orientation,
and span were regulated in real time to facilitate manipulation tasks without requiring prior
mechanical attachment. Such applications demonstrate that tether dynamics can be exploited as
functional assets, rather than merely compensated for.

Recent advancements in tethered aerial systems have enabled the development of multi-vehicle
configurations featuring dynamic tether management. In particular, systems comprising chains of
multicopters connected via elastic tethers have been implemented, where tether lengths are actively
adjusted using controlled winches~\cite{fagianoSystemsTetheredMulticopters2017}. These
architectures employ hierarchical control frameworks that consider the elastic nature of the
tethers and their unidirectional load-bearing characteristics, thereby enhancing endurance and task
flexibility across various operational domains.

The modeling and control of tethered aerial vehicles have evolved to incorporate novel mechanical
arrangements, such as pulley-gimbal mechanisms. These configurations allow \acp{uav} to move freely
along tethers while enabling accurate position tracking~\cite{kosarnovskyStringTetheredDrones2019}.
The overall system can be conceptualized as a robotic manipulator, wherein each tether segment
functions as a variable-length link and each \ac{uav} acts as an articulated joint actuator. This
perspective facilitates complex coordinated motion through hybrid geometric and linear control
strategies.

Traditional \acp{tms} have evolved from mechanical winch-based
deployment to actively controlled architectures. Dynamic mooring models have been introduced to
simulate tether response to hydrodynamic loading, cable tension, and anchor-point
movement~\cite{aaseModelingControlCatenary2018}. These systems often incorporate safety mechanisms
such as tension constraints and alarm protocols, alerting operators when operational limits are
exceeded.

Advanced cable routing and manipulation techniques have emerged as key enablers for intervention
missions. Robotic cable routing frameworks now incorporate spatial and topological representations
to plan cable paths between initial and target configurations~\cite{jinRoboticCableRouting2022}.
These frameworks draw from manipulation primitives learned through human demonstrations and apply
high-level planning methods for intermediate cable state transitions.

Hybrid systems have demonstrated the effectiveness of combining tether length regulation mechanisms
with vehicle propulsion for coordinated control. Experimental studies have shown that while tether
length adjustment primarily governs vertical motion, horizontal motion is efficiently controlled
through propeller-based actuation~\cite{wuTrajectoryFollowingTethered2019}.

Fundamental properties of tethered aerial robots have been analytically characterized, revealing
differential flatness with respect to specific output pairs. Notable flat outputs include
combinations such as the link elevation and vehicle attitude, or link elevation and longitudinal
tension~\cite{tognonDynamicsControlEstimation2017}. This characterization supports the formulation
of almost globally convergent nonlinear controllers and observers that are capable of tracking
smooth, time-varying trajectories using only onboard accelerometers and gyroscopes. These
approaches offer robustness against nonlinear coupling effects, parameter uncertainties, and sensor
noise.

In the domain of space robotics, tethers have been investigated for applications in momentum
exchange, orbital transfer, and energy harvesting. Electrodynamic tethers, for instance, have been
explored as mechanisms for satellite deorbiting or artificial gravity generation. These systems
impose unique control challenges involving deployment dynamics, tension regulation, and oscillation
suppression in microgravity environments. The absence of atmospheric damping further complicates
the stabilization of long, flexible tethers during deployment and
retrieval~\cite{modiDeploymentDynamicsTether1979,huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}.

Advanced control methodologies have been formulated to address critical issues such as tether
entanglement around uncooperative, tumbling targets. Energy-based control frameworks exploiting the
system’s passivity properties have been proposed, wherein potential energy is expressed in terms of
tether torque to direct the target’s angular velocity toward the tether
axis~\cite{wangAntitangleControlTethered2019}. These strategies frequently incorporate sliding mode
controllers optimized via model predictive control, and integrate adaptive weighting mechanisms to
enhance entanglement avoidance.

Dexterous tethered space robots have demonstrated feasibility for missions involving geostationary
orbit debris removal. Such systems typically combine lightweight construction with cost-efficient
operational paradigms. Integrated vision-based pose estimation and coordinated control designs have
enabled precise manipulation of non-cooperative targets in
orbit~\cite{huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}. These control schemes must account for the coupling
introduced by the tether, while ensuring that mission objectives are reliably satisfied through
sophisticated coordination between the robotic platform and its tethered base.

Robust and adaptive control techniques have been integrated to ensure reliable trajectory tracking
and position regulation in uncertain and time-varying environments. Controllers based on sliding
mode and \ldscal{L}{1} adaptive architectures have shown strong resilience against model
uncertainties and environmental disturbances, including during manipulator operation and under
fluctuating cable
lengths~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,maaloufNovelApplicationMultivariable2012}. Empirical
evaluations indicate that incorporating tether force feedback at the termination point, along with
adaptive gain tuning in real time, substantially enhances control performance and disturbance
rejection capabilities.

In parallel, hydrodynamic-aware control strategies have been explored to explicitly incorporate
fluid-structure interaction into control laws. \Ac{cfd}-informed models, supported by experimental
validation, have demonstrated that understanding the coupled influence of the tether, vehicle
geometry, and flow dynamics can be leveraged to optimize maneuverability and energy efficiency in
strong current environments~\cite{wuManeuverabilityHydrodynamicsTethered2021}.

Hybrid and dual-mode systems have introduced new requirements for flexible control architectures.
Platforms such as \ac{ifremer}'s Ariane operate in both tethered and autonomous configurations,
necessitating controllers capable of mode switching and environmental
adaptation~\cite{brignoneFirstSeaTrials2015}. Further, dual-robot systems --such as the MEDUSA
architecture-- combine aerial and underwater agents linked via a tether, expanding the control
domain to include inter-vehicle coordination for deployment, sampling, and retrieval
tasks~\cite{debruynMEDUSAMultiEnvironmentDualRobot2020}.

Sensor-driven control strategies have also been developed to regulate tether geometry and maintain
relative positioning in multi-agent configurations. Catenary-based visual servoing algorithms have
employed onboard cameras and inertial measurement units to estimate and control the
three-dimensional shape of the tether in real time. These systems support stable coordination even
in scenarios involving visual occlusion and external
disturbances~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.

Overall, the literature indicates that effective control of underwater tethered robotic systems
relies on the integration of high-fidelity dynamic modeling, adaptive and robust control methods,
and sensor-based feedback architectures. These elements collectively address the nonlinear,
coupled, and uncertain dynamics that characterize tethered marine robotics and enable reliable
operation in complex environments.

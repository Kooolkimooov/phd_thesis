\section{Introduction}
\label{sota_introduction}

This chapter provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art concerning the
control of chains of underwater robots interconnected via tethers, operating within known but
constrained environments. Two primary scientific challenges are addressed throughout this chapter:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The safe and reliable control of underwater robots navigating through constrained environments;
	\item The incorporation of tether dynamics—specifically, their shape and the forces they exert—into the
	      overall control framework.
\end{itemize}

This chapter is structured as follows. First, developments in unmanned underwater robotics are
reviewed in \cref{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}, emphasizing the various types of \acp{uuv} and
their operational characteristics. Subsequently, systems modelling approaches are examined in
\cref{sota_systems_modelling}, covering \ac{uuv} modelling, tether modelling, and coupled
vehicle-tether dynamics. Control strategies for underwater robots are then assessed in
\cref{sota_control}, with particular attention given to both general \ac{uuv} control and the
specific challenges of tethered configurations. Finally, \cref{sota_discussion} discusses the key
findings and identifies research gaps relevant to the thesis objectives.

\section{\glsentrytitlecase{uuv}{long}}
\label{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}

Unmanned underwater robots are essential for a broad range of applications, including marine
research, environmental monitoring, and underwater exploration. These robotic platforms are
generally categorized into two primary classes according to their operational modes:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Utilizing tethers, \acp{rov} maintain a physical link with a surface vessel, thereby enabling
	      high-bandwidth, real-time communication and control, teleoperation, and continuous power supply;
	\item Operating independently, \acp{auv} rely on preprogrammed missions, limited communications, and
	      onboard sensors for navigation and data acquisition.
	\item other, more specialized vehicles, which may combine features of both \acp{rov} and \acp{auv}, or
	      employ novel designs to enhance maneuverability, energy efficiency, or mission adaptability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{rov}{long}}
\label{sota_uuv_remotely_operated_vehicles}

Remotely operated vehicles \acp{rov} are widely deployed in marine science and industrial
operations. These systems are typically equipped with cameras, environmental sensors, and robotic
manipulators, facilitating tasks such as underwater inspection, maintenance, and deep-sea
exploration. \Acp{rov} are highly maneuverable and are capable of operating at substantial depths,
thus allowing them to perform missions deemed hazardous or impractical for human divers. Their
operations are continuously supervised and controlled by human operators from the surface, which
limits their range to the tether length. However, this tether allows the \ac{rov} to receive power
and consequently operate for extended durations.

To ensure reliable high-bandwidth communication, \acp{rov} are connected to surface vessels via
tethers. This configuration is necessitated by the high absorption of electromagnetic waves in
water, which precludes conventional wireless communication over distances exceeding a few meters.

As illustrated in \cref{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum}, the absorption coefficient of water
decreases with increasing wavelength. Thus, \ac{elf} electromagnetic waves (in the
\qtyrange{3}{300}{\hertz} range) can propagate farther underwater. However, as discussed
in~\cite{altgeltWorldsLargestRadiostation2005}, such frequencies would require impractically large
antennas --on the order of kilometers-- and high transmission power, rendering them unsuitable for
use in mobile underwater platforms.

According to~\cite{christROVManualUser2013}, \acp{rov} are classified by size and functionality
into three main categories:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Work-class \acp{rov} (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_victor_6000}) exceed \qty{1000}{\kilo\gram} and are
	      designed for complex tasks such as subsea construction, maintenance, and repair. These systems are
	      typically equipped with hydraulic-powered thrusters and manipulators, in addition to a
	      comprehensive suite of sensors. Work-class \acp{rov} can operate at depths greater than
	      \qty{3000}{\meter} and are launched from specialized surface support vessels
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}). Their tethers are designed to deliver power to the
	      robot over great distances and require substantial cable management infrastructure aboard the host
	      vessel due to their weight and length.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{auv}{long}}
\label{sota_uuv_autonomous_underwater_vehicles}

An \ac{auv} is an untethered robotic platform capable of operating without real-time communication
(\cref{sota_uuv_auv_asterix}). Although data exchange is possible via acoustic channels, it remains
highly constrained in range and bandwidth (\cite{creuzeRobotsMarinsSousmarins2014}). \Ac{auv} are
typically programmed prior to deployment with mission objectives and rely on onboard sensors,
control algorithms, and occasional communication upon surfacing to accomplish tasks such as
bathymetric mapping, search-and-rescue operations, environmental monitoring, sample collection, and
seabed surveys (\cite{bechlioulisRobustFormationControl2019, merciSimulatorUnderwaterGlider2023}).

The physical configuration of an \ac{auv} is determined by its operational requirements. However,
most are torpedo-shaped \acp{auv} that are optimized for long-range and extended-duration missions.
Their streamlined form minimizes hydrodynamic drag, enabling efficient travel over large distances.
Propulsion may be achieved using conventional thrusters, or, in the case of gliders, through a
buoyancy-driven system. The latter allows vertical movement by adjusting overall density and
internal mass distribution. Horizontal motion is then generated by hydrofoils that convert vertical
displacement into forward glide (\cite{meyerGliderTechnologyOcean2016}).

\subsection{Other Vehicles}
\label{sota_uuv_other_vehicles}

Several underwater vehicle designs exist beyond conventional \ac{rov} or \ac{auv} architectures.
These platforms are intended to improve maneuverability, energy efficiency, or mission
adaptability. Notable examples include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item Biomimetic vehicles, which are inspired by aquatic organisms and aim to replicate their propulsion
	      methods using flexible fins, undulating body motions, or flapping appendages. These systems
	      generate minimal hydrodynamic disturbance, thereby enhancing stealth and environmental
	      compatibility. Examples include the RoboTuna, presented
	      in~\cite{triantafyllouEfficientSwimmingMachine1995}. The humanoid Ocean One K
	      (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_ocean_one_k}) also exhibits biomimetic traits through its human-like structure
	      and dexterous manipulation capabilities.

	\item Reconfigurable vehicles, which may be adaptable in multiple ways. Some systems allow for thruster
	      repositioning to optimize hydrodynamic performance and energy usage depending on specific mission
	      requirements~\cite{fagundesgasparotoAdvancesReconfigurableVectorial2021}. Others, such as Hybrid
	      \acp{rov}, are capable of operating either in autonomous mode or under remote control. These
	      vehicles generally rely on onboard batteries and a lightweight fiber-optic tether, in contrast to
	      standard work-class \acp{rov}. The Ariane vehicle, developed by \ac{ifremer}, exemplifies this
	      class with its ability to switch to autonomous operation when
	      required~\cite{raugelOperationalScientificCapabilities2019}.
\end{itemize}

In this thesis, \acp{rov} are the primary focus due to their tethered nature.

\section{Systems Modelling}
\label{sota_systems_modelling}

The modelling of \acp{uuv} has constituted a foundational element of underwater robotics, providing
the basis for both simulation and model-based control frameworks. The dynamics of underwater
vehicles are commonly described by the classical six-degree-of-freedom rigid body model formulated
for marine craft, as detailed in~\cite{fossenHandbookMarineCraft2011}.

\subsection{Tether Modelling}
\label{sota_sm_tether_modelling}

In this thesis, chains of tethered underwater vehicles are considered in constrained and cluttered
workspaces, where the umbilical simultaneously acts as a safety constraint, a disturbance source,
and a sensing or actuation resource. The tether models required for such scenarios must therefore
operate in real time in order to support online planning and collision avoidance through fast
geometric reasoning and conservative occupancy bounds. At the same time, they must deliver
sufficiently accurate predictions of shape and forces to inform constraint-aware feedback control
and tension management. Compatibility with perception-driven estimation pipelines and practical
tether-management hardware is also essential.

The accurate modeling of tethers represents a fundamental challenge in underwater robotics, as
these flexible structures exhibit complex nonlinear dynamics that significantly influence the
overall system behavior. Tether models vary considerably in their complexity, computational
requirements, and accuracy, ranging from simple geometric approximations to sophisticated continuum
mechanics formulations.

The simplest approach to tether modeling employs geometric approximations, where the cable shape is
described by analytical functions. A common approach is the hyperbolic catenary curve, which
assumes a static equilibrium configuration under the influence of gravity and buoyancy forces; it
should be noted that parabolic curves may also be used~\cite{smolentsevShapeVisualServoing2023}.
This approach has been extensively validated for quasi-static
conditions~\cite{druptValidityCatenaryModel2022, laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.
Catenary-based models offer excellent computational efficiency, typically requiring only a few
milliseconds for evaluation, making them suitable for real-time applications. However, their
accuracy is limited to scenarios with low cable dynamics and minimal hydrodynamic
loading~\cite{filliungAugmentedCatenaryModel2024}.

Another geometric approximations is the use of active shape
control.~\cite{vielROVLocalizationBased2023} proposed a method where the umbilical is equipped with
moving ballasts and buoys to enforce straight-line segments, enabling analytical position
estimation based on angular measurements. Similarly,~\cite{vielSelfManagementROVUmbilical2022}
introduced sliding buoys to maintain cable tension and prevent self-entanglement, resulting in
predictable geometric configurations suitable for real-time navigation feedback.

Finally,~\cite{vielEllipsoidTetherModel2024} developed an ellipsoid model that provides
conservative geometric bounds on the tether's spatial occupation. This approach offers
computational efficiency with guaranteed collision-free motion, requiring only the knowledge of
attachment points and cable length.

The lumped mass approaches discretizes the continuous cable into a finite number of lumped masses
connected by joints modelled as springs, damped springs, ball joints or any other appropriate
system (\cref{sota_sm_tm_lumped_mass}). In \cite{dasilvagomesNewDynamicModel2021} a formalism was
proposed using the Euler-Lagrange method to model towing systems with rigid links connected by
fictitious elastic joints. This approach demonstrated scalable algorithmic procedures capable of
generating dynamic models for arbitrary numbers of segments.

	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/lumped_mass_model}
	\caption{
		Lumped-mass cable model: the tether is discretized into point masses \ldscal{m}{i} linked by
		compliant segments, subjected to weight and buoyancy forces.
	}
	\label{sota_sm_tm_lumped_mass}
\end{figure}

This modelling approach strikes a balance between computational efficiency and accuracy, typically
having an inverse relation between accuracy and computation time, meaning more segments yield more
accuracy but require more computation.

Finite element approaches provide the highest fidelity in tether modeling by discretizing the
continuous cable into numerous elements, each governed by fundamental mechanics
principles.~\cite{buckhamDevelopmentFiniteElement2004} developed a computationally efficient
third-order finite element specifically designed for low-tension dynamics simulation. Their
approach incorporates bending and torsional stiffness effects while maintaining numerical stability
under slack conditions.

In~\cite{chenDynamicCharacteristicsDeepsea2021} Kirchhoff rod theory were employed combined with
differential quadrature and Newmark methods for spatial and temporal discretization. This approach
enabled comprehensive analysis of deep-sea umbilical cables under complex sea conditions, revealing
critical insights into amplitude amplification effects and optimal cable lengths.

For specialized applications,~\cite{dehadraiTransientPlanarDynamics2022} utilized geometrically
exact beam theory to model cable-payload systems, incorporating large displacements, rotations, and
cross-sectional shear. This formulation proved particularly effective for transient flight
scenarios and aerodynamic loading analysis.

In~\cite{beltranEvaluationCoupledEffect2020} nonlinear cable-beam elements were developed
incorporating helical rope structure and multi-directional coupling effects. Their model
successfully predicted static response of damaged ropes under various loading conditions,
demonstrating the importance of coupled axial-bending, axial-torsional, and bending-torsional
interactions.

Finite element models typically require seconds to minutes for evaluation, depending on mesh
resolution and nonlinearity, making them more suitable for offline analysis and detailed design
studies rather than real-time control applications.

semi-implicit numerical methods on finite element representation of flexible bodies, achieving
improved stability and reduced computational requirements for real-time applications. The
development of efficient numerical schemes remains an active area of research, particularly for
applications requiring both high fidelity and real-time performance.

\subsection{Coupled Modelling}
\label{sota_sm_coupled_modelling}

The modeling of coupled vehicle-tether systems represents a critical advancement in underwater
robotics, as the dynamic interactions between flexible cables and underwater vehicles significantly
influence overall system behavior. Unlike standalone tether or vehicle models, coupled approaches
explicitly account for the bidirectional force transmission, hydrodynamic interactions, and
constrained motion that characterize tethered underwater systems.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	% \input{}
	\caption{\todo{figure for coupled models}}
	% \label{}
\end{figure}

The most sophisticated coupled modeling approaches employ advanced continuum mechanics formulations
to capture complex fluid-structure interactions between the tether, vehicle, and surrounding
fluid.~\cite{wuIntegratedHydrodynamicsControl2018} developed \ac{cfd}-based methods solving
Navier-Stokes equations coupled with finite difference cable models, providing detailed insights
into flow-structure interactions. These integrated simulation environments model the complete
dynamics between \acp{rov}, tethers, and propulsion systems, offering detailed insights into
flow-structure interactions.

In~\cite{xuHydrodynamicAnalysisTethered2022, xuNumericalSimulationHydrodynamics2022} comprehensive
hydrodynamic analysis were employed incorporating both cable and vehicle dynamics under various sea
conditions. These approaches offer high accuracy in representing coupled behaviors but require
substantial computational resources, often necessitating high-performance computing clusters for
practical implementation.

In~\cite{suSPHDEMModelingCablecontrolled2024} novel \ac{sph}-\ac{dem} modeling approaches were
introduced for cable-controlled systems, enabling detailed particle-scale simulation of
cable-environment interactions while maintaining computational tractability for coupled system
analysis.

Multi-body dynamic simulations have emerged as powerful tools for modeling
vehicle-tether-management system interactions, particularly in deployment and retrieval
scenarios.~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021, htunTheoryApplicationAbsolute2022}
developed sophisticated multi-body dynamic models using \acp{ancf} and mixed-coordinate methods to
assess vehicle-tether-management system interactions during deployment and retrieval operations.
These approaches enable precise modeling of nonlinear coupling effects and large deformations
during real-time mission operations.

In~\cite{banerjeeDeploymentControlCable1995}, the focus was on deployment control scenarios,
developing variable mass cable models subject to motion constraints and hydrodynamic forces. Their
approach addresses the iterative coupling between cable dynamics and vehicle motion, where
constraint forces depend nonlinearly on both systems.

Advanced coupled systems incorporate smart cables with integrated sensing and actuation
capabilities. These systems transform the traditional passive tether into an active component of
the robotic system.~\cite{yuArmlessUnderwaterManipulation2013} demonstrated the use of smart cables
offering relative positioning capabilities and active buoyancy control, enabling the deployment of
small, agile \acp{rov} as manipulative end-effectors tethered to a primary platform.

For specialized applications,~\cite{abreuMarineVehiclesStreamers2016} addressed marine vehicles
with sensor-equipped cables (streamer) for geotechnical surveys, employing Kane's method for
coupled streamer-vehicle dynamics. This approach demonstrates the adaptability of multi-body
methods to specific operational configurations while maintaining computational efficiency.

	\centering

	\caption{\todo{figure for active and smart tethers}}

\end{figure}

The integration of controllable elements along the tether enables predictable geometric
configurations.~\cite{vielROVLocalizationBased2023} proposed methods where the umbilical is
equipped with moving ballasts and buoys to enforce straight-line segments, transforming the cable
into a position sensing element. In this configuration, the umbilical becomes a source of position
feedback, converting a traditional constraint into an estimation asset.

Similarly,~\cite{vielSelfManagementROVUmbilical2022} introduced sliding buoys for passive cable
management, maintaining tension and preventing self-entanglement while providing predictable
configurations suitable for coupled system modeling.

State estimation in tethered configurations presents unique challenges due to tight dynamic
coupling between vehicle and cable. Multi-body dynamic models have been developed to estimate the
combined state of vehicle-tether systems, accounting for force transmission, tether deformation,
and fluid interaction effects~\cite{htunSingularityfreeMultibodyModeling2021}. These models
recognize that accurate localization and velocity estimation depend fundamentally on modeling the
tether's influence on overall vehicle motion.

Vision-based estimation methods have demonstrated particular effectiveness in coupled systems.
Visual servoing approaches simultaneously estimate vehicle pose and tether shape by optimizing
parameterized catenary models through nonlinear least-squares
methods~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These techniques remain effective even
with partial cable visibility or environmental perturbations.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	% \input{}
	\caption{\todo{figure for vision based}}

\end{figure}

Sensor fusion techniques enhance estimation robustness by integrating heterogeneous sensor inputs
from both vehicle and tether systems. Common combinations include \acp{imu}, depth sensors, and
visual sensors, with fusion frameworks designed to mitigate the limitations of each
modality~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.

For systems involving multiple tethers, specialized estimation techniques reduce sensor dependency
while preserving observability. Nonlinear observer designs have been shown to accurately estimate
full system states using minimal instrumentation such as dual accelerometers, encoders, or
inclinometers~\cite{tognonNonlinearObserverControl2015}. These observers support precise regulation
of both Cartesian position and internal tether forces in bi-tethered multi-agent systems.

Comprehensive simulation platforms have been developed to analyze cable-robot-environment
interactions in coupled systems. The \ac{uuv} Simulator, based on the Gazebo framework, supports
modeling of hydrodynamic effects, thruster dynamics, and environmental disturbances for tethered
vehicles~\cite{manhaesUUVSimulatorGazebobased2016}. Class-specific simulators such as the BlueROV2
model implemented in Simulink\texttrademark{} offer benchmarks for coupled system algorithm
development and validation~\cite{vonbenzonOpenSourceBenchmarkSimulator2022}.

Parent-child robotic configurations, where large carrier vehicles deploy smaller tethered agents,
introduce additional modeling complexities. Estimation frameworks for such systems must account for
nonlinear hydrodynamic coupling, tether forces, and time delays introduced by flexible link
dynamics~\cite{kimParentchildUnderwaterRobotbased2023}. Hybrid approaches combine mechanical models
with complementary sensing strategies adapted to specific operational requirements.

Integration of state estimation with cable shape reconstruction has emerged as a critical
capability for real-time control in tethered systems. Joint estimation frameworks utilize
parameterized cable models in conjunction with vehicle state estimation to provide unified
understanding of robot-cable configurations. These models support both motion planning and
disturbance rejection while maintaining sufficient real-time accuracy and computational
tractability.

Experimental validation remains essential for coupled system development. Controlled tank
experiments and open-water field trials evaluate algorithm performance under realistic disturbances
such as wave-induced motion, visibility reduction, and flow
turbulence~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}. These studies provide calibration data
and benchmarking capabilities for coupled estimation and control algorithms in operational
environments.

\section{Control}
\label{sota_control}

\subsection{\glsentrytitlecase{uuv}{long} control} \label{sota_c_uuv_control}

The control of underwater robots entails distinctive challenges arising from the nonlinear,
uncertain, and dynamic characteristics of the aquatic environment. These challenges include
hydrodynamic forces, buoyancy variability, unpredictable ocean currents, limited sensory input, and
the presence of static or dynamic obstacles. To address these issues, a wide array of modeling,
estimation, and control strategies has been developed in recent years.

Robust control approaches remain essential to ensure stable and reliable underwater operation.
\Acp{nmpc} schemes have been formulated to navigate vehicles in constrained workspaces, accounting
for environmental obstacles, boundary limitations, thruster saturation, and ocean
currents~\cite{heshmati-alamdariRobustPredictiveControl2020}. To mitigate the influence of
unmodeled hydrodynamic phenomena and reduce tracking errors, sliding mode control has been combined
with Gaussian process regression, producing data-driven compensatory
mechanisms~\cite{limaSlidingModeControl2020}. Additionally, adaptive neural network-based
controllers optimized using genetic algorithms have demonstrated resilience against parametric
uncertainty and external disturbances, particularly in the control of underwater
manipulators~\cite{salloomAdaptiveNeuralNetwork2020}.

As multi-agent operations gain prominence in subsea applications, formation control and cooperative
mission planning have become critical. Lyapunov-based control frameworks have been employed to
ensure that groups of \acp{auv} maintain prescribed formations and track reference trajectories,
thereby enhancing robustness and mission coherence~\cite{khalajiLyapunovBasedFormationControl2020}.
Concurrently, advances in robust state estimation and fault diagnosis have addressed issues related
to sensor noise, hydrodynamic disturbances, and actuator degradation. Notably, hybrid techniques
combining robust filtering with sliding-window estimation have been developed to improve system
resilience and autonomy under uncertain operational
conditions~\cite{songRobustStateEstimation2023}.

\subsection{Tethered \glsentrytitlecase{uuv}{long} Control}\label{sota_c_tethered_uuv_control}

A broad spectrum of control strategies has been formulated to address the unique challenges
associated with underwater tethered robotic systems, wherein dynamic coupling among the vehicle,
the tether, and the environment imposes significant influence on stability and maneuverability.
Early research on tether management systems laid foundational principles for the safe deployment
and retrieval of umbilicals, focusing on mechanical reliability and operational
robustness~\cite{abelUnderwaterVehicleTether1994}.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	% \input{}
	\caption{\todo{Tether Management System (TMS) schematic for deployment and retrieval}}
	% \label{}

Although this study primarily focuses on underwater robots, similar control challenges are
encountered in aerial robotics, where tethers may be used not only for energy delivery, data
transmission, or mechanical stabilization, but also as active elements in cooperative manipulation
tasks. For example, a catenary robot --comprising two \acp{uav} connected by a flexible cable-- was
designed to autonomously locate and transport hook-shaped
objects~\cite{dantonioCatenaryRobotDesign2021}. In that system, the cable's position, orientation,
and span were regulated in real time to facilitate manipulation tasks without requiring prior
mechanical attachment. Such applications demonstrate that tether dynamics can be exploited as
functional assets, rather than merely compensated for.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	% \input{}
	\caption{\todo{Catenary robot: two UAVs manipulating a hook via a flexible cable}}
	% \label{}
\end{figure}

Recent advancements in tethered aerial systems have enabled the development of multi-vehicle
configurations featuring dynamic tether management. In particular, systems comprising chains of
multicopters connected via elastic tethers have been implemented, where tether lengths are actively
adjusted using controlled winches~\cite{fagianoSystemsTetheredMulticopters2017}. These
architectures employ hierarchical control frameworks that consider the elastic nature of the
tethers and their unidirectional load-bearing characteristics, thereby enhancing endurance and task
flexibility across various operational domains.

The modeling and control of tethered aerial vehicles have evolved to incorporate novel mechanical
arrangements, such as pulley-gimbal mechanisms. These configurations allow \acp{uav} to move freely
along tethers while enabling accurate position tracking~\cite{kosarnovskyStringTetheredDrones2019}.
The overall system can be conceptualized as a robotic manipulator, wherein each tether segment
functions as a variable-length link and each \ac{uav} acts as an articulated joint actuator. This
perspective facilitates complex coordinated motion through hybrid geometric and linear control
strategies.

Traditional \acp{tms} have evolved from mechanical winch-based deployment to actively controlled
architectures. Dynamic mooring models have been introduced to simulate tether response to
hydrodynamic loading, cable tension, and anchor-point
movement~\cite{aaseModelingControlCatenary2018}. These systems often incorporate safety mechanisms
such as tension constraints and alarm protocols, alerting operators when operational limits are
exceeded.

Fundamental properties of tethered aerial robots have been analytically characterized, revealing
differential flatness with respect to specific output pairs. Notable flat outputs include
combinations such as the link elevation and vehicle attitude, or link elevation and longitudinal
tension~\cite{tognonDynamicsControlEstimation2017}. This characterization supports the formulation
of almost globally convergent nonlinear controllers and observers that are capable of tracking
smooth, time-varying trajectories using only onboard accelerometers and gyroscopes. These
approaches offer robustness against nonlinear coupling effects, parameter uncertainties, and sensor
noise.

In the domain of space robotics, tethers have been investigated for applications in momentum
exchange, orbital transfer, and energy harvesting. Electrodynamic tethers, for instance, have been
explored as mechanisms for satellite deorbiting or artificial gravity generation. These systems
impose unique control challenges involving deployment dynamics, tension regulation, and oscillation
suppression in microgravity environments. The absence of atmospheric damping further complicates
the stabilization of long, flexible tethers during deployment and
retrieval~\cite{modiDeploymentDynamicsTether1979,huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}.

Advanced control methodologies have been formulated to address critical issues such as tether
entanglement around uncooperative, tumbling targets. Energy-based control frameworks exploiting the
system's passivity properties have been proposed, wherein potential energy is expressed in terms of
tether torque to direct the target's angular velocity toward the tether
axis~\cite{wangAntitangleControlTethered2019}. These strategies frequently incorporate sliding mode
controllers optimized via model predictive control, and integrate adaptive weighting mechanisms to
enhance entanglement avoidance.

Dexterous tethered space robots have demonstrated feasibility for missions involving geostationary
orbit debris removal. Such systems typically combine lightweight construction with cost-efficient
operational paradigms. Integrated vision-based pose estimation and coordinated control designs have
enabled precise manipulation of non-cooperative targets in
orbit~\cite{huangDexterousTetheredSpace2017}. These control schemes must account for the coupling
introduced by the tether, while ensuring that mission objectives are reliably satisfied through
sophisticated coordination between the robotic platform and its tethered base.

Robust and adaptive control techniques have been integrated to ensure reliable trajectory tracking
and position regulation in uncertain and time-varying environments. Controllers based on sliding
mode and \ldscal{L}{1} adaptive architectures have shown strong resilience against model
uncertainties and environmental disturbances, including during manipulator operation and under
fluctuating cable
lengths~\cite{soyluDynamicsControlTethered2010,maaloufNovelApplicationMultivariable2012}. Empirical
evaluations indicate that incorporating tether force feedback at the termination point, along with
adaptive gain tuning in real time, substantially enhances control performance and disturbance
rejection capabilities.

In parallel, hydrodynamic-aware control strategies have been explored to explicitly incorporate
fluid-structure interaction into control laws. \Ac{cfd}-informed models, supported by experimental
validation, have demonstrated that understanding the coupled influence of the tether, vehicle
geometry, and flow dynamics can be leveraged to optimize maneuverability and energy efficiency in
strong current environments~\cite{wuManeuverabilityHydrodynamicsTethered2021}.

Hybrid and dual-mode systems have introduced new requirements for flexible control architectures.
Platforms such as \ac{ifremer}'s Ariane operate in both tethered and autonomous configurations,
necessitating controllers capable of mode switching and environmental
adaptation~\cite{brignoneFirstSeaTrials2015}. Further, dual-robot systems --such as the MEDUSA
architecture-- combine aerial and underwater agents linked via a tether, expanding the control
domain to include inter-vehicle coordination for deployment, sampling, and retrieval
tasks~\cite{debruynMEDUSAMultiEnvironmentDualRobot2020}.

Sensor-driven control strategies have also been developed to regulate tether geometry and maintain
relative positioning in multi-agent configurations. Catenary-based visual servoing algorithms have
employed onboard cameras and inertial measurement units to estimate and control the
three-dimensional shape of the tether in real time. These systems support stable coordination even
in scenarios involving visual occlusion and external
disturbances~\cite{laranjeiraCatenarybasedVisualServoing2020}.

Overall, the literature indicates that effective control of underwater tethered robotic systems
relies on the integration of high-fidelity dynamic modeling, adaptive and robust control methods,
and sensor-based feedback architectures. These elements collectively address the nonlinear,
coupled, and uncertain dynamics that characterize tethered marine robotics and enable reliable
operation in complex environments.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sota_discussion}

This chapter reviewed recent advances in underwater robotic platforms, the modeling of vehicles and
tethers, coupled vehicle-tether dynamics, and control strategies for both untethered and tethered
systems. The literature revealed consistent themes directly relevant to this thesis, which targets
the safe control of chains of tethered underwater robots operating in known but constrained
environments.

A first theme concerns the fidelity-computational-efficiency trade-off in tether modeling
(\cref{sota_sm_tether_modelling}). Geometric models such as the catenary or parabolic curves are
computationally light and can run at control rates, but they neglect cable dynamics and complex
hydrodynamic loading. Lumped-mass models provide a tunable compromise, whereas finite-element and
rod-theory approaches achieve the highest fidelity at the cost of seconds-to-minutes evaluation
times, typically unsuitable for real-time onboard control. For online planning and constraint
handling in cluttered workspaces, conservative geometric representations are particularly
attractive because they provide safety guarantees with modest computational requirements.

A second theme is that accurate control in practice requires treating the vehicle and tether as a
coupled system (\cref{sota_sm_coupled_modelling}). Multi-body and continuum mechanics formulations
capture the bidirectional exchange of forces and the constrained motion induced by the tether.
While high-fidelity \ac{cfd}-informed and \ac{sph}-based schemes yield rich insights, real-time
control benefits from reduced-order coupled models, often complemented by sensor fusion and
vision-based estimation of tether shape or attachment geometry. The integration of
cable-configuration estimation with vehicle-state estimation emerges as a recurring requirement for
robust performance.

A third theme is the trend toward control approaches that combine constraint-aware optimization
with robustness to modeling errors and disturbances (\cref{sota_c_uuv_control}). Modern techniques
such as \ac{mpc}, sliding-mode control, and adaptive or neural augmentation exemplify this
evolution. For tethered systems, hybrid strategies that regulate tension and cable geometry while
delegating fast motion to the vehicle actuators have proven effective
(\cref{sota_c_tethered_uuv_control}). Safety mechanisms such as tension limits, slack avoidance,
and collision prevention are typically enforced either through dedicated tether-management
subsystems or embedded as constraints in the control layer.

Finally, analogies with tethered aerial robots and dual-mode platforms highlight architectural
principles that are transferable to the underwater domain, including hierarchical control,
flatness-inspired trajectory generation, and the use of the tether as an active element for both
sensing and actuation. Nevertheless, underwater operation introduces additional difficulties such
as strong currents, limited visibility, and complex fluid-structure interactions, which complicate
the direct transfer of these methods.

Despite these advances, several open challenges remain. The incorporation of hard safety
constraints on tether slack, and collision avoidance into online planners with provable feasibility
and stability is still unresolved. The tether itself can be exploited as a sensing device, whether
through smart or actively managed designs such as buoys, moving ballasts, or distributed sensors,
or through sensor fusion that integrates \acp{imu}, depth, tension, and partial vision measurements
to enable robust localization and cable-shape estimation under degraded conditions. A further
challenge lies in the development of reduced-order coupled models that remain efficient yet
reliable, preserving key behaviors relevant for safety and performance while enabling real-time
optimization and control.

In summary, the state of the art supports a thesis strategy that combines conservative geometric
representations for safety, reduced-order coupled models for online decision making, and robust
constraint-aware control with integrated system estimation. Within this framework, the present work
addresses the gap of reliable real-time control for chains of tethered underwater robots in
constrained environments, aiming for formalized safety handling and experimentally validated
performance under realistic sensing and hydrodynamic conditions.

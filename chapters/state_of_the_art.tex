\section{Introduction}

The present chapter will provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art
concerning the control of chains of underwater robots interconnected via tethers, operating within
known but constrained environments. Two primary scientific challenges will be addressed throughout
this thesis:

\begin{itemize}
	\item The safe and reliable control of underwater robots navigating through constrained environments;
	\item The incorporation of tether dynamics—specifically, their shape and the forces they exert—into the
	      overall control framework.
\end{itemize}

Accordingly, this chapter will begin by reviewing developments in unmanned underwater robotics in
\cref{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}, emphasizing the various types of unmanned underwater
vehicles (UUVs). It will then examine research related to cables used in underwater robotics
(\cref{sota_cables_in_underwater_robotics}), with a focus on cable shape estimation and management
techniques. Lastly, the chapter will assess control strategies for underwater robots in
\cref{sota_robot_control}, with particular attention given to tethered configurations and their
specific control challenges.

\section{Unmanned underwater robots}
\label{sota_unmanned_underwater_robots}

This section provides an overview of unmanned underwater robots, which are essential for various
applications such as marine research, environmental monitoring, and underwater exploration. These
robots can generally be classified into two main categories based on their operational modes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Remotely operated vehicles use a tether to connect to a surface vessel, allowing for high
	      bandwidth, real-time feedback, control, teleoperation, and power supply,
	\item Autonomous underwater vehicles referred to as ``AUVs'' operate independently, relying on
	      pre-programmed missions, sparse communications and onboard sensors for navigation and data
	      collection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Remotely operated vehicles}
\label{sota_uuv_remotely_operated_vehicles}

Remotely operated vehicles also referred to as ``ROVs'' are commonly used in marine research and
are typically equipped with cameras, sensors, and manipulators for various underwater tasks,
underwater inspections, and exploration of deep-sea environments. They are usually highly
maneuverable (honolomous) and can operate at significant depths, making them suitable for tasks
that are too dangerous or impractical for human divers. ROVs are continuously monitored and given
instructions by an operator and operate in a limited range.

In order to provide high bandwidth communication, ROVs use a tether to connect to a surface vessel.
The use of tethers is necessitated by the absorption of electromagnetic waves in water which
prevents common wireless communications at a range greater than a few meters.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\inputtikz{figures/water_absorption_spectrum}
	\caption{Absorption of electromagnetic waves by water (\cite{downingOpticalConstantsWater1975}) and water vapor (\cite{rothmanHITRAN2004Molecular2005}), the biggest contributor of absorption in the atmosphere.}
	\label{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum}
\end{figure}

As \cref{sota_uuv_rov_absorption_spectrum} suggests, the absorption coefficient of water lowers
with increasing wavelength, which means that lower electromagnetic frequencies (for example, the
extremely low frequency range: \qtyrange{3}{300}{\hertz}) can travel further in water. However,
as~\cite{altgeltWorldsLargestRadiostation2005} discusses, such communications would require
excessively large antennas (on the order of kilometers) and a ludicrous amount of power therefore
making them impractical for underwater robots.

According to \cite{christROVManualUser2013}, ROVs are typically classified into three categories
based on their size and capabilities: ``Work class ROVs'' (\cref{sota_uuv_rov_victor_6000}) are
heavy-duty (more than \qty{1000}{\kilo\gram}) vehicles designed for complex tasks such as
underwater construction, maintenance, and repair. They are equipped with powerful thrusters and
manipulators usually powered by hydraulic systems as well as a variety of sensors already
discussed. These ROVs are capable of operating at significant depths (more than
\qty{3000}{\meter}). Work class ROVs are deployed from dedicated surface vessels
(\cref{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}). The sheer length of the tether necessitated by this
class of ROVs requires a correspondingly heavy-duty cable management system that takes up a
significant amount of space on the surface vessel.

	}
	\label{sota_uuv_rov_atlante_and_victor_6000}
\end{figure}

``Mid-sized class ROVs'' are similar to work
class ROVs in a lot of ways, but are smaller and lighter (between
\qtyrange{100}{1000}{\kilo\gram}), are designed for medium depths operations (between
\qtyrange{300}{3000}{\meter}) and can be operated by a lesser crew from a smaller vessel. Their
systems are generally electrically powered.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\figurewidth, trim=350 200 350 200, clip]{ocean_one_k.png}
	\caption{
		A mid-sized class, humanoid, ROV: the Ocean One K, used by the Stanford University for underwater
		exploration (\cite{StanfordRoboticsLab,khatibOceanOneRobotic2016}).
	}
	\label{sota_uuv_rov_ocean_one_k}
\end{figure}

``Observation class ROVs'' are smaller and lighter
(less than \qty{100}{\kilo\gram}) vehicles designed for observation and inspection tasks. They are
typically equipped with cameras and basic sensors, but lack manipulators or other complex systems.
Observation class ROVs are often used in shallow waters (less than \qty{300}{\meter}) and can be
operated from small boats or even by divers.

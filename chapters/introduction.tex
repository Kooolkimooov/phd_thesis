\section{Focus and Contributions}
\label{i_focus_and_contributions}

This thesis is concerned with the modeling and control of chains of tethered underwater robots
operating in constrained environments. The central premise is that cable geometry must be
explicitly accounted for in both modeling and control, while still being represented with
sufficient computational efficiency to allow real-time use. Two interrelated strands are addressed:
the development of fast, analytically tractable models of tether shape suitable for online
estimation and control, and the design of chain-level control methods that incorporate tether
geometrical constraints while remaining practical for implementation.

The following research questions are investigated:
\begin{itemize}
	\item What level of accuracy can an analytic cable model achieve while still being fast enough for online
	      control? Which minimal representation are sufficient to capture steady hydrodynamic effects without
	      resorting to full continuum dynamics?
	\item How should a robot chain be modeled so that tether forces and geometry map consistently into each
	      vehicle's dynamics, and how can controllers exploit this structure to enforce environmental
	      clearance and inter-robot spacing?
	\item How do the proposed models and controllers behave under realistic conditions across different
	      operating regimes?
\end{itemize}

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \cref{cable_modelling}:
	      \begin{itemize}
		      \item Fast cable modeling for control. The classical catenary is revisited with practical parameter
		            relations and a curvilinear discretization that yields uniformly spaced samples along the arc. An
		            augmented, two-degree-of-freedom variant is introduced to capture steady hydrodynamic effects by
		            tilting and rotating the catenary. Its accuracy is evaluated over a multi-cable dataset and
		            compared with a fully dynamic model. The augmented form is shown to achieve substantially lower
		            residuals than the purely vertical catenary across cables and conditions, while remaining
		            computationally efficient.
		      \item Preliminary work toward dynamics for the augmented parameters. A physics-inspired surrogate is
		            proposed to endow the augmentation parameters with simple dynamics using a pendulum analogy driven
		            by the motion of the attachment points. In addition, similar preliminary work using data-driven
		            symbolic regression is presented.
	      \end{itemize}

	\item \cref{robot_chain_control}:
	      \begin{itemize}
		      \item Unified robot-chain modeling with catenary forces. A compact chain model is formulated in which
		            each vehicle follows a standard marine-craft definition, and the tethers are represented by
		            catenaries. This structure enables coherent constraints on environment clearance, inter-robot
		            distances, and actuator bounds.
		      \item Controller design and comparison for tethered chains. Three control schemes are proposed and
		            evaluated. Simulations with varying bathymetry and a mixed \ac{rov}/\ac{usv} chain are performed.
		            Tracking errors, catenary clearances, inter-robot distances, and computation times are reported,
		            revealing trade-offs between constraint satisfaction and computational load.
	      \end{itemize}

	\item \cref{experiments,additionnal_figures}:
	      \begin{itemize}
		      \item Experimental resources and validation. A motion-capture dataset covering multiple cables, lengths,
		            and motion regimes is collected to evaluate the augmented catenary. Furthermore, a robot chain
		            experiment is carried out to assess the feasibility of the proposed control methods.
	      \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Taken together, these results contribute an analytically tractable modeling layer for tethers and a
set of control strategies for robot chains that explicitly respect tether geometry.

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{i_thesis_outline}

\Cref{state_of_the_art}
surveys the literature on unmanned underwater vehicles and tethered systems. It reviews platform
families (\acp{rov}, \acp{auv}, and related vehicles), modeling approaches for vehicles, tethers,
and coupled vehicle-tether dynamics, and control strategies for both free-swimming and tethered
robots.

\Cref{cable_modelling}
develops fast cable models for model-based control. A statistical assessment over a multi-cable
dataset is presented, together with a comparison against a fully dynamic cable model.

\Cref{robot_chain_control}
formulates a unified model of a robot chain. Three complementary controllers are then studied.

\Cref{conclusion}
summarizes the contributions and limitations, provides a general conclusion, and outlines future
directions.

The appendices gather complementary material: \cref{experiments} details the experimental
campaigns, including the dataset used to evaluate the augmented catenary model across multiple
cables and conditions, and a scaled robot-chain experiment validating the proposed control methods.
\cref{additionnal_figures} provides additional figures and results, while \cref{summary_in_french}
presents an extended summary of the thesis in French.

\section{Focus and Contributions}
\label{i_focus_and_contributions}

This thesis focuses on modeling and controlling a chain of tethered underwater robots operating in
constrained environments. The central premise is that cable geometry must be accounted for
explicitly in both modeling and control, yet represented with sufficient computational efficiency
to enable real-time use. Two intertwined strands are addressed: fast, analytically tractable models
of tether shape suitable for online estimation and control; and chain-level control methods that
incorporate tether geometrical constraints while remaining practical to implement.

Concretely, the thesis investigates the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item How accurate can an analytic cable model be while remaining fast enough for online control? What
	      minimal extensions capture steady hydrodynamic effects without resorting to full continuum
	      dynamics?
	\item How should a robot chain be modeled so that tether forces and geometry map consistently into each
	      vehicle's dynamics, and how can controllers leverage this structure to enforce environmental
	      clearance and inter-robot spacing?
	\item How do these models and controllers behave under realistic conditions across different operating
	      regimes?
\end{itemize}

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Fast cable modeling for control (\cref{cable_modelling}). The classical catenary is revisited with
	      practical parameter relations and a curvilinear discretization that yields uniformly spaced samples
	      along the arc. An augmented, two-degree-of-freedom variant is introduced to capture steady
	      hydrodynamic effects by tilting and rotating the catenary. Its accuracy is quantified over a
	      multi-cable dataset and contrasted with a fully dynamic model, showing that the augmented form
	      achieves substantially lower residuals than the purely vertical catenary across cables and
	      conditions while remaining computationally lightweight (see
	      also~\cite{filliungAugmentedCatenaryModel2024,filliungAugmentedCatenaryModel2024a}).

	\item Toward dynamics for the augmented parameters (\cref{cable_modelling}). A physics-inspired surrogate
	      is proposed to endow the augmentation parameters with simple dynamics using a variable-length
	      pendulum analogy driven by the motion of the attachment points, providing a pathway toward
	      estimation and control without full fluid-structure simulation.

	\item Unified robot-chain modeling with catenary forces (\cref{robot_chain_control}). A compact chain
	      model is formulated where each vehicle follows a standard marine-craft description and the tethers
	      are represented by catenaries whose horizontal/vertical tensions map consistently into each body
	      frame. This enables coherent constraints on seafloor/surface clearance, inter-robot distances, and
	      actuator bounds.

	\item Controller design and comparison for tethered chains (\cref{robot_chain_control}). Three
	      complementary schemes are developed and evaluated: a constrained model predictive controller, a
	      waypoint-based planner tracked by a \acs{pid} loop, and a reactive peer-to-peer catenary regulation
	      law. In simulation with varying bathymetry and a mixed \ac{rov}/\ac{usv} chain, the study reports
	      tracking errors, catenary clearances, inter-robot distances, and computation times, revealing
	      trade-offs between constraint satisfaction and computational load.

	\item Experimental resources and validation (\cref{experiments,additionnal_figures}). A motion-capture
	      dataset covering multiple cables, lengths, and motion regimes is collected to evaluate the
	      augmented catenary; a scaled chain experiment demonstrates feasibility of the proposed control
	      methods. The dynamic cable model used for comparison leverages sensing/actuation concepts also
	      reported in~\cite{peraudIMUbasedMonitoringBuoyBallast2024}.
\end{itemize}

Collectively, these results contribute an analytically tractable modeling layer for tethers and a
set of control strategies for robot chains that explicitly respect tether geometry.

\section{Thesis Outline}\label{i_thesis_outline}

\Cref{state_of_the_art}
surveys the literature on unmanned underwater vehicles and tethered systems. It reviews platform
families (\acp{rov}, \acp{auv}, and related vehicles), modeling approaches for vehicles, tethers,
and coupled vehicle-tether dynamics, and control strategies for both free-swimming and tethered
robots.

\Cref{cable_modelling}
develops fast cable models for model-based control. A statistical assessment over a multi-cable
dataset is presented, together with a comparison against a fully dynamic cable model.

\Cref{robot_chain_control}
formulates a unified model of a robot chain. Three complementary controllers are then studied.

\Cref{conclusion}
summarizes the contributions and limitations, provides a general conclusion, and outlines future
directions.

The appendices gather complementary material: \cref{experiments} details the experimental
campaigns: the dataset used to evaluate the augmented catenary model across multiple cables and
conditions, and a scaled robot-chain experiment validating the proposed control methods.
\cref{additionnal_figures} provides additional figures and results.~\cref{summary_in_french}
presents an extended summary of the thesis in French.
